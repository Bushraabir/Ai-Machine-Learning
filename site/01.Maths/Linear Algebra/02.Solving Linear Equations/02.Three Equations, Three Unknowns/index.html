    Three Equations Three Unknowns

    The \(2 \times 2\) case is easy to draw. The \(n \times n\) case is where the full theory lives but is impossible to visualize. The \(3 \times 3\) case is the sweet spot — it is the last system I can see completely, in three dimensions, with the row and column pictures fully geometric. After this, I have to trust the algebra.

    For \(n = 3\), I am incredibly lucky:

    n = 3   →   vectors live in R^3   →   I can draw them
            planes exist in R^3   →   I can draw them
            transformations act on R^3 → I can see them

n = 4   →   vectors live in R^4   →   impossible to draw
            hyperplanes in R^4    →   impossible to draw
            transformations       →   algebra only

    The mathematics works perfectly in any dimension. It is us who cannot keep up past 3D. This is why I want to understand the \(3 \times 3\) case as deeply as possible — the geometric intuition built here carries into higher dimensions, even when I can no longer see them.

    The system I will carry through every picture: \(2x - y + z = 3\) \(x + 3y - z = 5\) \(-x + y + 4z = 2\)

    A \(3 \times 3\) matrix is a machine that acts on all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) at once. It grabs the entire coordinate system and warps it — stretching, squishing, rotating, collapsing.

    \[A : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3\]

    There are three lenses through which to read the same matrix. Each one reveals something different. The same equation \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) looks completely different depending on which lens I use.

    Matrix Form¶

    Always write the system as \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) first. This is the compact form that makes structure visible. $$ A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \ 1 & 3 & -1 \ -1 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \ y \ z \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} 3 \ 5 \ 2 \end{pmatrix} $$ \(A\) is \(3 \times 3\). The unknown is a vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3\). The right-hand side \(\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^3\). Everything that follows is just two ways of reading this one equation.

    1) The Transformation View¶

    What does this matrix actually do to space? A matrix is a function. It eats a vector and spits out a new one: \(A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}\). But the magic is that it does this consistently to every point in space at once — the whole coordinate grid moves together, following a single rule.

    The transformation is linear — straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, and the origin stays fixed. The grid warps but never curves.

    Transformation What it does to space
    Rotation Spins everything around an axis
    Scaling Stretches or squishes in some direction
    Reflection Flips space like a mirror
    Shearing Tilts space, like leaning a stack of papers sideways
    Projection Collapses a dimension — squashes 3D into 2D
    The left grid is your original space. After multiplying by \(A\), it becomes the warped grid on the right. Every point moved — but the transformation followed a consistent rule.

    The transformation is linear — straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, and the origin stays fixed. The grid warps but never curves.

    When I see a \(3 \times 3\) matrix, I should first ask: what kind of transformation is this? Does it rotate? Does it collapse a dimension? The entries encode the answer.

    2) The Column Picture¶

    Where do the axes go after the transformation? Regroup the same system by columns: $$ x \begin{pmatrix} 2 \ 1 \ -1 \end{pmatrix}-y \begin{pmatrix} -1 \ 3 \ 1 \end{pmatrix} - z \begin{pmatrix} 1 \ -1 \ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \ 5 \ 2 \end{pmatrix} $$ Let me name these: $$ \mathbf{a}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \ 1 \ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{a}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \ 3 \ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{a}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \ -1 \ 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \ 5 \ 2 \end{pmatrix} $$ The question is now purely geometric in \(\mathbb{R}^3\): can I find scalars \(x, y, z\) such that $\(x\mathbf{a}_1 + y\mathbf{a}_2 + z\mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{b}\)$ Think of it as: I am standing at the origin with three moves available — I can go \(x\) units in the direction of \(\mathbf{a}_1\), \(y\) units in the direction of \(\mathbf{a}_2\), \(z\) units in the direction of \(\mathbf{a}_3\). Can I reach \(\mathbf{b}\)?

    Write the matrix as three column vectors: $\(A = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \mathbf{a}_3 \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix}\)$ These columns tell me exactly where the standard basis vectors land: - \(\mathbf{a}_1\) = where \((1,0,0)\) — the \(x\)-axis — lands after the transformation - \(\mathbf{a}_2\) = where \((0,1,0)\) — the \(y\)-axis — lands after the transformation - \(\mathbf{a}_3\) = where \((0,0,1)\) — the \(z\)-axis — lands after the transformation The three columns together form a parallelepiped — the image of the unit cube under \(A\).

    Are the columns linearly independent? - Yes → parallelepiped has real 3D volume → transformation is invertible - No → columns are coplanar → space collapses into a plane or line → not invertible

    If the three columns span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (linearly independent, \(A\) invertible), then I can reach every \(\mathbf{b}\). If the columns are coplanar (linearly dependent), I can only reach vectors in that plane — most \(\mathbf{b}\) are unreachable. The column picture makes the existence question about \(\mathbf{b}\) and the column space completely transparent.

    3) The Row Picture¶

    Each row of \(A\) gives one equation. Each equation \(\mathbf{a}_i^T \mathbf{x} = b_i\) defines a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — the set of all points \((x, y, z)\) whose dot product with the normal vector \(\mathbf{a}_i\) equals \(b_i\). When I solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\), I am asking: where do these three planes intersect? For my system: Plane 1: \(2x - y + z = 3\), normal vector \(\mathbf{n}_1 = (2, -1, 1)\) Plane 2: \(x + 3y - z = 5\), normal vector \(\mathbf{n}_2 = (1, 3, -1)\) Plane 3: \(-x + y + 4z = 2\), normal vector \(\mathbf{n}_3 = (-1, 1, 4)\) The plane is perpendicular to its normal vector, sitting at signed distance \(b_i / |\mathbf{n}_i|\) from the origin. ![[Pasted image 20260413095222.png]] The solution is the point where all three planes intersect. Generically in \(\mathbb{R}^3\): two planes intersect in a line; three planes intersect in a point.

    When things go wrong: - Two planes parallel → no intersection → no solution - Three planes sharing a common line → infinitely many solutions - All three planes parallel → no solution - Three planes reducing to the same plane → infinitely many solutions The row picture makes the geometry of failure completely clear. But it tells me nothing efficient about how to find the solution.

    Determinant — The Volume Story¶

    The determinant is the single most important scalar attached to a matrix. It answers:

    By what factor does this transformation scale volume?

    $\(\det(A) = \text{signed volume of the parallelepiped formed by the columns}\)$ ![[Pasted image 20260413095454.png]]

    \(\det(A)\) Volume Orientation Invertible?
    \(> 0\) Scaled Preserved Yes
    \(= 0\) Zero Lost No
    \(< 0\) Scaled Flipped Yes

    If \(\det(A) = 0\), the matrix is not invertible. 3D space has been squashed into a 2D plane (or worse, a line or a point). Information is irreversibly lost. There is no way to undo the transformation.

    - Row Picture Column Picture
    Objects Three planes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) Three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
    Solution Point where all planes meet Weights \(x, y, z\) combining columns to give \(\mathbf{b}\)
    Unique solution Planes meet at one point Columns span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(\mathbf{b}\) reachable uniquely
    No solution Some planes are parallel \(\mathbf{b}\) outside column space
    \(\infty\) solutions Planes share a line or coincide \(\mathbf{b}\) in column space, columns dependent
    Test Pivots all nonzero? \(\det(A) \neq 0\)?
    Built with rigor, curiosity, and a lot of first-principles derivations.
