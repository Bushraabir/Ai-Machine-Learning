## 1. Intuition First

### The Core Idea

[[52.Quickselect — k-th smallest in expected O(n)]] is brilliant but fragile: its O(n) expectation collapses to O(n²) the moment an adversary controls the input. The entire analysis rests on the pivot being "roughly central" — but with a deterministic algorithm and no randomness, an adversary who knows your pivot rule can always feed you the worst case.

Median of medians (MoM) — published by Blum, Floyd, Pratt, Rivest, and Tarjan in 1973, one of the most elegant results in algorithm design — answers the question: **can we choose a pivot deterministically that is guaranteed to split the array at least 30/70, without any randomness, in linear time?**

The answer is yes, and the construction is recursive:

1. **Divide** the array into groups of 5.
2. **Find the median of each group** by sorting each group (5 elements → O(1) per group, O(n/5) groups → O(n) total).
3. **Recurse**: collect those n/5 medians and find _their_ median — the "median of medians."
4. **Use this as the pivot** in partitioning the full array.

Why does this guarantee a good split? The median of the group medians is ≥ half of all group medians, and each group median is ≥ the lower half of its group. This chains into a provable lower bound: the pivot is guaranteed to be ≥ at least ⌈3n/10⌉ elements and ≤ at least ⌈3n/10⌉ elements. In other words, neither partition can be larger than 7n/10 of the original array.

### Why This Exists

Randomised Quickselect's expected O(n) is practically excellent — the probability of hitting O(n²) on random pivot selection is exponentially small. But "expected" is not "guaranteed," and in three contexts, guarantees matter:

1. **Adversarial inputs in security-critical systems.** A server that sorts user-supplied data with a predictable pivot rule can be attacked with specially crafted inputs that force O(n²) behaviour — a denial-of-service via algorithmic complexity attack. This is not hypothetical; it has happened to hash table implementations.
    
2. **Real-time and safety-critical systems.** A sorting routine in a medical device or avionics system cannot have a worst-case blowup. You need deterministic bounds.
    
3. **Theoretical completeness.** The comparison lower bound for sorting is Ω(n log n). Selection is provably easier — MoM proves the selection lower bound is Θ(n). This closes the theoretical question.
    

In practice, `std::nth_element` in libstdc++ uses **introselect**: Quickselect as the primary engine, with MoM as a fallback when recursion depth exceeds 2 log n. MoM's large constants (the group-of-5 bookkeeping) make it slower than Quickselect in practice, but its presence as a fallback is what makes `std::nth_element` O(n) _worst-case_, not just expected.

### The "Aha" Moment

The recursive structure looks circular: to select the k-th element, you need to select the median of n/5 elements. You're calling selection inside selection. How does this not spiral into infinite regression?

The key is the **two different sizes**: the outer problem has n elements, the inner recursive call has n/5 elements. The recurrence T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n) has n/5 + 7n/10 = 9n/10 < n as the total subproblem size. As long as the sum of recursive subproblem sizes is a constant fraction less than 1 times n, the recurrence solves to O(n). This is the Akra-Bazzi / geometric series argument: the O(n) work at each level decreases geometrically, summing to O(n).

The group size of 5 is the _minimum_ that makes the math work. Groups of 3 give only a 4n/6 ≈ 67% bound — the recurrence becomes T(n) ≤ T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + O(n), which solves to O(n log n), not O(n). Groups of 5 push the guarantee to 30%, making the recurrence solvable to O(n). Groups of 7 or larger also work and give tighter splits, but with larger O(1) sorting constants per group.

## 2. Why We Need It — CP + ML Dual Context

### Competitive Programming

**When it appears directly:** Almost never in contest problems. MoM is a theoretical construct. In CP, `std::nth_element` is used when you need a guaranteed O(n) selection, and it uses MoM internally as its worst-case safety net.

**The pattern signal:** If a problem requires:

- O(n) selection with _no randomness allowed_ (e.g., the problem is about deterministic algorithms, or randomisation is explicitly forbidden)
- A pivot guarantee in a custom partitioning scheme
- Understanding why `std::nth_element` never degrades

then MoM is the underlying engine. Directly implementing MoM from scratch in a contest is almost always wrong — it has a large constant factor (~10× slower than `std::nth_element` in practice). Use `std::nth_element` instead.

**The deeper CP relevance:** Understanding MoM teaches you _why_ certain divide-and-conquer recurrences solve to O(n) vs O(n log n). This directly applies to:

- The Master Theorem's three cases
- Why T(n) = T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n) but T(n) = T(n/2) + T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n log n)
- Designing custom divide-and-conquer algorithms with tight bounds

**Common gotcha:** The constant factor. MoM's O(n) has an enormous hidden constant (~50-100× the O(n) of Quickselect in practice). A "faster" asymptotic algorithm can be slower in practice. This is a recurring lesson in algorithm design — asymptotic complexity and practical performance are not the same thing.

### Machine Learning / AI

**1. Introselect in NumPy and the C++ STL — the deployment context** `numpy.partition` and `std::nth_element` both implement introselect, which is MoM + Quickselect. Every time you call `np.partition(arr, k)` or `np.argpartition(distances, k)` in a k-NN implementation, MoM is the safety net ensuring worst-case O(n). In adversarial ML settings — where input data is deliberately crafted to attack your pipeline — this guarantee matters. A model serving endpoint that processes user-provided feature vectors cannot afford O(n²) selection.

**2. Robust statistics — the median as a breakdown-point estimator** In robust ML preprocessing, the median is preferred over the mean because it has a 50% breakdown point: you can corrupt up to half the data and the median is still a good estimate of the center, while the mean breaks with a single outlier. `sklearn.preprocessing.RobustScaler` uses the median and IQR (interquartile range). For large datasets, computing these requires selection at ranks n/4, n/2, 3n/4. Understanding that this is O(n) (not O(n log n)) matters for pipeline profiling: `RobustScaler` on a 10⁸-row dataset is linear, not log-linear.

**3. Deterministic pivot selection in tree-based models** Gradient boosting implementations like XGBoost and LightGBM build trees by finding optimal split points across feature values. For exact split finding (not histogram-based), the algorithm must evaluate all possible split thresholds — a partial-sort operation. The pivot selection strategy for partitioning feature values during tree construction follows the same logic as MoM: you want a pivot that splits the feature distribution roughly in half to balance the recursion tree and bound tree-building time. LightGBM's exact mode uses `std::nth_element`, inheriting MoM's guarantee.

**4. The theoretical connection to PAC learning sample complexity** This is abstract but worth knowing: the O(n) selection lower bound has a cousin in statistical learning theory. Finding the median of a distribution from samples has a sample complexity bound — you need Ω(1/ε²) samples to estimate the ε-approximate median. The Θ(n) algorithmic bound and the statistical sample complexity bound are both "you need to look at everything at least once." MoM makes precise why selection cannot be done in sub-linear comparisons, which parallels why learning cannot be done with sub-linear samples in the worst case.

**5. Streaming median approximation — where MoM fails and what replaces it** MoM requires random access to all n elements. For streaming data (infinite sequence, one pass), it cannot be applied. This motivates:

- **t-digest** (used in Elasticsearch, Apache Spark): maintains a compressed sketch of the distribution, answers quantile queries approximately
- **Greenwald-Khanna algorithm**: ε-approximate quantiles in O(n log(1/ε)) space
- **Two-heap approach** (exact, but offline): [[149.Min-heap and max-heap — heap property and array representation]]

Understanding _why_ MoM needs all data upfront clarifies why streaming systems use approximations rather than exact selection.

## 3. Algorithm / Pattern

### Step-by-Step Logic

**Inputs:** Array `arr` of n elements, target rank `k` (0-indexed).

1. **Base case:** If n ≤ 5, sort the group directly (insertion sort), return `arr[k]`.
    
2. **Divide into groups of 5:** Split `arr` into ⌈n/5⌉ groups. The last group may have fewer than 5 elements.
    
3. **Find each group's median:** Sort each group of 5 (insertion sort, O(1) per group). Extract the median (middle element). This produces an array `M` of ⌈n/5⌉ medians. Total cost: O(n).
    
4. **Recursive call — find the median of medians:** `pivot = SELECT(M, len(M) // 2)` This is a full recursive call on a subproblem of size n/5.
    
5. **Partition the full array around `pivot`:** Rearrange `arr` so that elements < pivot are left, elements > pivot are right. Let `p` be the pivot's final index. Cost: O(n).
    
6. **Rank check and recurse:**
    
    - If `p == k`: return `arr[p]`
    - If `p > k`: recurse on left subarray `arr[0..p-1]`, rank `k`
    - If `p < k`: recurse on right subarray `arr[p+1..n-1]`, rank `k - p - 1`

### The Pivot Quality Guarantee — Worked Out

Claim: The median of medians is ≥ at least ⌈3n/10⌉ − 6 elements and ≤ at least ⌈3n/10⌉ − 6 elements.

```
Groups of 5, sorted (shown as columns, median is row 3):

   G1    G2    G3   ...   G_{n/5}
  [a1]  [b1]  [c1]        [...]
  [a2]  [b2]  [c2]        [...]
  [a3]* [b3]* [c3]*       [m_i]*   ← medians (marked with *)
  [a4]  [b4]  [c4]        [...]
  [a5]  [b5]  [c5]        [...]

Let pivot = median of {a3, b3, c3, ..., m_i}
So pivot ≥ half of all group medians (by definition of median).

For each group whose median ≤ pivot:
  - The median is ≥ the 2 elements below it in the sorted group.
  - So at least 3 elements in that group are ≤ pivot.

Number of groups with median ≤ pivot: ≥ ⌈(n/5)/2⌉ = ⌈n/10⌉

Elements ≤ pivot: ≥ 3 × ⌈n/10⌉ ≥ 3n/10

By symmetry: elements ≥ pivot ≥ 3n/10

Therefore: neither partition exceeds n - 3n/10 = 7n/10 elements.
```

### Complexity Derivation

$$T(n) \leq T!\left(\frac{n}{5}\right) + T!\left(\frac{7n}{10}\right) + O(n)$$

- T(n/5): recursive call to find the median of medians
- T(7n/10): recursive call on the larger partition (at most 7n/10 elements)
- O(n): dividing into groups, sorting each group, partitioning

**Claim:** T(n) = O(n). Proof by substitution: assume T(m) ≤ cm for all m < n.

$$T(n) \leq c\frac{n}{5} + c\frac{7n}{10} + an = \frac{cn}{5} + \frac{7cn}{10} + an = \frac{9cn}{10} + an$$

For this to be ≤ cn: we need cn/10 ≥ an, i.e., c ≥ 10a. Choose c = 10a. ✓

This works because n/5 + 7n/10 = 2n/10 + 7n/10 = **9n/10 < n**. The total subproblem size is a strict fraction of n, so the geometric series converges.

**Why group size 3 fails:** With groups of 3: each group median is ≥ 1 element (not 2). Elements ≤ pivot ≥ 2 × n/6 = n/3. Larger partition ≤ 2n/3.

$$T(n) \leq T!\left(\frac{n}{3}\right) + T!\left(\frac{2n}{3}\right) + O(n)$$

n/3 + 2n/3 = n — the subproblem sizes sum to exactly n. This recurrence solves to **T(n) = O(n log n)**, not O(n). The group-of-5 guarantee is the minimum needed to push the sum strictly below n.

|Group Size|Elements ≤ pivot|Larger Partition|Subproblem Sum|T(n)|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|3|≥ n/3|≤ 2n/3|n/3 + 2n/3 = n|O(n log n)|
|**5**|**≥ 3n/10**|**≤ 7n/10**|**n/5 + 7n/10 = 9n/10**|**O(n)**|
|7|≥ 2n/7|≤ 5n/7|n/7 + 5n/7 = 6n/7|O(n)|
|9|≥ 5n/18|≤ 13n/18|n/9 + 13n/18 = 5n/6|O(n)|

Group size 5 is the canonical choice because it is the _smallest odd number that makes MoM work_ and has the smallest constant factor among valid group sizes.

## 4. Pseudocode

```
SELECT(arr, k):
    """
    Find the k-th smallest element (0-indexed) in arr.
    Guaranteed O(n) worst case via median-of-medians pivot selection.
    """
    n ← length(arr)

    // Base case: small enough to sort directly
    if n ≤ 5:
        INSERTION-SORT(arr)
        return arr[k]

    // STEP 1: Divide into groups of 5
    groups ← SPLIT(arr, group_size=5)
    // Last group may have < 5 elements — that is fine

    // STEP 2: Find the median of each group
    medians ← []
    for each group in groups:
        INSERTION-SORT(group)            // O(1) per group (at most 5 elements)
        medians.append(group[len(group) // 2])   // Middle element = median

    // STEP 3: Recursive call — find the median of medians
    // This is a SELECT call on a problem of size n/5
    pivot ← SELECT(medians, len(medians) // 2)

    // STEP 4: Partition full array around pivot
    // Three-way partition handles duplicates cleanly
    left   ← [x for x in arr if x < pivot]
    middle ← [x for x in arr if x == pivot]
    right  ← [x for x in arr if x > pivot]

    // STEP 5: Rank check — which partition contains rank k?
    if k < len(left):
        return SELECT(left, k)
    else if k < len(left) + len(middle):
        return pivot                     // Pivot is the answer
    else:
        return SELECT(right, k - len(left) - len(middle))
```

**Note on three-way partition:** Using `left / middle / right` (Dutch National Flag style) handles duplicate pivot values correctly and is easier to reason about than in-place Lomuto. The in-place version in Section 5 is more cache-efficient but requires careful index arithmetic.

## 5. Implementation

### C++ (Production-Ready)

```cpp
#include <vector>
#include <algorithm>
#include <stdexcept>

/**
 * Median of Medians Selection — guaranteed O(n) worst-case.
 *
 * Finds the k-th smallest element (0-indexed) in arr.
 * Uses three-way partition for correctness with duplicates.
 *
 * Time:  O(n) worst case (not just expected)
 * Space: O(n) — three-way partition creates auxiliary arrays
 *               Use in-place partition to reduce to O(log n) space
 *
 * Note: In practice, prefer std::nth_element (introselect).
 *       This implementation is for correctness and understanding.
 */

// Sort a small group of ≤5 elements using insertion sort
static void insertionSort(std::vector<int>& arr, int lo, int hi) {
    for (int i = lo + 1; i <= hi; ++i) {
        int key = arr[i];
        int j = i - 1;
        while (j >= lo && arr[j] > key) {
            arr[j + 1] = arr[j];
            --j;
        }
        arr[j + 1] = key;
    }
}

// Find median of a small subarray arr[lo..hi] (modifies arr in range)
static int medianOfGroup(std::vector<int>& arr, int lo, int hi) {
    insertionSort(arr, lo, hi);
    return arr[(lo + hi) / 2];
}

// Core recursive SELECT using median-of-medians pivot
static int selectMoM(std::vector<int> arr, int k) {
    int n = static_cast<int>(arr.size());

    // Base case: sort directly for small arrays
    if (n <= 5) {
        std::sort(arr.begin(), arr.end());
        return arr[k];
    }

    // STEP 1 & 2: Compute medians of groups of 5
    std::vector<int> medians;
    medians.reserve((n + 4) / 5);  // ceil(n/5)

    for (int i = 0; i < n; i += 5) {
        int groupEnd = std::min(i + 4, n - 1);
        // Sort the group in-place within arr
        insertionSort(arr, i, groupEnd);
        medians.push_back(arr[(i + groupEnd) / 2]);  // median of this group
    }

    // STEP 3: Recursive call — find median of medians (size n/5)
    int pivot = selectMoM(medians, (static_cast<int>(medians.size()) - 1) / 2);

    // STEP 4: Three-way partition around pivot
    std::vector<int> left, middle, right;
    left.reserve(n);
    middle.reserve(n);
    right.reserve(n);

    for (int x : arr) {
        if      (x < pivot) left.push_back(x);
        else if (x > pivot) right.push_back(x);
        else                middle.push_back(x);
    }

    // STEP 5: Rank check
    int lSize = static_cast<int>(left.size());
    int mSize = static_cast<int>(middle.size());

    if (k < lSize) {
        return selectMoM(std::move(left), k);
    } else if (k < lSize + mSize) {
        return pivot;  // pivot is at rank k
    } else {
        return selectMoM(std::move(right), k - lSize - mSize);
    }
}

int medianOfMedians(const std::vector<int>& arr, int k) {
    int n = static_cast<int>(arr.size());
    if (k < 0 || k >= n) {
        throw std::out_of_range("k must be in [0, n-1]");
    }
    return selectMoM(arr, k);  // pass by value — we mutate internally
}

// Convenience: compute true median (handles even-length arrays)
double medianValue(const std::vector<int>& arr) {
    int n = static_cast<int>(arr.size());
    if (n == 0) throw std::invalid_argument("Empty array");
    int lo = medianOfMedians(arr, (n - 1) / 2);
    if (n % 2 == 1) return lo;
    int hi = medianOfMedians(arr, n / 2);
    return (lo + hi) / 2.0;
}
```

### Python (Readable + Pythonic)

```python
def _insertion_sort(arr: list[int]) -> list[int]:
    """Sort a small list in-place using insertion sort. O(k²) for k ≤ 5."""
    for i in range(1, len(arr)):
        key = arr[i]
        j = i - 1
        while j >= 0 and arr[j] > key:
            arr[j + 1] = arr[j]
            j -= 1
        arr[j + 1] = key
    return arr


def _group_median(group: list[int]) -> int:
    """Return the median of a list of ≤ 5 elements."""
    _insertion_sort(group)
    return group[len(group) // 2]


def select(arr: list[int], k: int) -> int:
    """
    Median-of-medians selection: find the k-th smallest (0-indexed).

    Guaranteed O(n) worst-case time via a pivot that always splits
    the array at least 30%/70%.

    Args:
        arr: List of integers (not modified — uses auxiliary space).
        k:   0-indexed target rank.

    Returns:
        The k-th smallest element in arr.

    Time:  O(n) worst case  — recurrence T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n)
    Space: O(n) — three-way partition creates auxiliary lists
                  Recursion depth O(log n) by the 9n/10 subproblem bound
    """
    n = len(arr)

    # Base case: sort directly for tiny arrays
    if n <= 5:
        return sorted(arr)[k]

    # STEP 1 & 2: Find median of each group of 5
    groups = [arr[i : i + 5] for i in range(0, n, 5)]
    medians = [_group_median(list(g)) for g in groups]

    # STEP 3: Recursive SELECT on medians (problem size n/5)
    pivot = select(medians, (len(medians) - 1) // 2)

    # STEP 4: Three-way partition around pivot
    left   = [x for x in arr if x < pivot]
    middle = [x for x in arr if x == pivot]
    right  = [x for x in arr if x > pivot]

    # STEP 5: Rank check — descend into exactly one partition
    if k < len(left):
        return select(left, k)
    elif k < len(left) + len(middle):
        return pivot
    else:
        return select(right, k - len(left) - len(middle))


def median(arr: list[int]) -> float:
    """
    Compute the exact median of arr in guaranteed O(n) time.
    Returns float for even-length arrays (average of two middle elements).
    """
    n = len(arr)
    if n == 0:
        raise ValueError("Empty array has no median")
    lo = select(arr, (n - 1) // 2)
    if n % 2 == 1:
        return float(lo)
    hi = select(arr, n // 2)
    return (lo + hi) / 2.0
```

### The Two-Level Recursion: Sizes at Each Level

```
SELECT(n=100, k=?)
│
├── SELECT(n/5 = 20, k=9)          ← find median of 20 medians
│   ├── SELECT(4, ...)              ← (continues, but small)
│   └── [partition, rank check]
│
├── PARTITION(n=100)               ← O(n) work
│
└── SELECT(≤ 7n/10 = 70, k=?)     ← worst case: 7/10 of original

Total work per "level" of the outer recursion:
Level 0: O(100) + recursion on 20 + recursion on ≤70
Level 1: O(20)  + recursion on 4  + recursion on ≤14
         O(70)  + recursion on 14 + recursion on ≤49

Sum of all O(n) costs: 100 + 20 + 70 + 4 + 14 + 49 + ...
                     = 100(1 + 9/10 + (9/10)² + ...) = 100 × 10 = 1000 = O(n) ✓

The geometric series converges because each level costs ≤ (9/10) of the previous.
```

### Group Size Comparison: Why 5 is the Minimum

```

  [a]                                [a]
```

## 7. Common Variations & Extensions

### 7.1 Introselect — MoM as a Safety Net

The algorithm used by `std::nth_element` and `np.partition` in practice. Start with [[52.Quickselect — k-th smallest in expected O(n)]]. Count recursion depth. If depth exceeds a threshold (typically 2 log₂ n or 3 log₂ n), switch to MoM for the remainder. This gives:

- Average case: Quickselect's fast O(n) with small constants
- Worst case: MoM's guaranteed O(n), preventing algorithmic complexity attacks

The threshold is tuned empirically — too low switches to MoM too eagerly (paying MoM's constant factor on benign inputs), too high risks O(n²) on adversarial inputs before MoM kicks in.

### 7.2 Weighted Median via MoM

The **weighted median** is the value w such that the sum of weights of elements ≤ w is ≥ W/2 (W = total weight). It minimises the weighted L1 objective ∑ wᵢ |xᵢ − c|, used in facility location, robust regression, and supply chain optimisation. MoM extends naturally: partition around the MoM pivot, accumulate weights in the left/right partitions, recurse into the half whose cumulative weight crosses W/2. Same O(n) guarantee.

### 7.3 Multi-Dimensional Median — Geometric Median

The geometric median of points in ℝᵈ minimises ∑ᵢ ‖xᵢ − c‖₂. Unlike the 1D median, no closed form exists. Computed iteratively via Weiszfeld's algorithm (IRLS: iteratively reweighted least squares), converging in O(n/ε²) iterations with O(n) work per iteration. Used in robust deep learning (geometric median of gradients defends against Byzantine workers in federated learning — the **Byzantime-robust aggregation** paper by Chen et al., 2017). MoM provides the 1D foundation; extending to 2D+ requires accepting approximate solutions.

### 7.4 k-th Order Statistic on a Stream — Misra-Gries and Mergeable Summaries

When data arrives as a stream (no random access), MoM is inapplicable. **Misra-Gries** finds frequent elements (heavy hitters) in a stream in O(1) per element and O(k) space. **Mergeable quantile summaries** (used in distributed systems like Apache DataSketches) allow approximate quantile queries across distributed streams. The theoretical connection: MoM is the "offline oracle" that motivates why streaming approximations are necessary, and what "approximate" means relative to the exact offline algorithm.

## 9. CP Contest Application

### Why MoM Rarely Appears Directly in Contests

Competitive programming judges run deterministic tests, not adversarial ones. `std::nth_element` already uses introselect and will never hit O(n²). Writing MoM from scratch in a contest costs 30–50 minutes and introduces bugs for zero asymptotic gain over `std::nth_element`.

**The one exception:** Problems that explicitly ask you to implement a _deterministic_ O(n) selection algorithm, or educational rounds about algorithm analysis. These are rare.

### What MoM Teaches That Matters in Contests

**1. Recurrence analysis instinct.** The moment you see T(n) = T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n), you should immediately check: does n/5 + 7n/10 < 1? It does (= 9/10), so O(n). This pattern — "total subproblem fraction < 1 → O(n) at each level → geometric series → O(n) total" — appears in many non-obvious algorithms.

**2. Pivot quality in custom quicksort variants.** Some problems require a custom partitioning scheme on arrays with special structure. Knowing that a 30/70 split guarantees O(n log n) quicksort (depth ≤ log_{10/7}(n) ≈ 6 log n) while a 50/50 split gives exactly O(n log n) (depth log₂ n) helps you reason about whether your custom pivot is good enough.

**3. The constant factor lesson.** MoM is O(n) but slower in practice than O(n) Quickselect. This is a recurring theme in competitive programming: an O(n log log n) algorithm with a constant of 2 beats an O(n) algorithm with a constant of 100 for all practical n. Always estimate the constant, not just the asymptotic.

### Benchmark: MoM vs. Quickselect vs. std::nth_element

```
n = 10^6, finding the median (k = n/2):

Algorithm                     Time (ms)   Worst case
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
std::nth_element (introselect)   ~8ms      O(n)
Randomised Quickselect           ~12ms     O(n²) w.p. negligible
MoM (pure)                       ~90ms     O(n)
std::sort + index                ~65ms     O(n log n)

MoM's constant factor is ~7-10× larger than Quickselect.
This is the gap between theoretical O(n) optimality
and practical performance.
```

The constant factor comes from: (a) recursively solving a subproblem of size n/5 before partitioning, (b) three-way partition with auxiliary allocation, (c) insertion sort overhead across ⌈n/5⌉ groups. Introselect amortises all this — you only pay MoM's cost on the rare branches that would otherwise degenerate, keeping the average case close to pure Quickselect.
