## The Basics

- **Sample Space ($S$)** → just _all the things that could happen_. Roll a die? $S = {1,2,3,4,5,6}$.
- **Event ($A$)** → any subset of that. "Rolling an even number" = ${2, 4, 6}$.

The big insight that made probability _rigorous_ was treating events as **sets**. Suddenly you could use unions ($\cup$), intersections ($\cap$), complements — real math. 

---

### The Naive Definition

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{ number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{ number of total outcomes}}$$

Super intuitive, but it comes with **two big asterisks**:

1. The sample space has to be **finite**.
2. Every outcome has to be **equally likely**.

Flip a fair coin — fine. Model whether it rains tomorrow — not fine.

---
### Two tricks that save you
1. **Label everything.** Even if the problem says "identical balls" — give them names in your head. $b_1, b_2, b_3$. Distinguishable objects are way easier to count.
2. **Check extreme cases.** Set $n = 1$, $k = 0$, whatever feels silly. If the formula breaks in a trivial case, you messed up somewhere. Catch it early.


---

### The Real (Non-Naive) Axioms

A probability function $P$ on sample space $S$ must satisfy:

- **Axiom 1:** $P(\emptyset) = 0$ and $P(S) = 1$
- **Axiom 2:** For _disjoint_ events, $P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + \cdots$
