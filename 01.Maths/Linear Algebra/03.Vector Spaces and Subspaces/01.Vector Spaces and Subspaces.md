## What Even Is a Vector Space?
Most people first meet vectors as arrows or tuples like $(3, -1, 2)$. But the real definition is much broader.

**A vector space $V$ is any set where two operations are defined:**
- **Addition:** you can add any two elements → still get something in $V$
- **Scalar multiplication:** you can multiply any element by a real number → still in $V$
...and these operations obey **8 rules** (axioms).

> [!note] The Big Insight The "vectors" don't have to be arrows or tuples. They can be **polynomials, matrices, functions, solutions to equations** — anything at all, as long as the 8 rules hold.
## The 8 Axioms (Plain English)

Think of these as the "rules of the game." If your set + operations satisfy all 8, you have a vector space.

|#|Name|What it says|Think of it as...|
|---|---|---|---|
|VS1|Commutativity|$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$|Order of addition doesn't matter|
|VS2|Associativity (add)|$(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})$|Grouping doesn't matter|
|VS3|Zero vector exists|$\exists, \mathbf{0}$ such that $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$|There's a "do nothing" element|
|VS4|Additive inverses|$\exists, {-\mathbf{v}}$ such that $\mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$|Every element has an "undo"|
|VS5|Scaling by 1|$1 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$|Multiplying by 1 changes nothing|
|VS6|Associativity (scale)|$(cd)\mathbf{v} = c(d\mathbf{v})$|Chaining scalars works fine|
|VS7|Distribute over vectors|$c(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$|Scalar spreads over addition|
|VS8|Distribute over scalars|$(c+d)\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{v}$|Scalar addition spreads too|

> [!tip] Which ones actually do the heavy lifting?
> 
> - **VS1–VS4** make $(V, +)$ an _abelian group_ — the backbone of addition.
> - **VS5–VS8** control how scalars interact with $V$.
> 
> When checking if something is **NOT** a vector space, the most commonly failed axioms are:
> 
> - **VS3** — zero vector missing (e.g., affine sets like ${x + y = 1}$)
> - **VS4** — no negative/inverse (e.g., positive functions, first quadrant)
> - **VS7/VS8** — distributivity breaks (e.g., weird nonlinear operations)

---

## Free Consequences (You Don't Have to Prove These Separately)

These follow automatically from the 8 axioms:

- $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ — scalar zero kills any vector
- $c \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ — any scalar times the zero vector is zero
- $(-1)\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v}$ — the additive inverse is just "multiply by $-1$"
- The zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ is **unique**
- Additive inverses are **unique**

> [!example] Quick Proof: Why $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ $$0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = (0 + 0)\mathbf{v} \overset{\text{VS8}}{=} 0\mathbf{v} + 0\mathbf{v}$$ Add $-(0\mathbf{v})$ to both sides → $\mathbf{0} = 0\mathbf{v}$ $\square$

## Examples of Vector Spaces

### 1. $\mathbb{R}^n$ — The Familiar One
Vectors are $n$-tuples. Addition and scaling are component wise. $$\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5\\7\\9\end{bmatrix}, \qquad 3\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\6\\9\end{bmatrix}$$
Zero vector: $(0, 0, \ldots, 0)$. **Dimension: $n$.**

---
### 2. $M_{m \times n}$ — Matrices as Vectors
The set of all $m \times n$ real matrices. Add them entry-by-entry, scale all entries.
$$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12\end{bmatrix}$$
Zero vector: the all-zeros matrix. **Dimension: $mn$.**
> [!note] Key Point The vector space structure on matrices **ignores matrix multiplication entirely**. We only use addition and scaling. Think of it as "unrolling" the matrix into a long column vector — $M_{m \times n}$ is essentially $\mathbb{R}^{mn}$ in disguise.

---
### 3. $\mathcal{P}_n$ — Polynomials of Degree $\leq n$
Elements look like: $a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_n x^n$
Add by matching like terms. Scale by multiplying all coefficients.
$$p(x) = 1 + 2x + x^2, \quad q(x) = 3 - x + 4x^2 \implies (p+q)(x) = 4 + x + 5x^2$$
Standard basis: ${1,\ x,\ x^2,\ \ldots,\ x^n}$. **Dimension: $n+1$.**

---
### 4. $C[a,b]$ — Continuous Functions (Infinite-Dimensional!)
Every continuous function $f: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is a "vector."
$$(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), \qquad (cf)(x) = c \cdot f(x)$$
Zero vector: the constant zero function $f(x) = 0$.
**This space is infinite-dimensional** — no finite set of functions spans it. The Fourier basis ${1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots}$ is an infinite basis here (under the $L^2$ inner product). This is where Fourier analysis lives.

---
### 5. ODE Solutions — A Surprising One
>[!note] **ODE = Ordinary Differential Equation.**
>It's an equation involving an unknown function and its derivatives. "Ordinary" means there is only **one independent variable** (usually xx x or tt t).
The contrast:
>- **ODE** → one independent variable, e.g. y(x)y(x) y(x)
>- **PDE** (Partial Differential Equation) → multiple independent variables, e.g. u(x,t)u(x, t) u(x,t)
**General form:**
$F\!\left(x,\; y,\; y',\; y'',\; \ldots,\; y^{(n)}\right)=0,\qquad y=y(x)$ where \(y\) is the unknown function.

The set of all solutions to a second-order linear ODE like $y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = 0$ forms a vector space of **dimension 2**.
If $y_1$ and $y_2$ are two independent solutions, every solution is $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$.

> [!note] Why This Works The ODE operator $L = \frac{d^2}{dx^2} + p\frac{d}{dx} + q$ is **linear**, so: $$L(c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2) = c_1 \underbrace{Ly_1}_{=0} + c_2 \underbrace{Ly_2}_{=0} = 0$$ Combinations of solutions are solutions. The "dimension 2" fact is the existence-uniqueness theorem in linear algebra clothing.

## Non-Examples: What Fails and Why
These look like they could be vector spaces but aren't.
> [!warning] The First Quadrant ${(x,y): x \geq 0, y \geq 0}$ **Fails VS4:** the inverse of $(1, 1)$ is $(-1, -1)$, which is outside the set. Closed under addition and _positive_ scaling — but not all scaling. ✗

> [!warning] The Unit Sphere $|\mathbf{x}| = 1$ **Fails VS3:** the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ has $|\mathbf{0}| = 0 \neq 1$, so it's not in the set. **Fails VS1:** $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1)$, which has length $\sqrt{2} \neq 1$. ✗

> [!warning] Positive Functions ${f : f(x) > 0\ \forall x}$ **Fails VS4:** the inverse of $f(x) = e^x$ is $-e^x$, which is negative. ✗
> 
> **Interesting twist:** if you redefine the operations as $f \oplus g = fg$ and $c \odot f = f^c$, then positive functions _do_ form a vector space! Zero vector becomes $f(x) = 1$. The operations matter just as much as the set.

> [!warning] Affine Subspace ${x + y = 1}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ **Fails VS3:** $(0,0)$ doesn't satisfy $0 + 0 = 1$. Any set defined by $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x} = b$ with $b \neq 0$ fails — it doesn't pass through the origin. ✗

# Subspaces
A **subspace** is a subset of a vector space that is itself a vector space (using the same operations).
### The Subspace Test
You only need to check **2 things** (everything else is inherited automatically):
> [!theorem] Subspace Test A nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace **iff**:
> 
> - **(S1)** Closed under addition: $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \Rightarrow \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W$
> - **(S2)** Closed under scalar multiplication: $\mathbf{v} \in W, c \in \mathbb{R} \Rightarrow c\mathbf{v} \in W$

These two collapse into **one condition**: $$\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \text{ and } c, d \in \mathbb{R} \implies c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v} \in W$$ i.e., $W$ is closed under all **linear combinations**.

> [!warning] Quick Disqualifier: Check for the Zero Vector First **Every subspace must contain $\mathbf{0}$.**
> 
> If $\mathbf{0} \notin W$ → not a subspace, stop immediately.
> 
> Why? Take $c = 0$ in S2: $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \in W$.
> 
> This instantly rules out affine sets, spheres, and anything with a nonzero constant constraint.

## Subspace Examples

### In $\mathbb{R}^n$

| Set                                      | Subspace? | Dimension |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------- | --------- |
| ${\mathbf{0}}$                           | ✓         | 0         |
| Any line through origin: ${t\mathbf{v}}$ | ✓         | 1         |
| Any plane through origin                 | ✓         | 2         |
| All of $\mathbb{R}^n$                    | ✓         | $n$       |
| First quadrant                           | ✗         | —         |

### In $\mathcal{P}_n$

- $\mathcal{P}_k$ for $k \leq n$ — dimension $k+1$
- Even polynomials: ${a_0 + a_2 x^2 + \ldots}$ — closed under addition and scaling ✓
- Polynomials with $p(0) = 0$: ${a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \ldots}$ — dimension $n$

### In $M_{n \times n}$

|Subspace|Dimension|
|---|---|
|Symmetric matrices ${A : A = A^T}$|$\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$|
|Skew-symmetric matrices ${A : A = -A^T}$|$\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$|
|Diagonal matrices|$n$|
|Upper triangular matrices|$\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$|

> [!note] Every Matrix = Symmetric + Skew-Symmetric $$A = \underbrace{\frac{A + A^T}{2}}_{\text{symmetric}} + \underbrace{\frac{A - A^T}{2}}_{\text{skew-symmetric}}$$ So $M_{n\times n} = \text{Sym}_n \oplus \text{Skew}_n$. Dimension check: $\frac{n(n+1)}{2} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = n^2$ ✓

## The Four Fundamental Subspaces

For any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with rank $r$, there are four natural subspaces attached to it:

| Subspace                                        | Definition                                           | Lives in       | Dimension |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | --------- |
| [[02.Column Space\|Column Space]] $C(A)$        | All outputs $A\mathbf{x}$                            | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $r$       |
| [[03.Null Space\|Null Space]] $N(A)$       | All inputs where $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$          | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $n - r$   |
| [[04.Row space\|Row Space]] $C(A^T)$            | Span of rows of $A$                                  | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $r$       |
| [[05.Left null space\|Left Null Space]]$N(A^T)$ | All $\mathbf{y}$ where $A^T \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $m - r$   |

> [!warning] Common Confusion $C(A)$ and $N(A)$ live in **different spaces** — you cannot compare them directly.
> 
> - $N(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ → lives in the **input** space
> - $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ → lives in the **output** space
> 
> The **rank-nullity theorem** connects their _dimensions_, not the spaces themselves: $$\dim C(A) + \dim N(A) = n$$

### Worked Example

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2&0\\
0&0&1\\
2&4&0
\end{bmatrix}
\;\xrightarrow{\,R_3-2R_1\,}\;
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Pivot columns: $1,3$ • Free variable: $x_2$ • Rank: $r=2$

| Subspace | Basis | Dimension |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| $C(A)$ | $\displaystyle \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ | $2$ |
| $N(A)$ | $\displaystyle \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ | $1$ |
| $C(A^T)$ | $\displaystyle \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ | $2$ |
| $N(A^T)$ | $\displaystyle \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ | $1$ |

**Dimension checks**
$\dim C(A)+\dim N(A)=2+1=3$
$\dim C(A^T)+\dim N(A^T)=2+1=3$✓

## Solvability and the Column Space

> [!theorem] When Does $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ Have a Solution? $$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \text{ is solvable} \iff \mathbf{b} \in C(A)$$
> 
> $A\mathbf{x}$ is literally a linear combination of the columns of $A$. So $\mathbf{b}$ must be reachable from those columns.

**Structure of all solutions:** if $\mathbf{x}_p$ is _any one_ particular solution, then: $${\text{all solutions}} = \mathbf{x}_p + N(A)$$

This is called an **affine subspace** — a shifted copy of the null space. It's parallel to $N(A)$ but doesn't pass through the origin (unless $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$).

## Span as the Smallest Subspace

Given vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k \in V$, their **span** is the set of all linear combinations:

$$
\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k)
=
\left\{
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_k\mathbf{v}_k
:\;
c_i\in\mathbb{R}
\right\}
$$

The span is always a subspace of $V$, and it is the **smallest subspace** containing all of the vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k$.

$$
C(A)=\operatorname{span}(\text{columns of }A)
$$

## Subspace Arithmetic

Two ways to combine subspaces always produce subspaces:

**Intersection $W_1 \cap W_2$:** vectors in _both_ subspaces → still a subspace.

**Sum $W_1 + W_2$:** vectors of the form $\mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{w}_2$ → the _smallest_ subspace containing both.

> [!warning] Union is NOT a Subspace (usually) $W_1 \cup W_2$ fails in general.
> 
> **Example in $\mathbb{R}^2$:** Let $W_1$ be the $x$-axis and $W_2$ be the $y$-axis. $(1,0) \in W_1$ and $(0,1) \in W_2$, but $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1) \notin W_1 \cup W_2$. ✗
> 
> Union is a subspace **only if** one is contained in the other.

**Dimension formula:** $$\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$$

This is the inclusion-exclusion principle for subspaces — same idea as $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$.

## All Subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$ — Classified

|Dimension|Geometric Object|Example|
|---|---|---|
|0|Just the origin ${\mathbf{0}}$|Trivial subspace|
|1|Line through origin|${t(1,1,0)}$|
|2|Plane through origin|${(x,y,z): 2x - y + z = 0}$|
|3|All of $\mathbb{R}^3$|$\mathbb{R}^3$ itself|

Nothing else is possible. No "in-between" dimensions.

## Direct Sum
$V = W_1 \oplus W_2$ means:
1. Every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ can be written as $\mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{w}_2$ with $\mathbf{w}_1 \in W_1, \mathbf{w}_2 \in W_2$
2. This decomposition is **unique** (which requires $W_1 \cap W_2 = {\mathbf{0}}$)

**Key examples:**
- $M_{n\times n} = \text{Sym}_n \oplus \text{Skew}_n$
- $\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A)$ (row space and null space — orthogonal complements)
- $\mathcal{P}_n = \text{even polynomials} \oplus \text{odd polynomials}$

$$\dim(W_1 \oplus W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2$$

## Quick Reference: Checklist

### Is $W$ a subspace of $V$?

1. Is $\mathbf{0} \in W$? → No → **Not a subspace. Stop.**
2. Is $W$ closed under addition? → No → **Not a subspace. Stop.**
3. Is $W$ closed under scalar multiplication (including negative scalars)? → No → **Not a subspace. Stop.**
4. All yes → **Subspace ✓**

### What are the four subspaces of $A$?

1. Row reduce $A$ → identify pivot columns and free variables
2. Rank $r$ = number of pivots
3. $C(A)$: span of _original_ pivot columns; $\dim = r$
4. $N(A)$: solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$; $\dim = n - r$
5. $C(A^T)$: nonzero rows of reduced form; $\dim = r$
6. $N(A^T)$: solve $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$; $\dim = m - r$
