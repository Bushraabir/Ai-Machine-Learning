> [!note] Why Abstract Vector Spaces?
> 
> For the first half of linear algebra, $\mathbb{R}^n$ is enough. Vectors are tuples, matrices act on them, done. But this conflates **the structure** with **one particular instance of it**. The axioms of a vector space isolate exactly what properties are needed for linear algebra to work — and once you see which axioms are load-bearing, three things happen:
> 
> - You realize that **polynomials, matrices, continuous functions, and differential equations** all obey the same theorems as $\mathbb{R}^n$ — because they satisfy the same axioms
> - Proofs that seemed to depend on coordinates become **coordinate-free** — they work in any vector space
> - The four fundamental subspaces stop being four separate definitions and become **one definition applied four times**
> 
> The one insight: **a vector space is not a space of arrows. It is any set where addition and scaling are self-consistent. The eight axioms are exactly the minimum needed for that consistency.**

---

## The Eight Axioms

A **vector space** over $\mathbb{R}$ is a set $V$ together with two operations:

- **Addition:** $V \times V \to V$, written $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$
- **Scalar multiplication:** $\mathbb{R} \times V \to V$, written $c\mathbf{v}$

satisfying the following eight axioms for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V$ and all $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$:

|#|Name|Statement|
|---|---|---|
|VS1|Commutativity|$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$|
|VS2|Associativity of addition|$(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$|
|VS3|Additive identity|$\exists, \mathbf{0} \in V$ such that $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$ for all $\mathbf{v}$|
|VS4|Additive inverse|$\forall, \mathbf{v} \in V,\ \exists, {-\mathbf{v}} \in V$ such that $\mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$|
|VS5|Multiplicative identity|$1 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$|
|VS6|Associativity of scaling|$(cd)\mathbf{v} = c(d\mathbf{v})$|
|VS7|Distributivity over vectors|$c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$|
|VS8|Distributivity over scalars|$(c + d)\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{v}$|

The elements of $V$ are called **vectors** — but this word now means any object satisfying these eight rules. Not arrows. Not tuples. Any object at all.

> [!note] Which Axioms Do the Real Work?
> 
> VS1, VS2, VS3, VS4 make $(V, +)$ an **abelian group** — this is the additive backbone. VS5–VS8 govern how scalars interact with $V$ — these are the **module axioms** over $\mathbb{R}$.
> 
> The most commonly violated axioms when checking non-examples:
> 
> - **VS3** (zero must be in the set) — rules out affine subspaces like ${x + y = 1}$
> - **VS4** (inverses must be in the set) — rules out the first quadrant, positive functions
> - **VS7/VS8** (distributivity) — fails for nonlinear scaling operations

> [!tip] Consequences Derivable from the Axioms Alone
> 
> These are not additional assumptions — they follow from VS1–VS8:
> 
> - $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ for all $\mathbf{v}$ (the scalar zero kills any vector)
> - $c \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ for all $c$ (any scalar times zero vector is zero)
> - $(-1)\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v}$ (the additive inverse is scalar multiplication by $-1$)
> - The zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ is unique
> - Additive inverses are unique
> 
> **Proof of $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:** $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = (0 + 0)\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} + 0\mathbf{v}$ by VS8. Adding $-(0\mathbf{v})$ to both sides gives $\mathbf{0} = 0\mathbf{v}$. $\square$

## Examples of Vector Spaces

### $\mathbb{R}^n$ — The Prototype

Vectors are $n$-tuples. Addition and scalar multiplication are componentwise:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}u_1 + v_1 \ \vdots \ u_n + v_n\end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix}cu_1 \ \vdots \ cu_n\end{bmatrix}$$

Zero vector: $(0, 0, \ldots, 0)$. All eight axioms hold by properties of $\mathbb{R}$. **Dimension $n$.**

### $M_{m \times n}$ — Matrices as Vectors

The set of all $m \times n$ real matrices. Addition is entrywise, scalar multiplication scales every entry:

$$\begin{bmatrix}a & b \ c & d\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}e & f \ g & h\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}a+e & b+f \ c+g & d+h\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda\begin{bmatrix}a & b \ c & d\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda a & \lambda b \ \lambda c & \lambda d\end{bmatrix}$$

Zero vector: the $m \times n$ zero matrix. **Dimension $mn$.**

> [!note] Why Matrices Form a Vector Space
> 
> $M_{m \times n}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{mn}$ as a vector space — just unroll the matrix into a column. The vector space structure on matrices ignores matrix multiplication entirely; it only uses the additive and scaling structure. The basis for $M_{2 \times 2}$ is the four elementary matrices $E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{21}, E_{22}$, where $E_{ij}$ has a $1$ in position $(i,j)$ and zeros elsewhere.

### $\mathcal{P}_n$ — Polynomials of Degree $\leq n$

$$\mathcal{P}_n = {a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_n x^n : a_i \in \mathbb{R}}$$

Addition: add coefficients of like terms. Scalar multiplication: scale all coefficients. Zero vector: $p(x) = 0$ (all coefficients zero).

Standard basis: ${1,\ x,\ x^2,\ \ldots,\ x^n}$. **Dimension $n + 1$.**

> [!example] Polynomial Addition in $\mathcal{P}_2$
> 
> $p(x) = 1 + 2x + x^2$ and $q(x) = 3 - x + 4x^2$
> 
> $(p + q)(x) = 4 + x + 5x^2$ — add corresponding coefficients
> 
> $3p(x) = 3 + 6x + 3x^2$ — scale all coefficients

### $C[a,b]$ — Continuous Functions (Infinite-Dimensional)

The set of all continuous functions $f : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$:

$$(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), \qquad (cf)(x) = c \cdot f(x)$$

Zero vector: the function $f(x) = 0$ identically. Axioms hold pointwise from properties of $\mathbb{R}$.

This is **infinite-dimensional** — no finite set of functions spans it. The Fourier basis ${1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots}$ is an infinite orthonormal basis in this space (under the $L^2$ inner product). This is where Fourier analysis lives.

### $\mathcal{P}$ — All Polynomials (Infinite-Dimensional, Countable Basis)

$\mathcal{P} = \bigcup_{n=0}^\infty \mathcal{P}_n$ — polynomials of any finite degree. Basis: ${1, x, x^2, x^3, \ldots}$ — countably infinite. Every element is a **finite** linear combination of basis vectors, but the basis itself is infinite.

### Solutions to Linear ODEs

The set of all solutions to $y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = 0$ on an interval $[a,b]$ is a vector space of **dimension 2** — spanned by any two linearly independent solutions $y_1, y_2$.

> [!note] Why ODE Solutions Form a Vector Space
> 
> If $y_1$ and $y_2$ both satisfy $Ly = 0$ (where $L = \frac{d^2}{dx^2} + p\frac{d}{dx} + q$ is a linear operator), then so does $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$, because $L$ is linear: $L(c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2) = c_1 Ly_1 + c_2 Ly_2 = 0$.
> 
> The solution space being 2-dimensional is the existence-uniqueness theorem for second-order linear ODEs in linear algebraic clothing.

## Non-Examples: What Fails and Why

> [!warning] The First Quadrant of $\mathbb{R}^2$
> 
> $V = {(x, y) : x \geq 0,\ y \geq 0}$
> 
> **Fails VS4:** The additive inverse of $(1,1)$ is $(-1,-1) \notin V$. **Fails VS8 (scalar mult. by negatives):** $(-1)(1,1) = (-1,-1) \notin V$.
> 
> Closed under addition, closed under _positive_ scaling, but not under all scalar multiplication. Not a vector space.

> [!warning] The Unit Sphere $S^{n-1} = {\mathbf{x} : |\mathbf{x}| = 1}$
> 
> **Fails VS3:** The zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ has $|\mathbf{0}| = 0 \neq 1$, so $\mathbf{0} \notin S^{n-1}$. **Fails VS1:** $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1)$, and $|(1,1)| = \sqrt{2} \neq 1$.
> 
> The sphere is not closed under addition and does not contain the zero vector.

> [!warning] Positive Functions ${f : f(x) > 0\ \forall x}$
> 
> **Fails VS4:** The additive inverse of $f(x) = e^x$ is $-e^x$, which is negative everywhere — not in the set.
> 
> Note: with a _different_ addition (define $f \oplus g = fg$, $c \odot f = f^c$), positive functions _do_ form a vector space! The zero vector becomes $f(x) = 1$, and the axioms hold. This shows the operations matter, not just the set.

> [!warning] Affine Subspaces: ${x + y = 1}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$
> 
> **Fails VS3:** $(0,0)$ does not satisfy $0 + 0 = 1$. Zero vector missing. **Fails VS1:** $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1)$, and $1 + 1 = 2 \neq 1$.
> 
> Any set defined by $a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b$ with $b \neq 0$ fails immediately — does not pass through the origin.

## Subspaces

A nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ is a **subspace** of $V$ if it is itself a vector space under the same operations inherited from $V$.

Since $W$ inherits operations from $V$, the distributivity, commutativity, and associativity axioms are automatic. Only closure needs checking.

> [!theorem] The Subspace Test
> 
> A nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace of $V$ **if and only if**:
> 
> **(S1)** $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \Rightarrow \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W$ — closed under addition
> 
> **(S2)** $\mathbf{v} \in W,\ c \in \mathbb{R} \Rightarrow c\mathbf{v} \in W$ — closed under scalar multiplication
> 
> **Proof ($\Leftarrow$):** Let $\mathbf{v} \in W$ (nonempty). By S2: $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \in W$ (zero vector present). By S2: $(-1)\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v} \in W$ (inverses present). All other axioms inherited from $V$. $\square$

> [!note] Combined Form
> 
> S1 and S2 collapse into one condition: $$\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \text{ and } c, d \in \mathbb{R} \implies c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v} \in W$$
> 
> $W$ is closed under all **linear combinations**. This is the single cleanest statement of the subspace condition.

> [!warning] The Zero Vector Test — Fastest Disqualifier
> 
> **Every subspace must contain $\mathbf{0}$.**
> 
> If $\mathbf{0} \notin W$, then $W$ is not a subspace — full stop. No further checking needed. This is derived by taking $c = 0$ in S2: $0 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \in W$.
> 
> Use this first. It eliminates affine sets, spheres, and any set with a nonzero constraint on all elements.

## Subspace Examples

### In $\mathbb{R}^n$

- **${\mathbf{0}}$**: trivially a subspace (dimension 0)
- **Any line through the origin**: ${t\mathbf{v} : t \in \mathbb{R}}$ for fixed $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ — dimension 1
- **Any plane through the origin**: ${s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v} : s,t \in \mathbb{R}}$ for linearly independent $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ — dimension 2
- **$\mathbb{R}^n$ itself**: dimension $n$

### In $\mathcal{P}_n$

- $\mathcal{P}_k$ for $k \leq n$ (polynomials of degree $\leq k$) — dimension $k+1$
- Even polynomials: ${a_0 + a_2 x^2 + a_4 x^4 + \cdots}$ — closed under addition and scaling ✓
- Polynomials with $p(0) = 0$: ${a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_n x^n}$ — dimension $n$

### In $M_{n \times n}$

- Symmetric matrices: ${A : A = A^T}$ — dimension $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$
- Skew-symmetric matrices: ${A : A = -A^T}$ — dimension $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$
- Diagonal matrices — dimension $n$
- Upper triangular matrices — dimension $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$

> [!note] Symmetric + Skew = All of $M_{n\times n}$
> 
> Every matrix decomposes uniquely as symmetric + skew-symmetric: $$A = \underbrace{\frac{A + A^T}{2}}_{\text{symmetric}} + \underbrace{\frac{A - A^T}{2}}_{\text{skew-symmetric}}$$
> 
> So $M_{n\times n}$ is the **direct sum** of the symmetric and skew-symmetric subspaces: $M_{n\times n} = \text{Sym}_n \oplus \text{Skew}_n$. Dimension check: $\frac{n(n+1)}{2} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = n^2$ ✓

### In $C[a,b]$

- Even functions: ${f : f(-x) = f(x)}$ — infinite-dimensional subspace
- Functions vanishing at endpoints: ${f \in C[a,b] : f(a) = f(b) = 0}$ — infinite-dimensional
- Solutions to $f'' + f = 0$: $\text{span}{\cos x, \sin x}$ — **2-dimensional** subspace of $C[-\pi, \pi]$

## The Four Fundamental Subspaces

For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with rank $r$, four subspaces are naturally attached to $A$:

$$\begin{array}{lll} \textbf{Subspace} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Lives in} \ \hline C(A) & {A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n} = \text{span of columns of } A & \mathbb{R}^m \ N(A) & {\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}} & \mathbb{R}^n \ C(A^T) & {A^T\mathbf{y} : \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m} = \text{span of rows of } A & \mathbb{R}^n \ N(A^T) & {\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m : A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}} & \mathbb{R}^m \end{array}$$

> [!note] Why Each Is a Subspace — One Argument Covers All
> 
> All four subspaces follow from the same template: a linear map sends subspaces to subspaces.
> 
> **$C(A)$:** If $\mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{c} = A\mathbf{y}$, then $\alpha\mathbf{b} + \beta\mathbf{c} = A(\alpha\mathbf{x} + \beta\mathbf{y}) \in C(A)$. ✓
> 
> **$N(A)$:** If $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ and $A\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$, then $A(\alpha\mathbf{x} + \beta\mathbf{y}) = \alpha A\mathbf{x} + \beta A\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$. ✓
> 
> $C(A^T)$ and $N(A^T)$: replace $A$ with $A^T$ in the same arguments. ✓

> [!warning] Common Confusion: $C(A)$ and $N(A)$ Live in Different Spaces
> 
> $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ (codomain — the output space) $N(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ (domain — the input space)
> 
> They cannot be compared directly. The rank-nullity theorem relates their **dimensions**, but the spaces themselves are entirely different objects. A vector in $N(A)$ is an input; a vector in $C(A)$ is an output. Conflating them is one of the most persistent early confusions in linear algebra.

### Worked Example: All Four Subspaces

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \ 0 & 0 & 1 \ 2 & 4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$$

**Row reduce:**

$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_1$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \ 0 & 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Pivots in columns 1 and 3. Free variable: $x_2$. **Rank $r = 2$.**

**Column space $C(A)$:** span of pivot columns of the original $A$: $$C(A) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\0\2\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}0\1\0\end{bmatrix}\right} \quad \dim = 2$$

**Null space $N(A)$:** $x_2 = t$ (free), $x_1 = -2t$, $x_3 = 0$: $$N(A) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}-2\1\0\end{bmatrix}\right} \quad \dim = 1$$

**Row space $C(A^T)$:** span of nonzero rows of the row-reduced form: $$C(A^T) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\2\0\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}0\0\1\end{bmatrix}\right} \quad \dim = 2$$

**Left null space $N(A^T)$:** $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$; the row $[2, 0, -1]$ of the elimination matrix gives: $$N(A^T) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}2\0\-1\end{bmatrix}\right} \quad \dim = 1$$

**Dimension check:** $2 + 1 = 3$ ✓ (domain), $2 + 1 = 3$ ✓ (codomain).

## The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra — Part 1

> [!theorem] Dimensions of the Four Fundamental Subspaces
> 
> If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has rank $r$, then:
> 
> |Subspace|Dimension|
> |---|---|
> |$C(A)$|$r$|
> |$N(A)$|$n - r$|
> |$C(A^T)$|$r$|
> |$N(A^T)$|$m - r$|
> 
> **Consistency checks:** $r + (n-r) = n$ and $r + (m-r) = m$.
> 
> The **rank-nullity theorem** is the $n$-side: $\dim C(A) + \dim N(A) = n$.

> [!note] Part 2 — Orthogonality (Preview)
> 
> The deeper statement — proved after inner products:
> 
> $$C(A) \perp N(A^T) \quad \text{in } \mathbb{R}^m, \qquad C(A^T) \perp N(A) \quad \text{in } \mathbb{R}^n$$
> 
> The column space and left null space are **orthogonal complements** in $\mathbb{R}^m$. The row space and null space are **orthogonal complements** in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> Every $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ splits uniquely as $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_r + \mathbf{x}_n$ where $\mathbf{x}_r \in C(A^T)$ and $\mathbf{x}_n \in N(A)$. This is the SVD picture.

## Solvability and the Column Space

> [!theorem] Solvability Criterion
> 
> The system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution **if and only if** $\mathbf{b} \in C(A)$.
> 
> **Proof:** $A\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n$ — a linear combination of the columns of $A$. So $\mathbf{b}$ is achievable iff $\mathbf{b}$ is in the span of the columns, i.e., $\mathbf{b} \in C(A)$. $\square$

**Structure of the complete solution:** If $\mathbf{x}_p$ is any particular solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{x}_n \in N(A)$, then $\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{x}_n$ is also a solution. Moreover, **every** solution has this form:

$${\mathbf{x} : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{x}_p + N(A) \qquad \text{(an affine subspace — not a subspace unless } \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\text{)}$$

> [!note] Affine Subspaces
> 
> The solution set of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ (with $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$) is an **affine subspace** — a translate of the null space. It does not contain $\mathbf{0}$ (since $A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \neq \mathbf{b}$), so it is not a subspace. It is a coset of $N(A)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> Geometrically: the null space is a subspace (passes through origin); the solution set is a parallel copy of it, shifted by $\mathbf{x}_p$.

## Span as the Smallest Subspace

Given $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k \in V$, the **span** is:

$$\text{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k) = \left{\sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{v}_i : c_i \in \mathbb{R}\right}$$

> [!theorem] Span is the Smallest Containing Subspace
> 
> $\text{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k)$ is a subspace. Moreover, it is the **smallest** subspace of $V$ containing all $\mathbf{v}_i$.
> 
> **Proof (subspace):** Let $\mathbf{u} = \sum c_i \mathbf{v}_i$ and $\mathbf{w} = \sum d_i \mathbf{v}_i$. Then $\alpha\mathbf{u} + \beta\mathbf{w} = \sum(\alpha c_i + \beta d_i)\mathbf{v}_i \in \text{span}$. ✓
> 
> **Proof (smallest):** Any subspace $W$ containing all $\mathbf{v}_i$ must contain all their linear combinations by closure — hence $\text{span}(\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_k) \subseteq W$. $\square$

$$C(A) = \text{span}(\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n)$$

The column space is the span of the columns. Every subspace can be expressed as a span; every span is a subspace.

## Subspace Arithmetic

Two operations on subspaces always produce subspaces:

**Intersection $W_1 \cap W_2$:**

If $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W_1 \cap W_2$, then $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W_1$ and $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W_2$. By closure in each, $c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v} \in W_1$ and $c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v} \in W_2$, so $c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v} \in W_1 \cap W_2$. ✓

**Sum $W_1 + W_2 = {\mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{w}_2 : \mathbf{w}_1 \in W_1,\ \mathbf{w}_2 \in W_2}$:**

Also a subspace — the smallest subspace containing both $W_1$ and $W_2$.

> [!warning] Union is NOT a Subspace (in General)
> 
> $W_1 \cup W_2$ fails closure under addition unless one contains the other.
> 
> **Standard example in $\mathbb{R}^2$:** Let $W_1 = $ $x$-axis, $W_2 = $ $y$-axis. $(1,0) \in W_1$ and $(0,1) \in W_2$, but $(1,0) + (0,1) = (1,1) \notin W_1 \cup W_2$.
> 
> The union of two subspaces is a subspace iff one is contained in the other.

**Dimension formula for the sum:**

$$\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$$

This is the **inclusion-exclusion principle for subspaces** — the direct analogue of $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$ for finite sets.

## Complete Classification of Subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$

Every subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is **exactly one** of:

|Dimension|Geometric Type|Description|
|---|---|---|
|0|Point|${\mathbf{0}}$ — the trivial subspace|
|1|Line|${t\mathbf{v} : t \in \mathbb{R}}$ for fixed $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ — through origin|
|2|Plane|${\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{x} = 0}$ for fixed $\mathbf{n} \neq \mathbf{0}$ — through origin|
|3|All of $\mathbb{R}^3$|$\mathbb{R}^3$ itself|

Nothing else. Dimension is always a non-negative integer bounded above by 3. There is no "2.5-dimensional subspace."

> [!note] The Plane as a Null Space
> 
> Every 2-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the null space of some $1 \times 3$ matrix: $${(x,y,z) : ax + by + cz = 0} = N!\left(\begin{bmatrix}a & b & c\end{bmatrix}\right)$$
> 
> Every 1-dimensional subspace (line) is the column space of some $3 \times 1$ matrix: $${t\mathbf{v} : t \in \mathbb{R}} = C!\left(\begin{bmatrix}v_1 \ v_2 \ v_3\end{bmatrix}\right)$$
> 
> The four fundamental subspaces unify all subspace geometry.

## Direct Sum

A vector space $V$ is the **direct sum** of subspaces $W_1$ and $W_2$, written $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$, if:

1. $V = W_1 + W_2$ (every vector in $V$ is a sum of one from each)
2. $W_1 \cap W_2 = {\mathbf{0}}$ (the only overlap is zero)

Equivalently: every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ has a **unique** decomposition $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{w}_2$.

> [!example] Direct Sums in Practice
> 
> - $\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A)$ — row space and null space (orthogonal complement, Part 2)
> - $M_{n\times n} = \text{Sym}_n \oplus \text{Skew}_n$ — every matrix = symmetric + skew-symmetric
> - $\mathcal{P}_n = \text{Even polynomials} \oplus \text{Odd polynomials}$ (for even $n$)
> 
> **Dimension of a direct sum:** $\dim(W_1 \oplus W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2$ (no overlap means no cancellation in inclusion-exclusion).

## Summary: The Subspace Hierarchy

```
Vector Space V
│
├── Subspace W ⊆ V
│     ├── Must contain 0  ← fastest check
│     ├── Closed under addition
│     └── Closed under scalar multiplication
│
├── For A ∈ ℝ^{m×n}, rank r:
│     ├── C(A)   ⊆ ℝ^m,  dim = r        [column space]
│     ├── N(A)   ⊆ ℝ^n,  dim = n-r      [null space]
│     ├── C(Aᵀ)  ⊆ ℝ^n,  dim = r        [row space]
│     └── N(Aᵀ)  ⊆ ℝ^m,  dim = m-r      [left null space]
│
└── Subspace arithmetic:
      ├── Intersection: always a subspace
      ├── Sum: always a subspace
      └── Union: NOT a subspace (in general)
```

> [!tip] Decision Rule
> 
> - Is $W$ a subspace? → Check $\mathbf{0} \in W$ first (kills most counterexamples instantly)
> - Proving $W$ is a subspace? → Show closure under linear combinations $c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v} \in W$
> - Finding $C(A)$? → Row reduce; pivot columns of **original** $A$ form the basis
> - Finding $N(A)$? → Row reduce; express free variables, write solution as span
