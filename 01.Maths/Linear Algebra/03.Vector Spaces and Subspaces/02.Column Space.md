> [!note] Why Column Space?
> 
> Every question about $Ax = b$ reduces to one question: **is $b$ in the column space of $A$?**
> 
> - Linear systems have a solution ↔ $b \in \mathcal{C}(A)$
> - Least squares finds the projection of $b$ onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$
> - The rank of $A$ is the dimension of $\mathcal{C}(A)$
> - PCA, SVD, and low-rank approximation are all about finding the "best" column space
> 
> Understanding column space is not optional. It is the geometric language in which all of linear algebra speaks.

---

## Definition

Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

$$\mathcal{C}(A) = { A\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n } = \left{ \sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j \mathbf{a}_j \mid x_j \in \mathbb{R} \right}$$

> [!theorem] Column Space is a Subspace $\mathcal{C}(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$. It contains the zero vector (take $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$), is closed under addition, and closed under scalar multiplication — inherited directly from the linearity of matrix-vector multiplication.

The column space lives in $\mathbb{R}^m$ (the **output space**), not $\mathbb{R}^n$ (the **input space**). This is the single most important fact to internalize.

---

## Geometric Picture

$A\mathbf{x}$ forms **all possible linear combinations** of the columns of $A$. As $\mathbf{x}$ ranges over all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the output $A\mathbf{x}$ sweeps out a flat subspace — a plane, a line, or all of $\mathbb{R}^m$ — depending on how many columns are linearly independent.

```
A is 3×2:          columns a1, a2 ∈ ℝ³

         z
         |   /a2
         |  /
         | /   ← C(A) is a 2D plane through the origin in ℝ³
         |/_________ y
        /
       / a1
      x

Any b NOT in this plane → Ax = b has no solution.
Any b IN this plane → Ax = b has at least one solution.
```

> [!example] Concrete 3×2 Example
> 
> $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Columns: $\mathbf{a}_1 = (1, 0, 2)^T$ and $\mathbf{a}_2 = (0, 1, 3)^T$.
> 
> $\mathcal{C}(A) = { x_1(1,0,2)^T + x_2(0,1,3)^T } = { (x_1,\ x_2,\ 2x_1 + 3x_2)^T \mid x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}}$
> 
> This is the plane $2x + 3y - z = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Any $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)^T$ with $2b_1 + 3b_2 \neq b_3$ has **no solution**.

## Column Space and the Existence of Solutions

> [!theorem] Solvability Condition The system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution **if and only if** $\mathbf{b} \in \mathcal{C}(A)$.

**Proof (both directions):**

- $(\Rightarrow)$ If $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution $\mathbf{x}^_$, then $\mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{x}^_ = \sum_j x_j^* \mathbf{a}_j \in \mathcal{C}(A)$ by definition.
- $(\Leftarrow)$ If $\mathbf{b} \in \mathcal{C}(A)$, then $\mathbf{b} = \sum_j c_j \mathbf{a}_j$ for some scalars $c_j$. Take $\mathbf{x}^* = (c_1, \ldots, c_n)^T$. Then $A\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{b}$. $\square$

> [!warning] Common Confusion: "Solvable" vs "Unique Solution" $\mathbf{b} \in \mathcal{C}(A)$ tells you a solution **exists** — it says nothing about **uniqueness**.
> 
> - Existence is governed by the column space (output side)
> - Uniqueness is governed by the **null space** $\mathcal{N}(A)$ (input side)
> 
> See [[Null Space and Left Null Space]].

## Rank = Dimension of Column Space

> [!theorem] Rank The **rank** of $A$ is: $$\text{rank}(A) = \dim(\mathcal{C}(A)) = \text{number of linearly independent columns of } A$$

The rank is computed by row reduction. The **pivot columns** of $A$ (in reduced row echelon form) form a basis for $\mathcal{C}(A)$.

> [!warning] Trap: Use Pivot Columns from the Original $A$, Not from RREF Row reduction changes the column vectors. The pivot **positions** (column indices) are identified from the RREF, but the basis vectors for $\mathcal{C}(A)$ are taken from the corresponding columns of the **original** $A$.
> 
> **Wrong:** basis = pivot columns of RREF **Correct:** basis = pivot columns of original $A$

### Example: Finding a Basis for $\mathcal{C}(A)$

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \ 2 & 4 & 7 \ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reduce:

$$\xrightarrow{R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Pivots in columns 1 and 3. Basis for $\mathcal{C}(A)$:

$$\left{ \begin{bmatrix}1\2\0\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}3\7\1\end{bmatrix} \right}$$

(taken from original $A$, columns 1 and 3). Rank = 2.

## The Four Fundamental Subspaces

Column space is one of four subspaces attached to every matrix. They come in orthogonal pairs.

|Subspace|Notation|Lives in|Dimension|
|---|---|---|---|
|Column space|$\mathcal{C}(A)$|$\mathbb{R}^m$|$r$|
|Null space|$\mathcal{N}(A)$|$\mathbb{R}^n$|$n - r$|
|Row space|$\mathcal{C}(A^T)$|$\mathbb{R}^n$|$r$|
|Left null space|$\mathcal{N}(A^T)$|$\mathbb{R}^m$|$m - r$|

where $r = \text{rank}(A)$.

> [!theorem] Rank-Nullity Theorem $$\dim(\mathcal{C}(A)) + \dim(\mathcal{N}(A)) = n$$ $$r + (n - r) = n$$
> 
> Every vector entering $A$ (in $\mathbb{R}^n$) either contributes to the output (hits $\mathcal{C}(A)$) or gets annihilated (lands in $\mathcal{N}(A)$). The two pieces partition the input dimension exactly.

> [!theorem] Orthogonality of Fundamental Subspaces $$\mathcal{C}(A) \perp \mathcal{N}(A^T) \quad \text{in } \mathbb{R}^m$$ $$\mathcal{C}(A^T) \perp \mathcal{N}(A) \quad \text{in } \mathbb{R}^n$$
> 
> The column space and the left null space are **orthogonal complements** in $\mathbb{R}^m$. Every vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$ decomposes uniquely as $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}_{\mathcal{C}} + \mathbf{b}_{\perp}$ with $\mathbf{b}_{\mathcal{C}} \in \mathcal{C}(A)$ and $\mathbf{b}_{\perp} \in \mathcal{N}(A^T)$.

```
In ℝ^m:                              In ℝ^n:

  ┌────────────────────────┐           ┌────────────────────────┐
  │   C(A)        N(A^T)   │           │   C(A^T)      N(A)     │
  │   dim r    ⊕  dim m-r  │           │   dim r    ⊕  dim n-r  │
  │   ───────────────────  │           │   ───────────────────  │
  │       orthogonal       │           │       orthogonal       │
  └────────────────────────┘           └────────────────────────┘
         output space ℝ^m                    input space ℝ^n
```

## Column Space vs Row Space

> [!note] Row Operations Preserve Row Space, Not Column Space Row reduction preserves the **row space** $\mathcal{C}(A^T)$ but **changes** the column space. This is why you cannot read off a basis for $\mathcal{C}(A)$ from the RREF columns directly.

However, $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}(A^T)$: the dimension of the column space always equals the dimension of the row space. This is the **rank theorem** — the most non-obvious basic fact in linear algebra.

**Why?** Row reduction shows that the number of pivot rows = number of pivot columns. The pivot rows form a basis for $\mathcal{C}(A^T)$ and the pivot column positions mark the basis for $\mathcal{C}(A)$. Both counts give the same $r$.

## Projection onto the Column Space

When $\mathbf{b} \notin \mathcal{C}(A)$, the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no solution. The best we can do is find the $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ minimising $|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}|^2$ — i.e., project $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$.

> [!theorem] Projection Formula The projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$ is: $$\hat{\mathbf{b}} = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b} = P\mathbf{b}$$
> 
> where $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is the **projection matrix** onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$.
> 
> The least-squares solution satisfies the **normal equations**: $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$.

> [!warning] $A^T A$ Invertible ↔ Columns of $A$ Are Linearly Independent If columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, $A^T A$ is singular and the formula breaks. Use the pseudoinverse $A^+$ instead. $$\hat{\mathbf{b}} = A A^+ \mathbf{b}$$ where $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ from the SVD. See [[SVD and Low-Rank Approximation]].

### Projection Matrix Properties

The matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ satisfies:

- $P^2 = P$ — applying the projection twice does nothing (idempotent)
- $P^T = P$ — symmetric
- $\mathcal{C}(P) = \mathcal{C}(A)$ — same column space
- $I - P$ projects onto $\mathcal{N}(A^T)$, the orthogonal complement

```
Projection of b onto C(A):

         b
         |\ ← error vector e = b - Pb ∈ N(A^T), perpendicular to C(A)
         | \
         |  \
   ──────+───● Pb = b_hat ∈ C(A)  ← closest point in C(A) to b
         |
       C(A) (the flat subspace / plane)
```

## Column Space in Full-Rank Cases

> [!tip] Decision Rule: Full Column Rank vs Full Row Rank
> 
> |Case|Condition|$\mathcal{C}(A)$|Solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$|
> |---|---|---|---|
> |Full column rank|$r = n \leq m$|$n$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$|At most 1 (0 or 1)|
> |Full row rank|$r = m \leq n$|All of $\mathbb{R}^m$|Always at least 1 (possibly many)|
> |Full rank (square)|$r = m = n$|All of $\mathbb{R}^m$|Exactly 1 — $A$ invertible|
> |Rank-deficient|$r < \min(m,n)$|Strict subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$|0 or infinitely many|
> 
> **Mnemonic:** Full column rank → $\mathcal{N}(A) = {\mathbf{0}}$ → at most one solution. Full row rank → $\mathcal{C}(A) = \mathbb{R}^m$ → always solvable.

## ML Connection: Where Column Space Appears

### 1. Least Squares Regression

Linear regression finds $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{y}$. The fitted values $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = P\mathbf{y}$ are the **projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto $\mathcal{C}(X)$**, the column space of the design matrix. The residual $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$ — this is why the normal equations hold. See [[Least Squares and Normal Equations]].

### 2. PCA and SVD: Finding the "Best" Column Space

PCA finds the $k$-dimensional subspace (i.e., the column space of rank-$k$ approximation $A_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^T$) that captures maximum variance. The columns of $U_k$ form an **orthonormal basis for the best rank-$k$ column space** of $A$. This is the Eckart-Young theorem. Internally, `sklearn.decomposition.PCA` calls `numpy.linalg.svd` and returns exactly $U_k$.

### 3. Rank Deficiency in Neural Network Weight Matrices

In overparameterised networks, weight matrices often become **low-rank** during training — the effective column space shrinks. Intrinsic dimensionality research (Aghajanyan et al., 2021) shows that fine-tuning large language models happens in a very low-dimensional subspace of the full parameter column space. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) exploits this: instead of updating $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, it updates $W + BA$ where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$, $r \ll \min(m,n)$ — explicitly constraining the update to live in an $r$-dimensional column space.

### 4. Attention Mechanism

In the transformer attention layer, $\text{softmax}(QK^T/\sqrt{d_k})V$ projects queries into the column space of $V$ (the value matrix), weighted by attention scores. The column space of $V$ is the subspace of information that can be retrieved. Low-rank attention (Linformer, Performer) explicitly constrains $\mathcal{C}(K)$ and $\mathcal{C}(V)$ to have small dimension.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|Lives in|$\mathbb{R}^m$|$\mathbb{R}^n$|$\mathbb{R}^n$|$\mathbb{R}^m$|
|Dimension|$r$|$n-r$|$r$|$m-r$|
|Found by|Pivot cols of $A$|Solving $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$|Pivot rows of $A$|Solving $A^T\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0}$|
|Orthogonal to|$\mathcal{N}(A^T)$|$\mathcal{C}(A^T)$|$\mathcal{N}(A)$|$\mathcal{C}(A)$|
|Preserved by row ops?|**No**|Yes|Yes|**No**|
|ML role|Feature subspace, image of layer|Dead directions, redundant params|Row features|Residuals, error directions|

## Worked Example: Full Four-Subspace Analysis

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \ 1 & 2 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 1: Row reduce.**

$$\xrightarrow{R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Pivots in columns 1 and 3. Free variables: $x_2, x_4$. Rank $r = 2$.

**Step 2: $\mathcal{C}(A)$** — basis from pivot columns of original $A$: $$\mathcal{C}(A) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\0\1\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}0\1\1\end{bmatrix}\right}$$

**Step 3: $\mathcal{N}(A)$** — set $x_2 = s, x_4 = t$, back-substitute:

$x_1 = -2s - t,\quad x_3 = -3t$

$$\mathcal{N}(A) = s\begin{bmatrix}-2\1\0\0\end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix}-1\0\-3\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Check: $r + (n-r) = 2 + 2 = 4 = n$ ✓

**Step 4: $\mathcal{C}(A^T)$** — pivot rows of RREF: $$\mathcal{C}(A^T) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\2\0\1\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}0\0\1\3\end{bmatrix}\right}$$

**Step 5: $\mathcal{N}(A^T)$** — solve $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$:

From row 3 of RREF: row 3 of $A$ = row 1 + row 2, so $y_1 + y_2 = 0$ in the nullspace. One free variable: $y_3 = s, y_1 = 1, y_2 = 1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{y} = (1, 1, -1)^T$ (up to sign).

$$\mathcal{N}(A^T) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\1\-1\end{bmatrix}\right}$$

Check: $r + (m-r) = 2 + 1 = 3 = m$ ✓
