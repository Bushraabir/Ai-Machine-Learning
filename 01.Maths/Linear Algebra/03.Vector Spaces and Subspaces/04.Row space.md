
## What Is the Row Space?

You already know the **column space** $C(A)$: all linear combinations of the columns of $A$. The row space is the same idea, applied to the rows.

For a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$:

$$C(A^T) = \text{span}{\text{rows of } A} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$$

We write it as $C(A^T)$ because the rows of $A$ are the columns of $A^T$. So the row space of $A$ **is** the column space of $A^T$.

> [!note] Why $C(A^T)$ and not just "row space"? Using $C(A^T)$ ties it into the **four fundamental subspaces** framework and makes the orthogonality relationships easy to state. Everything about column spaces applies to row spaces — just transpose.

---

## Where Does It Live?

This is the first thing to get straight about all four subspaces — they live in different spaces.

|Subspace|Lives in|Dimension|
|---|---|---|
|Column space $C(A)$|$\mathbb{R}^m$ — the output space|$r$|
|Null space $N(A)$|$\mathbb{R}^n$ — the input space|$n - r$|
|**Row space $C(A^T)$**|**$\mathbb{R}^n$ — the input space**|$r$|
|Left null space $N(A^T)$|$\mathbb{R}^m$ — the output space|$m - r$|

> [!warning] Row space and null space share the same ambient space Both $C(A^T)$ and $N(A)$ live in $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is not a coincidence — they are **orthogonal complements** of each other in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ splits uniquely into a row-space component and a null-space component.

---

## How to Find the Row Space

The most important fact about row reduction:

> [!theorem] Row Reduction Preserves the Row Space Elementary row operations do **not** change the row space.
> 
> $$C(A^T) = C(R^T) \quad \text{where } R \text{ is any row echelon form of } A$$
> 
> Why? Each row operation replaces a row with a linear combination of rows — the span doesn't change.

So to find a clean basis for the row space:

1. Row reduce $A$ to echelon form $R$
2. Take the **nonzero rows of $R$**
3. These are a basis for $C(A^T)$

> [!warning] Do NOT do this for the column space Row operations **do** change the column space. For $C(A)$, you must use the pivot columns of the **original** $A$, not the reduced form. This asymmetry trips up almost everyone at first.
> 
> ||Use original $A$|Use reduced $R$|
> |---|---|---|
> |Column space basis|✓ (pivot columns of $A$)|✗|
> |Row space basis|✗|✓ (nonzero rows of $R$)|

---

## A Worked Example

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \ 2 & 4 & 6 \ 1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 1: Row reduce.**

$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$, then $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 0 \ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Swap $R_2$ and $R_3$:

$$R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \ 0 & 1 & 1 \ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 2: Read off the nonzero rows.** Rank $r = 2$.

$$C(A^T) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\2\3\end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix}0\1\1\end{bmatrix}\right} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3 \quad \dim = 2$$

**Step 3: Find the null space** (to verify orthogonality later).

Free variable: $x_3 = t$. Back-substitute: $x_2 = -t$, $x_1 = -t$.

$$N(A) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}-1\-1\1\end{bmatrix}\right} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3 \quad \dim = 1$$

**Dimension check:** $r + (n - r) = 2 + 1 = 3 = n$ ✓

**Orthogonality check:** $$\begin{bmatrix}1\2\3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1\-1\1\end{bmatrix} = -1 - 2 + 3 = 0 \checkmark \qquad \begin{bmatrix}0\1\1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1\-1\1\end{bmatrix} = 0 - 1 + 1 = 0 \checkmark$$

Every row-space vector is perpendicular to every null-space vector.

## Row Space vs Column Space: Are They Equal?

For a general matrix, $C(A) \neq C(A^T)$ — they don't even live in the same space (one is in $\mathbb{R}^m$, the other in $\mathbb{R}^n$).

But their **dimensions are always equal**: $$\dim C(A) = \dim C(A^T) = r$$

This is the content of the statement "row rank equals column rank" — a non-obvious fact that falls out of the rank theorem.

> [!example] Row Rank = Column Rank (Why It's Surprising) Take a $3 \times 5$ matrix of rank 2. It has 3 rows and 5 columns — completely different objects in completely different spaces. Yet both the row space and column space have dimension exactly 2. There's no obvious geometric reason this _has_ to be true. It's a deep structural fact about linear maps.

For **square matrices**, $C(A)$ and $C(A^T)$ both live in $\mathbb{R}^n$. They're not usually the same subspace, but they have the same dimension. Example:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

- $C(A) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\0\end{bmatrix}\right}$ — the $x$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$
- $C(A^T) = \text{span}\left{\begin{bmatrix}1\2\end{bmatrix}\right}$ — a different line through the origin

Same dimension, different subspace.

## The Big Picture: Orthogonal Complements

This is the heart of why the row space matters.

> [!theorem] Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra — Orthogonality In $\mathbb{R}^n$: $$C(A^T) \perp N(A) \quad \text{and} \quad C(A^T) \oplus N(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$$
> 
> In $\mathbb{R}^m$: $$C(A) \perp N(A^T) \quad \text{and} \quad C(A) \oplus N(A^T) = \mathbb{R}^m$$

Every vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ decomposes **uniquely** as:

$$\mathbf{x} = \underbrace{\mathbf{x}_r}_{\in, C(A^T)} + \underbrace{\mathbf{x}_n}_{\in, N(A)}$$

> [!note] Why This Is Powerful When you solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$:
> 
> - The null space component $\mathbf{x}_n$ contributes **nothing** to $A\mathbf{x}$ (it gets killed)
> - Only the row space component $\mathbf{x}_r$ produces output
> 
> The "effective" part of any solution always lives in $C(A^T)$. The "freedom" in choosing solutions lives in $N(A)$. This decomposition is the geometric foundation of the SVD.

---

## Proof: Row Space is Orthogonal to Null Space

> [!theorem] $C(A^T) \perp N(A)$ If $\mathbf{x} \in N(A)$, then $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, meaning every **row** of $A$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{x}$.
> 
> Since $C(A^T)$ is spanned by the rows of $A$, and every row is perpendicular to $\mathbf{x}$, every vector in $C(A^T)$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{x}$.
> 
> **Formally:** let $\mathbf{r} \in C(A^T)$, so $\mathbf{r} = A^T \mathbf{y}$ for some $\mathbf{y}$. Then: $$\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{x} = (A^T\mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{0} = 0 \quad \square$$

The proof is almost one line. The key move: $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is exactly the condition that kills the dot product.

## What the Row Space Tells You About Solutions

### Existence

$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution $\iff$ $\mathbf{b} \in C(A)$.

### Uniqueness

The solution is unique $\iff$ $N(A) = {\mathbf{0}}$ $\iff$ $\dim C(A^T) = n$ (row space fills all of $\mathbb{R}^n$).

### The Particular Solution Lives in the Row Space

Among all solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, there is exactly one that lies in $C(A^T)$: the **minimum-norm solution** $\mathbf{x}^+ = A^T(AA^T)^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ (when $A$ has full row rank). This is the pseudoinverse solution — it picks the "smallest" $\mathbf{x}$ by ignoring the null space entirely.

> [!note] Connection to Least Squares and ML In overdetermined systems ($m > n$, more equations than unknowns), we minimize $|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}|^2$. The normal equations $A^T A \mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ project $\mathbf{b}$ onto $C(A)$ and find the solution in $C(A^T)$. The row space is where the "signal" lives; the null space is where underdetermined freedom lives. This geometry is at the core of linear regression.

---

## Rank-Nullity Revisited

The four subspace dimensions always satisfy:

$$\underbrace{r}_{\dim C(A^T)} + \underbrace{(n-r)}_{\dim N(A)} = n \qquad \text{(partition of } \mathbb{R}^n\text{)}$$

$$\underbrace{r}_{\dim C(A)} + \underbrace{(m-r)}_{\dim N(A^T)} = m \qquad \text{(partition of } \mathbb{R}^m\text{)}$$

These are not two separate theorems — they are the same rank-nullity theorem applied once to $A$ and once to $A^T$.

## Quick Reference: Row Space Checklist

### Finding the row space basis

1. Row reduce $A$ → echelon form $R$
2. Take the **nonzero rows of $R$** as the basis
3. $\dim C(A^T) = $ number of pivots = rank $r$

### Checking if a vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is in $C(A^T)$

- $\mathbf{v} \in C(A^T)$ $\iff$ $\mathbf{v} \perp N(A)$ $\iff$ $\mathbf{v}$ is perpendicular to every null space vector

### Checking if $\mathbf{v} \in N(A)$

- Compute $A\mathbf{v}$. If $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{v} \in N(A)$, which means $\mathbf{v} \perp C(A^T)$.
