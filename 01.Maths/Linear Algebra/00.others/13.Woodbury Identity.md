Suppose we already know $A^{-1}$.
Now we modify the matrix by adding a **low-rank update**: $(A+UCV)$
where $U$ and $V$ are tall/thin matrices and $C$ is a small matrix.
Instead of recomputing the inverse from scratch, the Woodbury Identity lets us update the old inverse:

>[!formula] $(A+UCV)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}U(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U)^{-1}VA^{-1}$

### What is a low-rank update?
In Sherman-Morrison, we added a rank-1 matrix: $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$.

Woodbury generalizes this idea to rank-$k$ updates: $UCV$.
Think of it as adding a few new directions instead of just one.

### Matrix Picture:
Suppose
$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$,
$U=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$,
$C=\begin{pmatrix}1\end{pmatrix}$,
$V=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\end{pmatrix}$.

Then
$UCV=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\6&8\end{pmatrix}$.
Notice that this is exactly the rank-1 update from Sherman-Morrison.
Therefore, [[11.Sherman-Morrison --Updating an Inverse After a Small Change|Sherman-Morrison]] is just the special case of Woodbury when the update has rank 1.

---

### Geometric Meaning
The matrix $A$ represents a transformation that we already understand.
The update $UCV$ adds only a few new directions to that transformation.
Since only a small part of the matrix changes, it would be wasteful to recompute the entire inverse.
Woodbury tells us how to correct the old inverse using information from those few new directions.


### Mathematical Intuition

Instead of inverting the large matrix $(A+UCV)$ directly, Woodbury moves most of the work into the much smaller matrix $(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U)$.
If $A$ is $10000\times10000$ but the update has rank $5$, then Woodbury replaces one huge inversion with a small $5\times5$ inversion.

---
### Relationship to Sherman-Morrison

Sherman-Morrison:
$(A+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}-\dfrac{A^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^TA^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{v}^TA^{-1}\mathbf{u}}$

Woodbury:
$(A+UCV)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}U(C^{-1}+VA^{-1}U)^{-1}VA^{-1}$

Woodbury is the more general formula. Sherman-Morrison is the rank-1 case.

---

### Why is it useful?

Recomputing an inverse costs: $O(n^3)$
Woodbury reduces the expensive computation to inverting a small $k\times k$ matrix.

When $k\ll n$, the savings are enormous.

> Woodbury says that if a matrix changes by only a low-rank amount, update the inverse using a small matrix inversion instead of recomputing the whole inverse.
