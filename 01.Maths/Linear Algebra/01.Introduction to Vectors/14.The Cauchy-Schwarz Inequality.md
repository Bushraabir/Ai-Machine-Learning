For any two vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|$$

Equality holds **if and only if** $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ point in the same (or opposite) direction — i.e., one is a scalar multiple of the other.

## What It's Saying Geometrically
The dot product satisfies $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta$.
So the inequality is just saying: $|\cos\theta| \leq 1$
That's it. The cosine of any angle is always between −1 and 1. Cauchy–Schwarz is a precise algebraic statement of that obvious geometric fact.

## Three Proofs (Each Gives a Different Insight)
- ### Proof 1: Discriminant Argument
	Define $f(t) = |\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v}|^2$. This is a squared distance, so $f(t) \geq 0$ for all $t$.
	Expanding: $f(t) = |\mathbf{v}|^2 t^2 - 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})t + |\mathbf{u}|^2$
	This is a quadratic in $t$ that never goes negative. That means its discriminant must be $\leq 0$:
	 $[2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})]^2 - 4|\mathbf{v}|^2|\mathbf{u}|^2 \leq 0$
     $\implies (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 \leq |\mathbf{u}|^2|\mathbf{v}|^2 \qquad \blacksquare$

  **Why equality holds:** The discriminant equals zero only when $f(t)$ touches zero — meaning $|\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v}|^2 = 0$, so $\mathbf{u} = tv$. The vectors are proportional.
- ### Proof 2 — Projection Argument (Most Intuitive)
	Project $\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$:
	             $\mathbf{u} = \underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}\mathbf{v}}_{\text{shadow on }\mathbf{v}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{e}}_{\perp,\mathbf{v}}$
	By the Pythagorean theorem:
	     $|\mathbf{u}|^2 = \left|\text{proj}_\mathbf{v}\mathbf{u}\right|^2 + |\mathbf{e}|^2 \geq \left|\text{proj}_\mathbf{v}\mathbf{u}\right|^2 = \frac{(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}$
	Multiply both sides by $|\mathbf{v}|^2$: $|\mathbf{u}|^2|\mathbf{v}|^2 \geq (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 \qquad \blacksquare$
	**Intuition:** The shadow of $\mathbf{u}$ on $\mathbf{v}$ can never be longer than $\mathbf{u}$ itself. A component cannot exceed the whole.
- ### Proof 3 — Lagrange's Identity (Algebraic)
	This one gives an _exact identity_, not just an inequality:
	    $|\mathbf{u}|^2|\mathbf{v}|^2 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 = \sum_{i < j}(u_i v_j - u_j v_i)^2$
	The right side is a sum of squares, so it's always $\geq 0$. Cauchy–Schwarz follows immediately.
	**In $\mathbb{R}^2$**, there's only one term, and it reduces to: $|\mathbf{u}|^2|\mathbf{v}|^2 = (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 + (u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)^2$
	which is exactly $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, Cauchy–Schwarz _is_ the Pythagorean theorem.

## The Same Inequality in Other Settings

The proof idea (a nonnegative quadratic has discriminant $\leq 0$) works word-for-word in all of these settings:

**Sequences** — For real numbers $a_i, b_i$:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i\right)^2 \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right)$$

**Functions** — For $f, g$ on $[a, b]$:

$$\left(\int_a^b f(x)g(x),dx\right)^2 \leq \left(\int_a^b f(x)^2,dx\right)\left(\int_a^b g(x)^2,dx\right)$$

**Random Variables** — For $X, Y$ with finite second moments:

$$|\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)| \leq \operatorname{SD}(X)\cdot\operatorname{SD}(Y)$$

This last one tells you why correlation $\rho$ is always between $-1$ and $1$ — that's just Cauchy–Schwarz.

## Cauchy–Schwarz Gives You the Triangle Inequality for Free
$|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}|^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2 + 2,$
$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + |\mathbf{v}|^2 \leq |\mathbf{u}|^2 + 2|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}| + |\mathbf{v}|^2$
$= (|\mathbf{u}| + |\mathbf{v}|)^2$

Take square roots: $|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}| + |\mathbf{v}|$.

The logical chain: **positive definiteness → Cauchy–Schwarz → triangle inequality**.

## When Does Equality Hold?

In every version of the inequality, equality means the two objects are _proportional_:

|Setting|Equality condition|
|---|---|
|Vectors|$\mathbf{u} = c,\mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $c$|
|Sequences|$a_i = c,b_i$ for all $i$|
|Functions|$f = c,g$ almost everywhere|
|Random variables|$X = cY$ almost surely|

Note: $c$ can be negative. Antiparallel vectors ($\theta = 180°$) also achieve equality.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the absolute value.** The left side is $|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}|$, not $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$.
- **Thinking equality requires $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}$.** It only requires proportionality — the vectors can have completely different lengths.
- **Thinking Cauchy–Schwarz is universal.** It depends on _positive definiteness_. In Minkowski spacetime (signature $(-,+,+,+)$), the inequality can reverse for timelike vectors.
