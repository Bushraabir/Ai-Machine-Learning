Gaussian elimination on a symmetric matrix $A$ produces the factorization $A = LU$. But if $A$ is symmetric, throwing away that structure is wasteful — $U$ is not just any upper triangular matrix, it is $D L^T$ where $D$ is diagonal. This gives the **LDL^T factorization**:
$$A = L D L^T$$
where:
- $L$ is **unit lower triangular** (1s on the diagonal, entries below),
- $D$ is **diagonal** (the pivots),
- $L^T$ is the corresponding unit upper triangular factor.
This is the symmetric version of LU — it exploits $A = A^T$ to halve the work and storage.
> [!important] **LDL^T requires half the operations of LU** and is numerically stable for symmetric positive definite matrices. It stores only $L$ and $D$ instead of both $L$ and $U$.
### Derivation from LU

Start with $A = LU$ where $L$ is unit lower triangular and $U$ is upper triangular. Factor the pivots out of $U$:
$$U = D \hat{U}$$
where $D = \mathrm{diag}(u_{11}, u_{22}, \ldots, u_{nn})$ and $\hat{U}$ is unit upper triangular. So:
$$A = L D \hat{U}$$
Now use symmetry. Since $A = A^T$:
$$L D \hat{U} = (L D \hat{U})^T = \hat{U}^T D^T L^T = \hat{U}^T D L^T$$
Multiply on the left by $L^{-1}$ and on the right by $(L^T)^{-1}$:
$$D \hat{U} (L^T)^{-1} = L^{-1} \hat{U}^T D$$
One can show (by uniqueness of LU for symmetric positive definite matrices) that $\hat{U} = L^T$. Therefore:
$$\boxed{A = L D L^T}$$

### Algorithm

Given symmetric $A$, compute $L$ and $D$ column by column. For $j = 1, 2, \ldots, n$:

1. Compute the $j$-th diagonal of $D$:

$$d_j = a_{jj} - \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} l_{jk}^2 , d_k$$

2. Compute the entries of $L$ below the diagonal in column $j$:

$$l_{ij} = \frac{1}{d_j}\left(a_{ij} - \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} l_{ik} , l_{jk} , d_k\right), \quad i = j+1, \ldots, n$$

**Complexity:** $\approx \frac{n^3}{6}$ multiplications — exactly half the cost of standard LU ($\approx \frac{n^3}{3}$).

### Example
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ -1 & 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$
**Step 1: $j = 1$**
$$d_1 = a_{11} = 4$$
$$l_{21} = \frac{a_{21}}{d_1} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad l_{31} = \frac{a_{31}}{d_1} = \frac{-1}{4}$$
**Step 2: $j = 2$**
$$d_2 = a_{22} - l_{21}^2 d_1 = 5 - \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \cdot 4 = 5 - 1 = 4$$
$$l_{32} = \frac{1}{d_2}\left(a_{32} - l_{31} l_{21} d_1\right) = \frac{1}{4}\left(3 - \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)(4)\right) = \frac{1}{4}\left(3 + \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{7}{8}$$

**Step 3: $j = 3$**
$$d_3 = a_{33} - l_{31}^2 d_1 - l_{32}^2 d_2 = 6 - \frac{1}{16}(4) - \frac{49}{64}(4) = 6 - \frac{1}{4} - \frac{49}{16} = 6 - \frac{4}{16} - \frac{49}{16} = 6 - \frac{53}{16} = \frac{43}{16}$$
**Result:**

$$L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{4} & \frac{7}{8} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{43}{16} \end{bmatrix}$$

Verify: $A = L D L^T$. ✓

### Connection to Positive Definiteness

> [!theorem] A symmetric matrix $A$ has an LDL^T factorization with all positive diagonal entries $d_j > 0$ **if and only if** $A$ is **positive definite**.

This is a direct test: run the LDL^T algorithm and check the signs of the pivots $d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n$.

- All $d_j > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is **positive definite**.
- Some $d_j < 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is **indefinite**.
- Some $d_j = 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $A$ is **singular** (algorithm breaks down).

### LDL^T vs Cholesky

For **positive definite** symmetric matrices, there is an even cleaner factorization: the **Cholesky decomposition** $A = R^T R$ where $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries. The relationship is:

$$A = L D L^T = (L D^{1/2})(D^{1/2} L^T) = R^T R$$

where $R = D^{1/2} L^T$ and $D^{1/2} = \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{d_1}, \ldots, \sqrt{d_n})$.

| -                | LDL^T              | Cholesky                       |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| **Requires**     | Symmetric          | Symmetric positive definite    |
| **Form**         | $LDL^T$            | $R^T R$                        |
| **Pivots**       | Explicit in $D$    | Implicit (square roots of $D$) |
| **Square roots** | Not needed         | Needed                         |
| **Stability**    | Handles indefinite | Only positive definite         |

> [!note] Prefer **LDL^T** when you are not sure $A$ is positive definite (e.g., in optimization where the Hessian can be indefinite). Prefer **Cholesky** when you know $A$ is positive definite and want slightly simpler code.

### Why LDL^T Appears Everywhere

- **Symmetric linear systems** $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: solve $L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$, then $D\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y}$, then $L^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{z}$. Each step is a triangular solve: $O(n^2)$.
- **Normal equations** in least squares: $A^T A \mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. Since $A^T A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, LDL^T (or Cholesky) is the go-to factorization.
- **Saddle-point systems** in constrained optimization and FEM: the system matrix is symmetric but indefinite, making LDL^T (not Cholesky) the correct choice.
- **Kalman filtering, Gaussian processes, Bayesian linear regression**: all require solving with symmetric covariance matrices repeatedly — LDL^T makes this efficient.
