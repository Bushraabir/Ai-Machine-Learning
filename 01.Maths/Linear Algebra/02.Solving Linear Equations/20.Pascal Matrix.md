> [!note] Why the Pascal Matrix?
> 
> Pascal's Triangle is a sequence. The **Pascal matrix** is its linear-algebraic incarnation — and it is extraordinary:
> 
> - It is the **change-of-basis matrix** between the monomial and binomial bases of polynomials
> - Its inverse is **itself with alternating signs** — no computation required
> - It factors cleanly into **lower × diagonal × upper** triangular form
> - Its eigenvalues, determinant, and powers all have closed forms
> - It appears in **finite differences**, **probability generating functions**, **digital signal processing**, and **combinatorial proofs by matrix identity**
> 
> The one insight: **the Pascal matrix is not just a notational convenience — it linearizes the combinatorial structure of Pascal's Triangle into a matrix that has remarkable algebraic properties.**

## Definition: Three Variants

Let $n \geq 1$. The **$n \times n$ Pascal matrices** are defined by their $(i,j)$ entries (1-indexed):

### Lower Triangular Pascal Matrix $L_n$

$$[L_n]_{ij} = \begin{cases} \binom{i-1}{j-1} & \text{if } i \geq j \ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

### Upper Triangular Pascal Matrix $U_n$

$$[U_n]_{ij} = \begin{cases} \binom{j-1}{i-1} & \text{if } j \geq i \ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

> [!note] Relationship Between $L_n$ and $U_n$
> 
> $U_n = L_n^T$. The upper Pascal matrix is simply the transpose of the lower. This is immediate from $\binom{i-1}{j-1} = \binom{j-1}{i-1}$ after transposition.

### Symmetric Pascal Matrix $S_n$

$$[S_n]_{ij} = \binom{i+j-2}{i-1}$$

This is the **outer product** structure: $S_n = L_n L_n^T = L_n U_n$.

## Explicit $4 \times 4$ Examples

$$L_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \ 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \ 1 & 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$S_4 = L_4 U_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \ 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \ 1 & 3 & 6 & 10 \ 1 & 4 & 10 & 20 \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!example] Verify $S_4 = L_4 U_4$
> 
> Entry $(3,3)$: row 3 of $L_4$ is $[1,\ 2,\ 1,\ 0]$, column 3 of $U_4$ is $[1,\ 2,\ 1,\ 0]^T$. Dot product: $1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 + 0 = 6 = \binom{4}{2}$ ✓
> 
> This is Vandermonde's identity in disguise: $[S_n]_{ij} = \sum_k \binom{i-1}{k-1}\binom{j-1}{k-1} = \binom{i+j-2}{i-1}$

## The Inverse: Alternating Signs

> [!theorem] Inverse of the Pascal Matrix
> 
> The inverse of $L_n$ is obtained by **negating the off-diagonal entries**:
> 
> $$[L_n^{-1}]_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \binom{i-1}{j-1} \quad (i \geq j)$$
> 
> Similarly, $[U_n^{-1}]_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}\binom{j-1}{i-1}$ for $j \geq i$.
> 
> And: $S_n^{-1} = U_n^{-1} L_n^{-1}$, with entries $[S_n^{-1}]_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}[S_n]_{ij}$.

> [!example] Verify for $3 \times 3$
> 
> $$L_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \ 1 & 1 & 0 \ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad L_3^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \ -1 & 1 & 0 \ 1 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Check $L_3 L_3^{-1}$: row 3 of $L_3$ times column 1 of $L_3^{-1}$: $1(1) + 2(-1) + 1(1) = 0$ ✓ Row 3 times column 2: $1(0) + 2(1) + 1(-2) = 0$ ✓ Row 3 times column 3: $1(0) + 2(0) + 1(1) = 1$ ✓

**Why this works — combinatorial proof:**

The $(i,j)$ entry of $L_n L_n^{-1}$ is: $$\sum_{k=j}^{i} \binom{i-1}{k-1}(-1)^{k+j}\binom{k-1}{j-1} = \delta_{ij}$$

This is the **upper summation identity with alternating signs**, a classical combinatorial identity.

## Determinant

> [!theorem] $\det(L_n) = \det(U_n) = \det(S_n) = 1$
> 
> **Proof for $L_n$:** $L_n$ is lower triangular with all diagonal entries $\binom{i-1}{i-1} = 1$. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries: $\det(L_n) = 1^n = 1$.
> 
> **For $S_n$:** $\det(S_n) = \det(L_n)\det(U_n) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$.

This is remarkable: despite entries growing exponentially, the determinant stays 1. The Pascal matrix is **unimodular**.

## LDL^T Factorization of $S_n$

Since $S_n$ is symmetric positive definite (shown below), it has an $LDL^T$ decomposition. But we already have it:

$$S_n = L_n \cdot I \cdot L_n^T$$

The $LDL^T$ factorization of the symmetric Pascal matrix is **exact**, with $L = L_n$ (the lower Pascal matrix) and $D = I$ (the identity). All pivots are 1.

> [!note] Connection to Cholesky
> 
> Since $D = I$, we have $D^{1/2} = I$, and the Cholesky factor is: $$\tilde{L} = L_n \cdot I = L_n$$
> 
> The **Cholesky factorization of $S_n$ is $S_n = L_n L_n^T$** — the lower Pascal matrix is its own Cholesky factor. This is one of the cleanest Cholesky factorizations in all of linear algebra.

## Positive Definiteness of $S_n$

> [!theorem] $S_n$ is Symmetric Positive Definite
> 
> Since $S_n = L_n L_n^T$ and $L_n$ is invertible ($\det L_n = 1 \neq 0$), the matrix $S_n$ is SPD.
> 
> **Proof:** For any nonzero $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$: $$\mathbf{x}^T S_n \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^T L_n L_n^T \mathbf{x} = |L_n^T \mathbf{x}|^2 > 0$$ The last inequality holds because $L_n^T$ is invertible, so $L_n^T \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$ for $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

All $n$ pivots equal 1, so all eigenvalues are positive. However, eigenvalues grow rapidly with $n$, making $S_n$ **increasingly ill-conditioned** despite being SPD.

## Powers of the Pascal Matrix

> [!theorem] Powers of $L_n$
> 
> $$[L_n^m]_{ij} = \binom{m(i-j) + (i-j)}{i-j} \cdot \ldots$$
> 
> For the **simplest case**: scalar shifts via the Pascal matrix. Define $L_n(t)$ with entries $[L_n(t)]_{ij} = \binom{i-1}{j-1} t^{i-j}$ for $i \geq j$. Then: $$L_n(s) \cdot L_n(t) = L_n(s+t)$$
> 
> The Pascal matrix family forms a **one-parameter group** under multiplication: $L_n(t)^{-1} = L_n(-t)$.

> [!example] Integer Powers
> 
> $$L_3^2 = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\1&1&0\1&2&1\end{bmatrix}^2 = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\2&1&0\4&4&1\end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> Entries are $[L_3^2]_{ij} = \binom{i-1}{j-1} 2^{i-j}$ for $i \geq j$ — a shifted binomial coefficient scaled by powers of 2.

## Connection to Finite Differences

The Pascal matrix is the **matrix of the forward difference operator** $\Delta$.

Define the forward difference: $\Delta f(x) = f(x+1) - f(x)$. For a polynomial vector $\mathbf{p} = [p(0),\ p(1),\ \ldots,\ p(n-1)]^T$:

$$\Delta \mathbf{p} = (L_n^{-1} - I)\mathbf{p}$$

More precisely, $U_n$ transforms the **Newton forward difference basis** ${1,\ x,\ x(x-1),\ x(x-1)(x-2),\ \ldots}$ into the **monomial basis** ${1,\ x,\ x^2,\ \ldots}$.

> [!note] Why This Matters for Interpolation
> 
> Newton's forward difference interpolation formula: $$p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \Delta^k f(0) \binom{x}{k}$$
> 
> The coefficients $\Delta^k f(0)$ are exactly the entries of $L_n^{-1} \mathbf{f}$, where $\mathbf{f} = [f(0), f(1), \ldots, f(n)]^T$. The Pascal matrix mediates the change between the two polynomial bases.

## Eigenvalues and Spectral Properties

The eigenvalues of $S_n$ do **not** have a simple closed form, but several structural facts hold:

|Property|$L_n$|$U_n$|$S_n$|
|---|---|---|---|
|All eigenvalues|$1$ (algebraic mult. $n$)|$1$ (algebraic mult. $n$)|Positive, grow with $n$|
|Characteristic polynomial|$(1-\lambda)^n$|$(1-\lambda)^n$|No closed form for $n \geq 4$|
|Diagonalizable?|No (for $n \geq 2$)|No (for $n \geq 2$)|Yes (symmetric)|
|Condition number $\kappa_2$|1 (trivially)|1 (trivially)|Grows exponentially in $n$|

> [!warning] Ill-Conditioning of $S_n$
> 
> Despite $\det(S_n) = 1$ and all positive eigenvalues, the **condition number** $\kappa_2(S_n) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ grows exponentially with $n$. For $n = 20$: $\kappa_2(S_{20}) \approx 10^{14}$ — near the limit of double-precision arithmetic. This is the same ill-conditioning pathology as the **Hilbert matrix**. Do not invert $S_n$ numerically for large $n$.

## Comparison Table — Pascal Matrix Variants

|Property|$L_n$ (Lower)|$U_n$ (Upper)|$S_n$ (Symmetric)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Definition|$\binom{i-1}{j-1},\ i \geq j$|$\binom{j-1}{i-1},\ j \geq i$|$\binom{i+j-2}{i-1}$|

|Inverse|Alternate signs|Alternate signs|$(-1)^{i+j} [S_n]_{ij}$|
|Determinant|$1$|$1$|$1$|
|Eigenvalues|All $1$|All $1$|Positive, no closed form|
|Diagonalizable?|No|No|Yes|

|Condition number|—|—|Exponential in $n$|

> [!tip] Decision Rule
> 
> - Need to transform between polynomial bases? → Use $L_n$ or $U_n$
> - Need a clean SPD example with exact Cholesky? → Use $S_n = L_n L_n^T$
> - Need to verify a matrix identity combinatorially? → Pascal matrix rows/columns encode Vandermonde
> - Numerical work for large $n$? → **Avoid** $S_n$ (exponential ill-conditioning)

## Algorithm: Constructing Pascal Matrices

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb

def pascal_lower(n):
    """Lower triangular Pascal matrix L_n."""
    L = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=int)
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            L[i, j] = comb(i, j)   # 0-indexed: C(i, j)
    return L

def pascal_symmetric(n):
    """Symmetric Pascal matrix S_n = L @ L.T"""
    L = pascal_lower(n)
    return L @ L.T

def pascal_inverse_lower(n):
    """Inverse of L_n: negate off-diagonal entries."""
    L_inv = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=int)
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1):
            L_inv[i, j] = ((-1) ** (i + j)) * comb(i, j)
    return L_inv

# Verification
n = 4
L = pascal_lower(n)
L_inv = pascal_inverse_lower(n)
assert np.allclose(L @ L_inv, np.eye(n))   # L * L^{-1} = I
S = pascal_symmetric(n)
assert np.allclose(np.linalg.cholesky(S), L.astype(float))  # Cholesky of S is L
```

> [!note] SciPy Built-in
> 
> ```python
> from scipy.linalg import pascal
> L = pascal(n, kind='lower')   # Lower triangular
> S = pascal(n, kind='symmetric')   # Symmetric
> ```
> 
> `scipy.linalg.pascal` uses exact integer arithmetic and avoids floating-point error.

## Summary: Why the Pascal Matrix Is Algebraically Special

The Pascal matrix sits at the intersection of combinatorics and linear algebra precisely because:

1. **Unit determinant** — despite combinatorial growth in entries, the volume is preserved
2. **Self-inverse structure** — $L_n^{-1}$ is obtained by a sign pattern, not by row reduction
3. **Cholesky = itself** — $S_n = L_n L_n^T$ is the cleanest possible Cholesky factorization ($D = I$)
4. **One-parameter group** — $L_n(s) L_n(t) = L_n(s+t)$ makes it a matrix exponential
5. **Finite difference operator** — it encodes Newton interpolation and polynomial basis changes

> [!note] Connection to Matrix Exponential
> 
> Define $N_n$ as the strictly lower triangular matrix with 1s on the first subdiagonal (the nilpotent shift matrix). Then: $$L_n = e^{N_n} = I + N_n + \frac{N_n^2}{2!} + \cdots$$
> 
> The lower Pascal matrix is the **matrix exponential of the nilpotent shift**. This explains the one-parameter group property: $e^{sN} e^{tN} = e^{(s+t)N}$. It also explains why $L_n^{-1} = e^{-N_n}$ — inversion just negates the exponent, producing the alternating sign pattern.
