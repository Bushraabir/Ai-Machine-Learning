13.Parametric Surfaces

13.Parametric Surfaces

Apr 23, 202624 min read

“A curve is a map from an interval into space — one parameter, one dimension of freedom. A surface is a map from a region in the plane into space — two parameters, two dimensions of freedom. The whole theory of surface integrals, Stokes’ theorem, and curvature flows from this one idea: a surface is not a set of points, it is a parametrization.”

§1. Motivation — Why Parametrize?

A sphere of radius R is the set (x,y,z):x2+y2+z2=R2. This implicit description tells you which points are on the sphere but tells you nothing about how to integrate over it, compute its area, or define a normal vector field on it. For all of those tasks you need a map — a function that sweeps through the surface as its inputs vary.

The same principle that lets us integrate over a curve (parametrize it, pull back to a single-variable integral) extends to surfaces: parametrize it, pull back to a double integral. The parametrization introduces coordinates on the surface; the Jacobian of the parametrization tells you how area is distorted.

Almost every computable surface integral — flux, area, work — requires a parametrization. The natural objects of Stokes’ theorem (the surface integral of curl) and the divergence theorem (the flux through a closed surface) are defined via parametrizations. This is not a computational trick; it is the correct definition.

§2. Definition

2.1 Parametric Surface

Definition. A parametric surface is a C1 map r:D⊆R2⟶R3 r(u,v)=⟨x(u,v),;y(u,v),;z(u,v)⟩ where D is a bounded region in the uv-plane (the parameter domain).

The image S=r(D)⊆R3 is the surface.

The variables (u,v) are the parameters. Each point (u0​,v0​)∈D maps to a single point r(u0​,v0​)∈S. As (u,v) varies over D, the image r(u,v) sweeps out S.

Warning. The parametrization r and the surface S=r(D) are different objects. Many different parametrizations can give the same surface. The geometric properties of S (area, normals, curvature) must be independent of the choice of parametrization; the formulas will involve the Jacobian precisely to ensure this independence.

2.2 Regular Points and Regular Surfaces

Definition. A point (u0​,v0​)∈D is regular if the partial derivatives ru​ and rv​ are linearly independent at (u0​,v0​): ru​(u0​,v0​)×rv​(u0​,v0​)=0 A regular surface is one where every interior point of D is regular.

Why this matters: At a regular point, ru​ and rv​ span a 2-dimensional tangent plane at r(u0​,v0​). At a non-regular (singular) point, the surface may self-intersect, form a cusp, or have no well-defined tangent plane. Singular points must be handled separately (they are often isolated and contribute zero area, so integrals are still well-defined).

§3. The Tangent Plane and Normal Vector

3.1 Partial Derivatives as Tangent Vectors

Fix (u0​,v0​)∈D. The curve u↦r(u,v0​) is a curve on S through r(u0​,v0​); its tangent at u0​ is:

ru​(u0​,v0​)=⟨∂u∂x​,;∂u∂y​,;∂u∂z​⟩

Similarly, v↦r(u0​,v) is a curve through r(u0​,v0​) with tangent:

rv​(u0​,v0​)=⟨∂v∂x​,;∂v∂y​,;∂v∂z​⟩

These two tangent vectors span the tangent plane to S at r(u0​,v0​).

3.2 The Normal Vector

The normal vector to S at r(u0​,v0​) is:

N(u,v)=ru​×rv​

This vector is perpendicular to both ru​ and rv​, hence perpendicular to the tangent plane — i.e., normal to the surface. The unit normal is:

n^=∣ru​×rv​∣ru​×rv​​

Orientation: The choice of parameter ordering (u,v) vs (v,u) determines the direction of N (flipping the order reverses the sign). A consistent orientation of S requires choosing N continuously across S. Not all surfaces admit a consistent orientation (e.g., the Möbius strip does not), but all the surfaces in Stewart and MIT 18.02 do.

3.3 The Fundamental Quantity: ∣ru​×rv​∣

The magnitude ∣ru​×rv​∣ is the area distortion factor of the parametrization — it tells you how much a small du,dv rectangle in parameter space gets stretched when mapped to the surface. This is the surface-theoretic analog of ∣f′(t)∣ for arc length.

§4. Surface Area

4.1 Definition and Formula

Definition. The surface area of a regular parametric surface r:D→R3 is: A(S)=∬D​∣ru​×rv​∣,dA

Derivation. Tile D with small rectangles [u,u+Δu]×[v,v+Δv]. The image of such a rectangle under r is approximately a parallelogram spanned by the vectors: ru​,Δuandrv​,Δv

The area of a parallelogram spanned by vectors a and b is ∣a×b∣. So the area of the image rectangle is approximately: ∣ru​,Δu×rv​,Δv∣=∣ru​×rv​∣,Δu,Δv

Summing over all rectangles and taking the limit gives the double integral. □

4.2 Special Case: Graph of a Function

If S is the graph z=f(x,y) over a region D in the xy-plane, parametrize by: r(x,y)=⟨x,;y,;f(x,y)⟩

Then: rx​=⟨1,0,fx​⟩,ry​=⟨0,1,fy​⟩

rx​×ry​=​i​j​k 1​0​fx​ 0​1​fy​​​=⟨−fx​,;−fy​,;1⟩

∣rx​×ry​∣=fx2​+fy2​+1​

A(S)=∬D​1+(∂x∂f​)2+(∂y∂f​)2​,dA​

This is the formula in Stewart §16.6 — it is a special case of the general formula.

§5. Surface Integrals

There are two kinds of surface integrals, analogous to the two kinds of line integrals: scalar (integrating a function) and vector (integrating a vector field — flux).

5.1 Scalar Surface Integral

Definition. For a scalar function f:R3→R, the surface integral of f over S is: ∬S​f,dS=∬D​f(r(u,v)),∣ru​×rv​∣,dA

The element dS=∣ru​×rv​∣,dA is the scalar area element — an infinitesimal patch of surface area.

What it computes:

  • f≡1: gives the surface area A(S)
  • f(r)= mass density: gives total mass of the surface
  • f(r)= temperature: gives total “heat content” on the surface

5.2 Vector Surface Integral (Flux)

Definition. For a vector field F:R3→R3, the flux integral (or vector surface integral) of F through S is: ∬S​F⋅dS=∬D​F(r(u,v))⋅(ru​×rv​),dA

The element dS=(ru​×rv​),dA is the vector area element — an infinitesimal area patch with direction given by the normal.

Equivalently: ∬S​F⋅dS=∬S​F⋅n^,dS, where n^=(ru​×rv​)/∣ru​×rv​∣ and dS=∣ru​×rv​∣,dA.

Physical meaning: The flux integral measures how much of F passes through the surface per unit time. If F is a fluid velocity field, ∬S​F⋅dS is the volumetric flow rate through S.

Key sign convention. The sign of the flux integral depends on orientation. Reversing the orientation of S (swapping (u,v) to (v,u)) changes the sign of ru​×rv​, hence the sign of the flux. Always specify the orientation: “outward normal” for closed surfaces, or specify which side.

§6. Proof: Independence of Parametrization

The area and flux integrals must be independent of the choice of parametrization. We verify this for area.

Setup. Let r:D→S and r~:D~→S be two regular parametrizations of the same surface, related by a C1 bijection φ:D~→D with r~=r∘φ. Let φ(s,t)=(u(s,t),v(s,t)).

Claim: ∬D​∣ru​×rv​∣,du,dv=∬D~​∣r~s​×r~t​∣,ds,dt.

Proof. By the chain rule: r~s​=ru​,us​+rv​,vs​,r~t​=ru​,ut​+rv​,vt​

Compute the cross product: r~s​×r~t​=(ru​,us​+rv​,vs​)×(ru​,ut​+rv​,vt​) =(us​vt​−ut​vs​)(ru​×rv​)=Jφ​⋅(ru​×rv​)

where Jφ​=us​vt​−ut​vs​=det(Dφ) is the Jacobian determinant of φ. Taking magnitudes: ∣r~s​×r~t​∣=∣Jφ​∣⋅∣ru​×rv​∣

By the change-of-variables theorem for double integrals: ∬D~​∣r~s​×r~t​∣,ds,dt=∬D~​∣Jφ​∣⋅∣ru​×rv​∣∘φ,ds,dt=∬D​∣ru​×rv​∣,du,dv□

The ∣Jφ​∣ from the cross product cancels with (or is identified as) the ∣Jφ​∣ from the change-of-variables formula. This is not a coincidence — it is the content of the fact that the integral of a 2-form over a surface is well-defined.

§10. The First Fundamental Form (Metric Tensor)

The first fundamental form encodes the intrinsic geometry of the surface — distances and angles as measured within the surface itself, without reference to the ambient R3.

Definition. The first fundamental form of a parametric surface r(u,v) is the 2×2 symmetric matrix: g=(E​F F​G​)=(ru​⋅ru​​ru​⋅rv​ rv​⋅ru​​rv​⋅rv​​)

The area element in terms of g: ∣ru​×rv​∣=EG−F2​=detg​

Proof. By the Lagrange identity: ∣a×b∣2=∣a∣2∣b∣2−(a⋅b)2. So:

∣ru​×rv​∣2=∣ru​∣2∣rv​∣2−(ru​⋅rv​)2=EG−F2=detg□

Meaning of each coefficient:

  • E=∣ru​∣2: squared speed along u-parameter curves
  • G=∣rv​∣2: squared speed along v-parameter curves
  • F=ru​⋅rv​: how oblique the coordinate curves are (F=0 means orthogonal parameter curves)

When F=0 (orthogonal parameters), the area element simplifies to EG​,du,dv.

Arc length on the surface: ds2=E,du2+2F,du,dv+G,dv2

This is the Riemannian metric induced on the surface by the ambient Euclidean metric. Everything about the intrinsic geometry of the surface — distances, angles, geodesics, Gaussian curvature — is determined by g.

§11. Stokes’ Theorem and the Divergence Theorem — Revisited

Now that we have the language of parametric surfaces, the two great theorems take their final form.

11.1 Stokes’ Theorem

Theorem (Stokes). Let S be an oriented regular parametric surface with boundary ∂S (oriented consistently via the right-hand rule), and let F be a C1 vector field. Then: ∬S​(∇×F)⋅dS=∮∂S​F⋅dr

The surface integral of the curl equals the line integral around the boundary. This requires a parametric surface to even state: dS is defined via ru​×rv​.

11.2 Divergence Theorem

Theorem (Gauss). Let E be a solid region in R3 with boundary ∂E (a closed surface, oriented with outward normals), and let F be C1. Then: ∭E​(∇⋅F),dV=∬​∂E​F⋅dS

The boundary integral ∬​∂E​F⋅dS is a surface integral over a closed parametric surface — it requires parametrizing the boundary.

The connection: Both theorems say the same thing in different dimensions:

∫∂M​ω=∫M​dω

This is Stokes’ theorem in differential forms, where M is a manifold with boundary and ω is a differential form. For M = curve, this is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. For M = surface, this is Stokes. For M = solid region, this is the Divergence Theorem. Parametric surfaces are the concrete objects that make ∫M​dω computable.

§12. Piecewise Smooth Surfaces

Most physically relevant surfaces are not globally smooth — they have edges and corners. A piecewise smooth surface is a finite union of regular parametric surfaces S1​,S2​,…,Sk​ meeting along curves (edges), with consistent orientation.

Examples:

  • A cube: 6 flat faces meeting at edges and corners
  • A cylinder with caps: lateral surface + top disk + bottom disk
  • Any polyhedron

For such surfaces: ∬S​F⋅dS=∑i=1k​∬Si​​F⋅dSi​

The edge contributions cancel if orientation is consistent, so Stokes’ and the divergence theorem still hold.

§13. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

Parametric surfaces appear in ML wherever a low-dimensional structure is embedded in a high-dimensional space, wherever a model outputs a surface, or wherever geometry is computed on learned manifolds. The keyword: manifold, mesh, implicit surface, surface reconstruction, 3D generation.

13.1 Neural Implicit Surfaces — The Zero Level Set

The dominant paradigm in neural 3D representation is to represent a surface implicitly: learn a function fθ​:R3→R such that the surface is the zero level set:

S=(x,y,z):fθ​(x,y,z)=0

This is how NeRF (Mildenhall et al. 2020), DeepSDF (Park et al. 2019), and Occupancy Networks (Mescheder et al. 2019) represent geometry. The surface is implicit; to compute surface integrals, area, or normals, one must extract a parametrization — either analytically (via implicit function theorem) or computationally (via marching cubes).

The unit normal at a point of the zero level set is: n^=∣∇fθ​∣∇fθ​​

This is the gradient of the implicit function, normalized. Computing normals requires computing ∇fθ​ — automatic differentiation through the network. The Eikonal constraint ∣∇fθ​∣=1 (used in IGR, NeuS) ensures the function is a signed distance function, which stabilizes training and ensures the normal computation is well-conditioned.

SDF (Signed Distance Functions): fθ​(x)= signed distance from x to S. For SDFs:

  • ∇fθ​(x)=n^ at surface points
  • The mean curvature of S is H=21​∇⋅(∇f/∣∇f∣)=21​∇⋅n^ — a divergence of the normal field
  • The surface area can be recovered as ∫∣∇f∣,dV via the co-area formula

13.2 Neural Parametric Surfaces — Explicit Surface Generation

Some models directly output a parametrization:

rθ​:[0,1]2→R3

AtlasNet (Groueix et al. 2018) represents a 3D shape as a collection of learned maps from the 2D unit square to patches on the surface — literally learned parametric surface patches. The model generates a point cloud by sampling (u,v) from [0,1]2 and evaluating rθ​(u,v,c) where c is a shape latent code.

The geometric quantities of these surfaces — area, normals, curvature — are computed via ru​×rv​ directly using automatic differentiation.

Surface area regularization in these models: Larea​=∬[0,1]2​∣ru​×rv​∣,du,dv

This prevents the learned surface from self-intersecting or becoming degenerate (the integrand going to zero = parametrization becoming singular). The regularizer is exactly the surface area formula — penalizing large area encourages compact surfaces.

13.3 Geometry Processing and Mesh-Based Deep Learning

Meshes are piecewise-linear parametric surfaces — a union of triangular patches. Each triangle is parametrized by barycentric coordinates (u,v) with u,v≥0, u+v≤1.

For a triangle with vertices v0​,v1​,v2​: r(u,v)=(1−u−v)v0​+uv1​+vv2​

ru​=v1​−v0​,rv​=v2​−v0​

∣ru​×rv​∣=2⋅Area(triangle)

This is the foundational formula in mesh-based neural networks (MeshCNN, Neural Subdivision, DiffMesh, etc.):

  • Normal prediction: compute ru​×rv​ per triangle — face normal
  • Laplace-Beltrami operator on meshes: the cotangent Laplacian, used as the graph Laplacian in spectral GNNs on 3D shapes, is derived from the cotangents of triangle angles — related to the metric coefficients E,F,G
  • Differentiable rendering: rendering a mesh requires integrating over visible surface patches — a surface integral with visibility weighting. Backpropagating through this requires differentiating ∣ru​×rv​∣ with respect to vertex positions vi​.

13.4 Differentiable Rendering — Surface Integrals in 3D Vision

Differentiable rendering (SoftRas, DIB-R, NVDiffrast, nvdiffrec) aims to backpropagate gradients through the rendering process to recover 3D geometry from 2D images. The rendering equation is a surface integral:

Lo​(x,ωo​)=∫Ω+​fr​(x,ωi​,ωo​),Li​(x,ωi​),(ωi​⋅n^),dωi​

where Lo​ is outgoing radiance, fr​ is the BRDF (bidirectional reflectance distribution function), and dωi​ is the solid angle measure. This integral is over the hemisphere above the surface — it is a surface integral on the sphere of directions, weighted by the cosine (ωi​⋅n^).

The normal n^ is computed from the parametrization via ru​×rv​. Backpropagating through the rendering equation to recover the geometry parameters θ requires differentiating surface integrals with respect to the parametrization — this is why differentiable rendering is hard and why the mathematical machinery of parametric surfaces is central to it.

13.5 Gaussian Splatting and Point Cloud Representations

3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al. 2023) represents a scene as a collection of 3D Gaussian “splats” — each Gaussian has a mean position, a covariance matrix, color, and opacity. The covariance matrix Σ=RSSTRT (R = rotation, S = scaling) implicitly defines a local surface patch: the level set x:(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ)=c is an ellipsoid — a parametric surface.

The rendering of Gaussian splatting involves projecting these 3D Gaussians onto the 2D image plane — a projection that depends on the viewing direction and the surface normal (computed from Σ). The per-Gaussian surface area and normal are computed from the eigendecomposition of Σ, which is the surface theoretic machinery of the first fundamental form in disguise.

13.6 Physics Simulations — FEM and Surface Integrals

Finite Element Method (FEM) for physics simulation (used in neural physics simulators like PAC-NeRF, DiffSim, NeuralSim) divides the domain into triangular or tetrahedral elements. Each element is a parametric surface/volume. Simulation quantities — stress, strain, force — involve surface integrals over element boundaries:

finternal​=∮∂E​σ⋅n^,dS

where σ is the stress tensor. This is a surface integral over each element’s boundary. Neural FEM methods learn to parameterize the displacement field and compute these surface integrals via automatic differentiation through the parametrization.

13.7 Manifold Learning and Intrinsic Geometry

Diffusion maps, UMAP, isomap, and other manifold learning methods assume that high-dimensional data lies near a low-dimensional manifold — a generalized parametric surface. The goal is to find coordinates (u1​,…,uk​) such that the data is (approximately) the image of a smooth map r:Rk→Rn.

The Riemannian metric gij​=rui​​⋅ruj​​ on the manifold — the first fundamental form — determines geodesic distances, which are what these algorithms try to preserve or compute.

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic distances:

  • Extrinsic: Euclidean distance in the ambient Rn
  • Intrinsic: geodesic distance along the manifold, measured by the metric g

UMAP and isomap approximate intrinsic distances. The correctness of these approximations depends on the geometry of the parametric surface: how curved it is, how the metric g compares to the flat Euclidean metric.

Latent diffusion models and VAEs can be understood as learning a parametrization of the data manifold: the decoder rθ​:Rk→Rn is a parametric map from latent space to data space, and the geometry of this map (Jacobian structure, metric, area distortion) affects the quality of generated samples.

§15. Personal Notes

  • The formula dS=∣ru​×rv​∣,du,dv is the analogy of ds=∣r′(t)∣,dt for arc length. Both involve the norm of a derivative (or cross product of derivatives) as the “stretching factor.” For curves: length of tangent vector. For surfaces: area of the parallelogram of two tangent vectors. This pattern extends to k-manifolds in Rn: the k-dimensional volume element is the norm of the wedge product of k tangent vectors.

  • The proof of parametrization-independence (§6) is the cleanest illustration of why the cross product and the change-of-variables theorem are duals of each other. The Jacobian of the reparametrization that appears in the cross product formula is exactly the one absorbed by the change-of-variables — this is the statement that a 2-form transforms correctly under diffeomorphism. The surface integral is really the integral of a differential form, and differential forms know how to transform.

  • The first fundamental form g=(E​F F​G​) is the Riemannian metric. It contains all intrinsic geometry. The Gaussian curvature K (a single function on S) is determined entirely by g — this is Gauss’s Theorema Egregium. Curvature is intrinsic. A flat map of the Earth must distort something (either areas or angles or both), and the quantitative measure of how much distortion is unavoidable is K. In ML, the Riemannian metric on the data manifold is what manifold learning algorithms are trying to approximate.

  • The two surface integrals (∬S​f,dS scalar vs. ∬S​F⋅dS flux) are genuinely different objects. The scalar integral is orientation-independent (reversing the normal doesn’t change dS=∣ru​×rv​∣,dA). The flux integral is orientation-dependent (reversing the normal changes dS=(ru​×rv​),dA to −dS). Always check which you are computing.

  • In ML, the most important surface integral is the rendering equation and the most important parametric surface tool is computing normals via n^=∇f/∣∇f∣ for implicit surfaces. Both reduce to the core of this note: ru​×rv​ gives normals and area elements. The entire pipeline from 3D neural reconstruction to differentiable rendering runs on this formula.

  • The fact that the Divergence Theorem and Stokes’ Theorem both require surface integrals over oriented surfaces is not a technicality — it reflects the physical reality that flux has a sign. Gauss’s law ∇⋅E=ρ/ε0​ integrates to ∬​S​E⋅dS=Qenc​/ε0​, where the sign of the enclosed charge determines the sign of the flux. Orientation is physics.
