19.The Divergence Theorem

Apr 23, 202616 min read

“The boundary knows everything about the interior — if you know how to listen.”

§ 0 — The Philosophical Setup

We have now seen the pattern twice:

TheoremIntegrates overBoundary is
Fundamental Theorem of CalculusInterval [a,b]Two points a,b
Green’s TheoremRegion D⊂R2Closed curve ∂D
Stokes’ TheoremSurface S⊂R3Closed curve ∂S
Divergence TheoremVolume E⊂R3Closed surface ∂E

Each theorem moves one dimension up. Each says the same thing in a new costume: a derivative integrated over a region equals the original quantity on the boundary.

The Divergence theorem closes the trilogy in R3. It relates the flux of a vector field through a closed surface to the divergence of that field inside the enclosed volume. It is the theorem behind fluid mechanics, electrostatics, heat flow, and a surprising amount of modern machine learning.

§ 1 — Divergence: What It Measures

Before the theorem, we need to internalize what divergence actually is.

1.1 Definition

For F=⟨P,Q,R⟩ with continuous partial derivatives:

∇⋅F=∂x∂P​+∂y∂Q​+∂z∂R​

1.2 Physical Meaning

Place an infinitesimal volume element δV at a point p. The divergence (∇⋅F)(p) is the net rate of flux leaving that volume per unit volume:

(∇⋅F)(p)=limδV→0​∣δV∣1​∬​∂(δV)​F⋅dS

  • ∇⋅F>0 at p: p is a source — field lines emanate outward.
  • ∇⋅F<0 at p: p is a sink — field lines flow inward.
  • ∇⋅F=0 at p: incompressible at p — no net creation or destruction.

Visualization:

  Source (div > 0)       Sink (div < 0)      Incompressible (div = 0)
  
   ↖  ↑  ↗               ↘  ↓  ↙               →  →  →
   ←  ·  →               →  ·  ←               →  →  →
   ↙  ↓  ↘               ↗  ↑  ↖               →  →  →
  
  (outward flow)         (inward flow)         (no net flux)

§ 2 — The Theorem

2.1 Setup

Let E be a simple solid region in R3 — meaning it is bounded, and its boundary ∂E=S is a piecewise-smooth closed surface. We orient S with the outward unit normal n (pointing away from E).

2.2 Statement

Theorem (Gauss–Ostrogradsky, commonly “Divergence Theorem”). Let E be a simple solid region with outward-oriented boundary surface S=∂E. Let F=⟨P,Q,R⟩ be a vector field with continuous partial derivatives on an open set containing E. Then:

∬​S​F⋅dS=∭E​(∇⋅F),dV

Left side: The total flux of F outward through the closed surface S.

Right side: The total divergence — the sum of all sources and sinks — inside E.

The ∬​ notation emphasizes that S is a closed surface (no boundary of its own).

2.3 The Intuition in One Sentence

What flows out of the box must have been created inside it.

This is a conservation principle. If more field lines exit than enter, there must be sources inside. If more enter than exit, there are sinks inside. If the field is divergence-free, the flux in equals the flux out — exactly.

§ 3 — Proof

We prove the theorem for a simple solid region — one that is simultaneously type I, II, and III. The full proof decomposes into three separate equalities:

∬​S​F⋅dS=∬​S​P,dydz+Q,dzdx+R,dxdy

and we prove each component separately. We demonstrate only the R component; the others are identical in structure.

Claim: ∬​S​R,dxdy=∭E​∂z∂R​,dV

Step 1: Write E as a Type I Region

E=(x,y,z):(x,y)∈D, ϕ1​(x,y)≤z≤ϕ2​(x,y)

where D is the projection of E onto the xy-plane, and ϕ1​,ϕ2​ are smooth functions with ϕ1​≤ϕ2​.

Step 2: Compute the triple integral

By Fubini:

∭E​∂z∂R​,dV=∬D​[∫ϕ1​(x,y)ϕ2​(x,y)​∂z∂R​,dz]dA

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in z:

=∬D​[R(x,y,ϕ2​(x,y))−R(x,y,ϕ1​(x,y))]dA

Step 3: Decompose the surface integral

The boundary S=∂E consists of three parts:

  • S2​: the top surface z=ϕ2​(x,y), with upward outward normal.
  • S1​: the bottom surface z=ϕ1​(x,y), with downward outward normal.
  • S3​: the lateral surface (if any), which is vertical: its outward normal is horizontal, so n⋅k=0, contributing nothing to the R,dxdy component.

Top surface S2​ (outward normal points up, dxdy>0):

∬S2​​R,dxdy=∬D​R(x,y,ϕ2​(x,y)),dA

Bottom surface S1​ (outward normal points down, dxdy<0):

∬S1​​R,dxdy=−∬D​R(x,y,ϕ1​(x,y)),dA

Step 4: Combine

∬​S​R,dxdy=∬S2​​+∬S1​​+=0∬S3​​​​=∬D​[R(x,y,ϕ2​)−R(x,y,ϕ1​)]dA

This equals the triple integral from Step 2. The P and Q components follow by the same argument treating E as a type II and type III region. Adding all three:

∬​S​F⋅dS=∭E​(∂x∂P​+∂y∂Q​+∂z∂R​)dV=∭E​(∇⋅F),dV■

Remark on the general case: For regions that are not simultaneously type I/II/III, we decompose E into finitely many such sub-regions. Interior boundary surfaces are shared between two adjacent regions with opposite outward normals, so their contributions to the surface integral cancel. Only the true outer boundary ∂E survives — the same telescoping argument as in Stokes.

§ 4 — Geometric Interpretation

The “Box” Picture

Imagine a vector field F representing fluid velocity. Enclose a region E in a box. The Divergence theorem says:

Net fluid leaving through walls=∫∫∫(fluid being created inside)

If the fluid is incompressible (∇⋅F=0 everywhere in E), then the net outward flux through any closed surface enclosing a region with no singularities is zero. Every drop that flows out was already there.

The Differential Version

The divergence theorem is the integral form of the divergence. The local statement is:

(∇⋅F)(p)=lim∣E∣→0, p∈E​∣E∣1​∬​∂E​F⋅dS

This is the definition physicists often use for divergence, and the divergence theorem is what makes it consistent with the coordinate formula Px​+Qy​+Rz​.

§ 5 — Worked Examples

Example 1: Direct Verification

Setup: F=⟨x,y,z⟩, E the unit ball x2+y2+z2≤1.

Right side (triple integral):

∇⋅F=1+1+1=3

∭E​3,dV=3⋅34π​=4π

Left side (surface integral):

On the unit sphere S, the outward unit normal is n=⟨x,y,z⟩ (since ∣r∣=1). So:

F⋅n=x2+y2+z2=1on S

∬​S​F⋅dS=∬​S​1,dS=Area(S)=4π✓

Example 2: Avoiding a Nasty Surface Integral

Problem: Compute ∬​S​F⋅dS where F=⟨x3,y3,z3⟩ and S is the surface of the cylinder x2+y2≤4, 0≤z≤3 (top, bottom, and lateral surface).

Why use the Divergence Theorem: Direct computation requires three separate surface integrals (top disk, bottom disk, curved lateral wall). The theorem replaces all three with one triple integral.

∇⋅F=3x2+3y2+3z2

In cylindrical coordinates (r,θ,z) with r∈[0,2], θ∈[0,2π], z∈[0,3]:

∭E​(3x2+3y2+3z2),dV=∫02π​∫02​∫03​3(r2+z2),r,dz,dr,dθ

=2π∫02​∫03​3r(r2+z2),dz,dr=2π∫02​[3r3z+rz3]03​dr

=2π∫02​(9r3+27r),dr=2π[49r4​+227r2​]02​

=2π(36+54)=180π

Example 3: The Divergence Theorem with a Hole

Setup: Let F=∣r∣3r​=(x2+y2+z2)3/2⟨x,y,z⟩​. This is the electric field of a point charge at the origin.

Compute: ∇⋅F=0 for all (x,y,z)=(0,0,0). But F has a singularity at the origin — so the theorem does not apply directly to any region containing the origin.

Fix: Let E be the region between a small sphere Sε​ of radius ε and a large sphere SR​ of radius R, both centered at the origin. The boundary of E is SR​ (outward normal pointing out) and Sε​ (outward normal pointing inward, i.e., toward origin).

Since ∇⋅F=0 on E:

0=∭E​∇⋅F,dV=∬​SR​​F⋅dS−∬​Sε​​F⋅dS

On Sε​: F⋅n=ε21​ and Area(Sε​)=4πε2, so:

∬​Sε​​F⋅dS=ε21​⋅4πε2=4π

Therefore ∬​SR​​F⋅dS=4π for any R>0.

This is Gauss’s Law in electrostatics. The total electric flux through any closed surface surrounding a point charge q is q/ε0​ — independent of the surface’s shape or size.

The singularity is captured by the Dirac delta distribution: ∇⋅F=4πδ(3)(r), which integrates to 4π over any region containing the origin. This is the distributional version of the divergence theorem — the full story lives in functional analysis.

Example 4: Computing Volume from a Surface Integral

An elegant corollary: since ∇⋅r=3 for r=⟨x,y,z⟩:

Vol(E)=31​∬​∂E​r⋅dS

This lets us compute volumes purely from boundary data — useful in computer graphics and numerical simulation.

Similarly, with F=⟨x,0,0⟩: ∇⋅F=1, so Vol(E)=∬​S​x,dydz. Different choices of F give different but equivalent formulas.

§ 6 — Special Cases and Connections

6.1 Green’s First and Second Identities

These are major consequences of the Divergence theorem, used throughout PDEs.

Green’s First Identity: Take F=f∇g, so ∇⋅F=∇f⋅∇g+f∇2g. Then:

∬​S​f(∇g⋅n),dS=∭E​(∇f⋅∇g+f∇2g),dV

Green’s Second Identity: Subtract the same with f and g swapped:

∬​S​(f∇g−g∇f)⋅n,dS=∭E​(f∇2g−g∇2f),dV

These identities are foundational in the study of harmonic functions and are the integral backbone of the theory of elliptic PDEs — which includes the Laplace and Poisson equations everywhere in physics.

6.2 Integration by Parts in Higher Dimensions

Green’s First Identity is the R3 analogue of integration by parts. The surface integral term is the “boundary term.” When f=0 on ∂E (Dirichlet boundary condition), the boundary term vanishes and we get:

∭E​f∇2g,dV=−∭E​∇f⋅∇g,dV

This is used constantly in the weak formulation of PDEs — the foundation of the Finite Element Method.

6.3 Incompressible Flow

In fluid mechanics, a velocity field u is incompressible (divergence-free) if ∇⋅u=0. The Divergence theorem then says: for any control volume E:

∬​∂E​u⋅dS=0

What flows in equals what flows out. Water, for practical purposes, is incompressible. So is the magnetic field (∇⋅B=0 — one of Maxwell’s equations). This is the fourth Maxwell equation in integral form:

∬​S​B⋅dS=0

There are no magnetic monopoles — no sources or sinks of magnetic field.

§ 7 — Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

7.1 Continuous Normalizing Flows and the Divergence

Normalizing flows learn a diffeomorphism x(t) satisfying an ODE x˙=v(x,t), where v is a neural network. The log-density evolves as:

dtdlogp(x(t))​=−∇⋅v(x(t),t)

The divergence of the neural velocity field is the core quantity. Computing ∇⋅v exactly requires computing the full Jacobian (O(d2) operations for dimension d) — expensive in high dimensions. This is why Hutchinson’s trace estimator is used:

∇⋅v=tr(Jv​)≈Eϵ​[ϵTJv​ϵ]

The divergence theorem guarantees the geometric meaning of this quantity: it is the infinitesimal flux per unit volume that determines how the probability density transforms. Getting this wrong — e.g., using a field with spurious divergence — will produce an incorrect generative model.

7.2 Score-Based Generative Models (Diffusion)

Score-based generative models (the backbone of modern image generation: Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, etc.) learn the score function ∇x​logp(x) — the gradient of the log-density.

The score matching objective can be derived using integration by parts (i.e., Green’s First Identity / Divergence theorem):

E[∣sθ​(x)−∇x​logp(x)∣2]=E[∣sθ​(x)∣2+2∇⋅sθ​(x)]+const

The 2∇⋅sθ​ term comes directly from integration by parts, where the boundary term vanishes because p(x)→0 at infinity. Without the Divergence theorem / Green’s identity, this simplification — which makes score matching tractable — does not go through.

7.3 Physics-Informed Neural Networks: Enforcing Conservation Laws

PINNs embed physical constraints directly into the training loss. Conservation laws — mass, momentum, energy — are fundamentally divergence conditions:

  • Mass conservation: ∂t∂ρ​+∇⋅(ρu)=0
  • Momentum conservation (Navier–Stokes): ∂t∂(ρu)​+∇⋅(ρu⊗u)=−∇p+μ∇2u

The Divergence theorem converts these differential conservation laws to integral balance laws over control volumes. When training PINNs, enforcing the divergence condition on the velocity field (incompressibility) is one of the hardest constraints to satisfy — getting it wrong produces a field that “creates” or “destroys” fluid at random points, giving nonphysical results.

7.4 Graph Neural Networks and Discrete Divergence

The Divergence theorem has a discrete analogue on graphs. If G=(V,E) is a graph and f:E→R is an edge flow, the discrete divergence at a node v is:

(div,f)(v)=∑e∋v​±f(e)

summing over edges incident to v with sign depending on orientation. The discrete Divergence theorem says:

∑v∈V​(div,f)(v)=∑e∈∂Ebdry​​f(e)

Graph Neural Networks that enforce flow conservation (e.g., for traffic prediction, molecular dynamics, power grids) use exactly this structure. The design of “divergence-free” message passing — where no “information” is created or destroyed at nodes — mirrors the continuous incompressibility condition.

7.5 Variational Inference and the ELBO

The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) in variational autoencoders involves:

KL[q(z∣x)∣p(z)]=∫q(z∣x)logp(z)q(z∣x)​,dz

When q is parameterized as a flow (change of variables through a diffeomorphism), the Jacobian determinant appears, and computing it uses the divergence of the flow field. The Divergence theorem provides the geometric grounding: the change of density under a flow is governed by how much the flow “compresses” or “expands” volume elements locally, measured by the divergence.

§ 8 — Stokes vs. Divergence: The Complete Picture

This is the question that trips everyone up. Here is the definitive comparison:

Stokes’ TheoremDivergence Theorem
IntegratesCurl over a surfaceDivergence over a volume
Boundary isA closed curveA closed surface
OrientationRight-hand rule (curve relative to surface normal)Outward normal on the surface
Dimension∂S is 1D, S is 2D∂E is 2D, E is 3D
MeasuresCirculationNet flux
Key identity∇×F integrated over S∇⋅F integrated over E
Green’s theoremSpecial case (flat surface)Special case (2D, via ∇⋅)

They are siblings in the generalized Stokes theorem ∫∂Ω​ω=∫Ω​dω:

  • Stokes: ω is a 1-form, dω is a 2-form (the curl).
  • Divergence: ω is a 2-form, dω is a 3-form (the divergence).

The unified framework: In the language of differential forms on an n-manifold:

∫∂Ω​ω=∫Ω​dω

Everything — FTC, Green, Stokes, Divergence — is this one equation.

Forgetting to include all pieces of ∂EA closed surface must enclose the region completelyCheck: top, bottom, lateral walls, or any missing caps
Confusing ∬​ (closed surface) with ∬ (open surface)Stokes allows open surfaces; Divergence requires a closed oneDivergence theorem always has the ∬​ notation
Computing ∇⋅F incorrectlyEspecially with products and compositionsWrite each component derivative carefully; check with simple test case

§ 10 — Summary Card

∬​∂E​F⋅dS=∭E​(∇⋅F),dV​

ObjectWhat it is
ESimple solid region, bounded, piecewise smooth boundary
∂E=SClosed surface, oriented with outward normal
FC1 vector field on open set ⊇E
∇⋅FDivergence: local source/sink density
F⋅dSOutward flux through the surface

The theorem says: Total outward flux through the boundary = Total divergence (source strength) inside.

Special cases:

  • ∇⋅F=0 everywhere in E ⇒ zero net flux through any enclosing surface.
  • ∇⋅r=3 ⇒ Vol(E)=31​∬​∂E​r⋅dS.

§ 11 — The Three Theorems Together

FTC∫ab​f′(x),dx=f(b)−f(a)​​⟶Stokes∬S​(∇×F)⋅dS=∮∂S​F⋅dr​​⟶Divergence∭E​(∇⋅F),dV=∬​∂E​F⋅dS​​

Each one says: integrate a derivative over a region, get the original on the boundary. The deepest version of this statement — valid for differential forms on smooth manifolds of any dimension — is the Generalized Stokes Theorem. Everything in vector calculus is a shadow of that one truth.
