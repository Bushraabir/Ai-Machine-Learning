“Integration is the act of adding up infinitely many infinitely small things. On a surface, each infinitely small thing is a tiny parallelogram.”

§0. Prefatory Remarks

A surface integral is the generalization of a line integral to two dimensions: instead of integrating along a curve, we integrate over a surface. There are two fundamentally different kinds:

  • Surface integrals of scalar fields ∬S​f,dS — integrate a function over the surface, weighted by area
  • Surface integrals of vector fields ∬S​F⋅dS — integrate the flux of a vector field through the surface

The second type requires the surface to be oriented — it must have a consistent notion of “inside” and “outside,” or “up” and “down.” This orientation is the new conceptual ingredient.

These integrals are the backbone of:

  • Physics: Electric flux (Gauss’s law), magnetic flux, fluid flow, heat transfer
  • Differential geometry: Integration of differential forms on manifolds
  • Deep learning: Information geometry, Fisher information matrix as a surface integral
  • PDEs: Green’s theorem, divergence theorem, Stokes’ theorem

Everything in this note builds from the parametric surface machinery developed in Surfaces_of_Revolution and Tangent_Planes.

§1. Parametric Surfaces — Recollection and Setup

Recall from Tangent_Planes §4: a parametric surface is a map

r:D⊂R2⟶R3,r(u,v)=(x(u,v),;y(u,v),;z(u,v)),

where D is a domain in the uv-plane (usually a rectangle or region with piecewise smooth boundary).

The tangent vectors: ru​=∂u∂r​=(∂u∂x​,∂u∂y​,∂u∂z​),rv​=∂v∂r​=(∂v∂x​,∂v∂y​,∂v∂z​).

The surface element. At a point (u0​,v0​), the small patch of surface corresponding to [u0​,u0​+du]×[v0​,v0​+dv] is approximated by the parallelogram spanned by ru​,du and rv​,dv. Its area is:

dS=∣ru​×rv​∣;du,dv.

Definition 1.1 (Regular Surface). The parametrization r is regular at (u0​,v0​) if

ru​(u0​,v0​)×rv​(u0​,v0​)=0.

At regular points, the cross product gives a well-defined normal direction, and the tangent plane exists.

Standard Examples of Parametric Surfaces

Sphere x2+y2+z2=a2: r(ϕ,θ)=(asinϕcosθ,;asinϕsinθ,;acosϕ),ϕ∈[0,π],;θ∈[0,2π].

Graph z=f(x,y) over region D: r(x,y)=(x,;y,;f(x,y)),(x,y)∈D.

Cylinder x2+y2=R2, 0≤z≤h: r(θ,z)=(Rcosθ,;Rsinθ,;z),θ∈[0,2π],;z∈[0,h].

Surface of revolution (from Surfaces_of_Revolution): r(x,θ)=(x,;f(x)cosθ,;f(x)sinθ),x∈[a,b],;θ∈[0,2π].

§2. Surface Integrals of Scalar Fields

Definition 2.1 (Surface Integral of a Scalar Field). Let f:S⊂R3→R be continuous and r:D→S a regular parametrization. The surface integral of f over S is

∬S​f,dS=∬D​f(r(u,v)),∣ru​×rv​∣;du,dv.​

Motivation. Partition D into small rectangles Δui​×Δvj​. Each maps to a small surface patch of area ≈∣ru​×rv​∣,Δui​,Δvj​. The Riemann sum ∑i,j​f(r(ui​,vj​)),∣ru​×rv​∣,Δui​,Δvj​ converges to the integral in the limit.

Special case: f≡1. Then ∬S​1,dS=∬D​∣ru​×rv​∣,du,dv is the surface area — consistent with Surfaces_of_Revolution §3.

For a graph z=g(x,y): use r(x,y)=(x,y,g(x,y)).

rx​=(1,0,gx​),ry​=(0,1,gy​),rx​×ry​=(−gx​,−gy​,1).

∣rx​×ry​∣=1+gx2​+gy2​​.

So for a graph: ∬S​f,dS=∬D​f(x,y,g(x,y))1+gx2​+gy2​​;dx,dy.

Example 2.1 — Mass of a Surface

A thin metallic sheet has the shape of the hemisphere x2+y2+z2=1, z≥0, with surface density ρ(x,y,z)=z. Find its total mass.

Parametrize: r(ϕ,θ)=(sinϕcosθ,sinϕsinθ,cosϕ), ϕ∈[0,π/2], θ∈[0,2π].

Compute cross product: rϕ​=(cosϕcosθ,cosϕsinθ,−sinϕ),rθ​=(−sinϕsinθ,sinϕcosθ,0).

rϕ​×rθ​=(sin2ϕcosθ,;sin2ϕsinθ,;sinϕcosϕ).

∣rϕ​×rθ​∣=sinϕsin2ϕ+cos2ϕ​=sinϕ.

Integrate: z=cosϕ on the sphere, so

M=∬S​z,dS=∫02π​∫0π/2​cosϕ⋅sinϕ;dϕ,dθ=2π∫0π/2​sinϕcosϕ;dϕ=2π⋅21​=π.✓

Example 2.2 — Surface Integral over a Graph

Compute ∬S​(x2+y2),dS where S is the surface z=1+2x+3y over D=[0,1]×[0,1].

gx​=2,;gy​=3,1+4+9​=14​.

∬S​(x2+y2),dS=14​∫01​∫01​(x2+y2),dx,dy=14​⋅32​.

§3. Oriented Surfaces

This is where the new geometry enters. To integrate a vector field over a surface, we need to know which side is “positive” — i.e., which way the normal vector points.

Definition 3.1 (Orientation). An orientation of a smooth surface S is a continuous choice of unit normal vector n^(P) at each regular point P∈S.

A surface is orientable if such a continuous choice exists globally. Most familiar surfaces are orientable: spheres, planes, graphs z=f(x,y), cylinders (if we specify inner or outer). The famous exception is the Möbius band — it has only one side.

Two orientations always exist on an orientable connected surface: n^ and −n^. Choosing one is called orienting the surface.

Standard Conventions:

SurfacePositive normal
Closed surface (sphere, box)Outward-pointing
Graph z=f(x,y)Upward: n^ has positive z-component
Parametric surface$\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v}{

Reversing orientation: Swapping u↔v reverses the sign of ru​×rv​, hence reverses the orientation. Swapping the parameters is how you change which side is “positive.”

Definition 3.2 (Oriented Surface Element). Given orientation n^, define the vector area element:

dS=n^,dS=∣ru​×rv​∣ru​×rv​​⋅∣ru​×rv​∣;du,dv=(ru​×rv​);du,dv.

The vector dS encodes both the area element dS and the direction n^ simultaneously.

§4. Surface Integrals of Vector Fields (Flux Integrals)

Definition 4.1 (Flux Integral). Let F:R3→R3 be a continuous vector field and S an oriented surface with unit normal n^. The flux of F through S is

∬S​F⋅dS=∬S​(F⋅n^),dS.​

In terms of parametrization r(u,v) with the induced orientation n^=∣ru​×rv​∣ru​×rv​​:

∬S​F⋅dS=∬D​F(r(u,v))⋅(ru​×rv​);du,dv.

Physical interpretation. F⋅n^=∣F∣cosθ where θ is the angle between F and n^. This is the component of F through the surface (not tangent to it).

  • If F is a fluid velocity field: flux = net volume flow rate per unit time through S
  • If F is electric field: flux = total electric charge enclosed (Gauss’s law)
  • If F is magnetic field: flux = magnetic flux (Faraday’s law involves its time derivative)

For a graph z=g(x,y), n^ upward:

rx​×ry​=(−gx​,−gy​,1),dS=(−gx​,−gy​,1),dx,dy.

If F=(P,Q,R):

∬S​F⋅dS=∬D​(−Pgx​−Qgy​+R)​z=g(x,y)​;dx,dy.

§5. Computing the Cross Product Systematically

Given r(u,v), the key computational step is always ru​×rv​. Here is the systematic approach:

ru​×rv​=​i​j​k ∂u​x​∂u​y​∂u​z ∂v​x​∂v​y​∂v​z​​=​∂u​y​∂u​z ∂v​y​∂v​z​​i−​∂u​x​∂u​z ∂v​x​∂v​z​​j+​∂u​x​∂u​y ∂v​x​∂v​y​​k.

In shorthand using the Jacobian notation:

ru​×rv​=(∂(u,v)∂(y,z)​,;∂(u,v)∂(z,x)​,;∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​),

where ∂(u,v)∂(y,z)​=​yu​​yv​ zu​​zv​​​=yu​zv​−yv​zu​, etc.

This Jacobian notation reveals the deep structure: each component of ru​×rv​ is the area of the projected parallelogram onto one coordinate plane. More on this in §8.

§6. Worked Examples — Flux Integrals

Example 6.1 — Flux Through a Sphere

Let F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z) (radial field). Compute the flux outward through the sphere S:x2+y2+z2=a2.

Method 1 (geometric shortcut). On the sphere, n^=a(x,y,z)​ (outward unit normal). So

F⋅n^=ax2+y2+z2​=aa2​=a.

The integrand is constant! So

∬S​F⋅dS=a⋅Area(S)=a⋅4πa2=4πa3.

(Preview: by the Divergence Theorem, ∇⋅F=3, and ∭B​3,dV=3⋅34​πa3=4πa3. ✓)

Example 6.2 — Flux Through a Cylinder (with caps)

Let F=(x,y,0). Compute the outward flux through the closed cylinder x2+y2≤1, 0≤z≤1.

The surface has three pieces: lateral side S1​, top cap S2​ (z=1), bottom cap S3​ (z=0).

Lateral side S1​: r(θ,z)=(cosθ,sinθ,z). rθ​=(−sinθ,cosθ,0),rz​=(0,0,1). rθ​×rz​=(cosθ,sinθ,0)(outward, since x-component positive when θ∈[0,π/2]). F⋅(rθ​×rz​)=cos2θ+sin2θ=1. ∬S1​​F⋅dS=∫02π​∫01​1;dz,dθ=2π.

Top cap S2​ (z=1, upward normal n^=k): F⋅n^=(x,y,0)⋅(0,0,1)=0.∬S2​​=0.

Bottom cap S3​ (z=0, downward normal n^=−k): F⋅(−k)=0.∬S3​​=0.

Total flux: 2π+0+0=2π.

(Check via Divergence Theorem: ∇⋅F=2, volume =π. 2π⋅1=2π. ✓)

Example 6.3 — Flux Through a Graph

Let F=(0,0,z) and S:z=x2+y2, 0≤z≤1 (paraboloid), upward orientation.

r(x,y)=(x,y,x2+y2),D=x2+y2≤1. gx​=2x,gy​=2y,dS=(−2x,−2y,1),dx,dy. F⋅dS=(0,0,x2+y2)⋅(−2x,−2y,1)=x2+y2. ∬S​F⋅dS=∬D​(x2+y2),dx,dy=∫02π​∫01​r2⋅r;dr,dθ=2π⋅41​=2π​.

Example 6.4 — Flux Through the Torus (Conceptual)

The torus has outward normal pointing away from the central circle (see Surfaces_of_Revolution §4 for parameterization). For a constant field F=k, the flux through the torus is zero by symmetry: for every patch where F⋅n^>0, there is a symmetric patch where F⋅n^<0. This is an instance of the general principle: ∬S​F⋅dS=0 for any closed surface when F is constant and the surface is closed (by the Divergence Theorem, ∇⋅F=0).

§8. The Differential Form Perspective

The surface integral of a vector field has a beautiful rewriting. If F=(P,Q,R), then

F⋅dS=F⋅(ru​×rv​),du,dv=P,∂(u,v)∂(y,z)​+Q,∂(u,v)∂(z,x)​+R,∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​;du,dv.

This is the 2-form: ∬S​F⋅dS=∬S​P,dy∧dz+Q,dz∧dx+R,dx∧dy.

This notation is not mere decoration — it is the language of differential geometry and Stokes’ theorem on manifolds.

The exterior derivative of the 1-form ω=P,dx+Q,dy+R,dz gives a 2-form dω, and Stokes’ theorem says ∫∂Σ​ω=∬Σ​dω for any oriented 2-manifold Σ with boundary ∂Σ. This unifies:

Classical theoremDifferential forms
Fundamental theorem of calculus∫ab​f′,dx=f(b)−f(a)
Green’s theorem∮C​P,dx+Q,dy=∬D​(Qx​−Py​),dA
Stokes’ theorem∮C​F⋅dr=∬S​(∇×F)⋅dS
Divergence theorem∬​S​F⋅dS=∭V​(∇⋅F),dV

All are special cases of ∫∂M​ω=∫M​dω.

§9. Stokes’ Theorem and the Divergence Theorem — Statement

We state these without proof, as they deserve their own notes, but place them here for context.

Theorem 9.1 (Stokes’ Theorem). Let S be an oriented piecewise-smooth surface bounded by a simple closed curve ∂S, oriented consistently (right-hand rule). Let F be C1 on an open set containing S. Then:

∮∂S​F⋅dr=∬S​(∇×F)⋅dS.

Theorem 9.2 (Divergence Theorem / Gauss’s Theorem). Let E be a simple solid region with outward-oriented boundary surface ∂E=S. Let F be C1 on an open set containing E. Then:

∬​S​F⋅dS=∭E​(∇⋅F),dV.

Mnemonic. Both theorems say: the integral of a derivative over a domain equals the integral of the original function over the boundary. Stokes: 2D → 1D. Divergence: 3D → 2D.

§10. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

10.1 Fisher Information Matrix as a Surface Integral

The Fisher information matrix I(θ) for a statistical model p(x;θ) is

I(θ)ij​=Ex∼p(⋅;θ)​[∂θi​∂logp​∂θj​∂logp​].

In geometric terms, I(θ) is the Riemannian metric on the manifold of probability distributions (Fisher-Rao metric). For a continuous model with density p(x;θ) over a domain Ω⊂R2:

I(θ)ij​=∬Ω​∂θi​∂logp​∂θj​∂logp​,p(x;θ);dx1​,dx2​.

This is a surface integral of a scalar field — integrating ∂θi​∂logp​∂θj​∂logp​ weighted by the probability measure p,dA. Natural gradient descent (used in KFAC, Shampoo, and other second-order optimizers) replaces the Euclidean gradient with the Riemannian gradient I(θ)−1∇L, which is coordinate-invariant.

10.2 Flux and Information Flow in Neural Networks

Consider a neural network layer as a map z=σ(Wx). The Jacobian J=∂x∂z​ maps the input tangent space to the output tangent space. The singular values of J measure how much “information flux” passes through the layer.

For a 2D intermediate representation (e.g., a 2D convolutional feature map), the information flux through a surface S in feature space is:

Φ=∬S​J⋅dS,

where J is the Jacobian field. Vanishing gradients correspond to Φ→0 — the “flux” of gradient information through the network collapses. Exploding gradients correspond to Φ→∞.

10.3 Gauss’s Law and Implicit Neural Surfaces

Implicit neural representations (e.g., Neural Unsigned Distance Functions) represent surfaces as Fθ​(x)=0. Gauss’s law applied to the learned signed distance function Fθ​:

∬​∂V​∇Fθ​⋅dS=∭V​ΔFθ​;dV,

where Δ=∇2 is the Laplacian. For a true signed distance function, ∣∇F∣=1 everywhere (Eikonal equation), so ΔF= (mean curvature of level sets). Eikonal loss in training NeRF and NeuS models enforces this constraint:

Leikonal​=Ex​[(∣∇Fθ​(x)∣−1)2].

This is precisely requiring the flux of the gradient field through every surface to be controlled.

10.4 Normalizing Flows and Change of Variables on Surfaces

A normalizing flow T:Rn→Rn transforms a base density pZ​ to a target density pX​. The log-likelihood involves log∣detJT​∣. Geometrically:

log∣detJT​(z)∣=log∣volume element distortion at z∣.

For a 2D flow (e.g., a planar flow representing a 2D surface), ∣detJT​∣ is the ratio of area elements — exactly the ∣ru​×rv​∣ factor in our surface integral formula! Training a normalizing flow to maximize likelihood is equivalent to learning a parametrization of a surface in probability space that minimizes area distortion.

10.5 Stokes’ Theorem in Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs solve PDEs by minimizing residuals. For Maxwell’s equations (governing electromagnetic fields in physics simulators and neural hardware models):

∇×B=μ0​J+μ0​ϵ0​∂t∂E​.

Stokes’ theorem converts this: ∮C​B⋅dr=∬S​(∇×B)⋅dS. A PINN enforces this integral form as a loss:

LStokes​=​∮C​Bθ​⋅dr−∬S​(∇×Bθ​)⋅dS​2.

The surface integral directly appears in the training objective.

10.6 Orientation and Signed vs. Unsigned Surfaces in 3D Deep Learning

In 3D point cloud networks (PointNet, PointNet++, DGCNN), surface orientation matters. A mesh-based network must consistently orient faces to define outward normals — exactly the orientation problem of §3. Learning orientation from unoriented point clouds is an active research area (2023–2025), and fails precisely on non-orientable surfaces — a topological obstruction, not a learning failure.

Watertight meshes (closed, orientable, consistently oriented) allow the Divergence Theorem to compute volumes from surface integrals:

Vol=31​∬​S​r⋅dS=31​∬​S​(x,y,z)⋅n^,dS.

This formula is used in computational geometry and 3D printing quality checks.

The conceptual hierarchy:

1D∫ab​f,dx​​⟶line integral∫C​F⋅dr​​⟶scalar surface∬S​f,dS​​⟶flux / vector surface∬S​F⋅dS​​

Each arrow adds one dimension and one new idea: parameterization, area distortion, orientation. The flux integral is the culmination — it integrates a vector field’s interaction with an oriented surface, and it is the object that Gauss, Stokes, and the entire theory of differential forms is built around.
