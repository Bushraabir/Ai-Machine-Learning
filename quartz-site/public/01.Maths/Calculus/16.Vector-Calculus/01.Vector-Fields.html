1. Motivation — Why Vector Fields?

In single-variable calculus we asked: how does a single quantity change? In multivariable calculus, the natural next question is: how does a direction-and-magnitude quantity vary across space?

Physical phenomena that demand this language:

  • Wind velocity at each point in the atmosphere
  • Electric and magnetic force at each point in space
  • Fluid flow inside a pipe
  • The force exerted on a charged particle moving through a field

The moment you say “at every point (x,y,z) there is a force vector”, you are talking about a vector field. Almost every fundamental law in physics — Maxwell’s equations, Navier–Stokes, Newton’s law of gravity — is written in the language of vector fields.

2. Definition

Definition. Let D⊆Rn be an open set. A vector field on D is a function F:D→Rn that assigns to each point x∈D a vector F(x)∈Rn.

In R2 we write: F(x,y)=P(x,y),i+Q(x,y),j=⟨P(x,y),,Q(x,y)⟩

In R3: F(x,y,z)=P(x,y,z),i+Q(x,y,z),j+R(x,y,z),k

Component functions P,Q,R are scalar-valued; the vector field is the tuple of them.

Smoothness. We say F is of class Ck if each component function has continuous partial derivatives up to order k. Almost everything in this note assumes at least C1.

3. Examples Worth Memorising

Example 1 — Constant Field (trivial but important)

F(x,y)=⟨1,0⟩ Every arrow points in the +x direction with unit length. This is what a uniform horizontal wind looks like.

Example 2 — Radial (outward) Field

F(x,y)=⟨x,y⟩=x,i+y,j At each point (x,y), the vector points directly away from the origin and has magnitude x2+y2​ — proportional to distance.

Think: a source pumping fluid radially outward from the origin.

Example 3 — Rotating Field

F(x,y)=⟨−y,x⟩ At (1,0): F=⟨0,1⟩ (pointing up). At (0,1): F=⟨−1,0⟩ (pointing left). The vectors are tangent to circles centred at the origin — pure counterclockwise rotation.

Example 4 — Gradient Field (sneak preview)

f(x,y)=x2+y2⟹∇f=⟨2x,2y⟩ This is the same as Example 2 scaled by 2. The gradient always points in the direction of steepest ascent of f.

Example 5 — Gravitational Field

F(r)=−∣r∣3GM​,rr∈R3∖0 This points toward the origin (the mass), and its magnitude falls off as 1/r2 — the inverse-square law.

4. Visualisation — How to Draw One

The standard way to sketch F(x,y)=⟨P,Q⟩:

  1. Pick a grid of sample points (xi​,yj​).
  2. At each sample point, draw an arrow in the direction ⟨P(xi​,yj​),Q(xi​,yj​)⟩.
  3. Scale all arrows by the same factor so they don’t overlap (the relative lengths still convey magnitude).

ASCII sketch of F=⟨−y,x⟩ (counterclockwise rotation):

     ↑   ↑   ↑
← ←  +   +   +  → →
← ←  +   +   +  → →
     ↓   ↓   ↓

(Arrows form concentric circles; centre is a “vortex” or saddle point depending on the field.)

Key visual features to identify:

FeatureWhat to look for
SourceArrows diverging outward from a point
SinkArrows converging inward
VortexArrows circling a point
SaddleArrows diverging in some directions, converging in others
UniformAll arrows parallel and equal length

5. Operations on Vector Fields

5.1 The Gradient (∇f)

The gradient converts a scalar field into a vector field: ∇f=⟨∂x∂f​,,∂y∂f​,,∂z∂f​⟩

∇f(x0​) points in the direction of steepest increase of f at x0​, with magnitude equal to the rate of increase in that direction.

Theorem (Gradient and Level Curves). ∇f(x0​) is perpendicular to the level curve (or level surface) of f passing through x0​.

5.2 The Divergence (∇ · F)

The divergence converts a vector field into a scalar field: divF=∇⋅F=∂x∂P​+∂y∂Q​+∂z∂R​

Intuition. (∇⋅F)(x0​) measures how much the field is spreading out (acting as a source) or converging (acting as a sink) near x0​.

∇⋅F>0⟹source (fluid leaving a region) ∇⋅F<0⟹sink (fluid entering a region) ∇⋅F=0⟹incompressible / divergence-free

Example. For the radial field F=⟨x,y,z⟩ in R3: ∇⋅F=1+1+1=3 Constant positive divergence: fluid is being created at every point at a uniform rate.

5.3 The Curl (∇ × F)

The curl converts a vector field in R3 into another vector field: curlF=∇×F=​i​j​k ∂x​​∂y​​∂z​ P​Q​R​​

=⟨∂y∂R​−∂z∂Q​,;∂z∂P​−∂x∂R​,;∂x∂Q​−∂y∂P​⟩

Intuition. (∇×F)(x0​) measures the tendency of the field to rotate near x0​. The direction of the curl vector gives the axis of rotation (via the right-hand rule); the magnitude gives the angular speed of rotation.

Example. For the rotating field F=⟨−y,x,0⟩: ∇×F=⟨0,,0,,∂x∂x​−∂y∂(−y)​⟩=⟨0,0,1+1⟩=⟨0,0,2⟩ Curl points in +z direction — counterclockwise rotation when viewed from above. Magnitude 2 gives the angular velocity.

In 2D, the curl reduces to the scalar quantity ∂x∂Q​−∂y∂P​, sometimes called the scalar curl or 2D curl.

6. Conservative Vector Fields

Definition. F is conservative on D if there exists a scalar function f:D→R such that F=∇f. The function f is called a potential function for F.

This is the vector field analogue of a function being an exact derivative.

Theorem (Curl Test). If F is C1 on a simply connected domain D⊆R3, then: F is conservative on D⟺∇×F=0 on D

Theorem (Path Independence). F is conservative ⟺ ∫C​F⋅dr is path-independent (depends only on endpoints).

Theorem (Closed Curves). F is conservative ⟺ ∮C​F⋅dr=0 for every closed curve C in D.

To find the potential function f given conservative F=⟨P,Q⟩:

  1. Integrate P with respect to x: f(x,y)=∫P,dx+g(y).
  2. Differentiate with respect to y and set equal to Q.
  3. Solve for g(y).

Example. F=⟨2xy,,x2+1⟩. Check: ∂y​(2xy)=2x, ∂x​(x2+1)=2x. ✓ Curl-free, so conservative. f=∫2xy,dx=x2y+g(y). ∂y​f=x2+g′(y)=x2+1⟹g′(y)=1⟹g(y)=y. Potential: f(x,y)=x2y+y.

7. Line Integrals Over Vector Fields

Given a curve C parametrised by r(t), t∈[a,b], and a vector field F:

∫C​F⋅dr=∫ab​F(r(t))⋅r′(t),dt

Physical meaning. This is the work done by F on a particle travelling along C.

Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals. If F=∇f on an open set containing C from A to B: ∫C​F⋅dr=f(B)−f(A) No need to parametrise the curve at all — only the endpoints matter.

Example. F=⟨2xy,x2+1⟩, f=x2y+y. Compute the line integral from (0,0) to (1,2) along any path: ∫C​F⋅dr=f(1,2)−f(0,0)=(1⋅2+2)−0=4

8. Key Theorems (Big Picture)

These theorems are the profound culmination of the theory — each generalises the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to higher dimensions.

TheoremStatementDimension
Green’s Theorem∮∂D​F⋅dr=∬D​(∂x∂Q​−∂y∂P​)dA2D
Stokes’ Theorem∮∂S​F⋅dr=∬S​(∇×F)⋅dS3D, surface/curve
Divergence Theorem∬​∂V​F⋅dS=∭V​(∇⋅F),dV3D, volume/surface

The pattern: integral of a derivative over a region = boundary integral of the original field. These are all special cases of the Generalised Stokes’ Theorem in differential geometry.

9. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

This is where vector fields are not just beautiful mathematics but working engineering.

9.1 The Loss Landscape is a Scalar Field

In training a neural network with parameters θ∈Rn (where n might be billions), the loss function

L:Rn→R

is a scalar field over parameter space. This is exactly the f you would compute the gradient of.

9.2 The Gradient is the Key Vector Field

∇θ​,L(θ)=⟨∂θ1​∂L​,∂θ2​∂L​,…,∂θn​∂L​⟩

This is the gradient vector field of the loss, evaluated at the current parameter point θ. Every iteration of training, you are:

  1. Evaluating this vector field at the current point.
  2. Taking a step in the negative gradient direction (steepest descent).

θt+1​=θt​−η,∇θ​L(θt​)

Gradient descent is literally following a vector field.

9.3 Divergence and the Continuity of Probability Flow

In normalising flows and score-based generative models (e.g. diffusion models like DDPM, Stable Diffusion), the probability distribution p(x) evolves according to a Fokker-Planck equation, which involves the divergence:

∂t∂p​=−∇⋅(p,v)

This is the continuity equation — conservation of probability mass. The divergence of the probability flux p,v governs how the distribution spreads or concentrates over time.

9.4 Score Functions as Vector Fields

The score function used in score-based generative models is: s(x)=∇x​logp(x)

This is a vector field over data space Rn that points in the direction of increasing probability density. Sampling algorithms (Langevin dynamics) follow this vector field, plus noise, to draw samples from p.

The entire idea of diffusion models is: learn the score vector field from data, then integrate it backwards to generate new samples.

9.5 Conservative Fields and Energy Functions

Hopfield networks and energy-based models explicitly construct a scalar energy E(x) and update rules that follow −∇E. The vector field −∇E is conservative by construction (it has a potential function E). This guarantees the dynamics converge to a local minimum — they cannot oscillate indefinitely.

Boltzmann Machines and RBMs are energy-based models where the equilibrium distribution is p∝e−E(x).

9.6 Curl in Neural ODE and Physics-Informed Networks

In Neural ODEs, the hidden state h(t) evolves as: dtdh​=Fθ​(h(t),t)

where Fθ​ is a learned vector field (parametrised by a neural network). The dynamics of the hidden state are literally integration along trajectories of a vector field.

Hamiltonian Neural Networks impose the constraint ∇×F=0 (or its Hamiltonian equivalent) to ensure the learned dynamics conserve energy — a hard inductive bias from physics.
