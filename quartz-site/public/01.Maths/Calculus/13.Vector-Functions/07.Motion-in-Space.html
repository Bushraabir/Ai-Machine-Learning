“Newton saw the planets and asked: what function r(t) produces an ellipse under the force law F=−GMm/r2,r^? The answer required him to invent calculus. We now have that calculus — let us use it properly.”

0. Setup and Philosophy

A particle moving through R3 is described by a smooth vector-valued function:

r(t)=⟨x(t),,y(t),,z(t)⟩,t∈I⊆R

Everything — velocity, acceleration, speed, arc length, force — is derived from this single function by differentiation. The miracle of vector calculus is that all of Newton’s mechanics packages cleanly into vector equations, and the geometry of the Frenet–Serret frame (see Normal and Binormal Vectors) tells us why acceleration splits the way it does.

Convention throughout: t is time. Prime ′ denotes d/dt. We assume r(t) is at least twice continuously differentiable.

1. Velocity

Definition

The velocity vector is the derivative of the position vector:

v(t)=r′(t)=⟨x′(t),,y′(t),,z′(t)⟩​

It is the instantaneous rate of change of position — both direction and magnitude carry meaning:

  • Direction of v(t): Tangent to the curve at r(t) — the direction of motion.
  • Magnitude ∣v(t)∣: The speed at time t.

Speed

v(t):=∣v(t)∣=[x′(t)]2+[y′(t)]2+[z′(t)]2​

Speed is a scalar; velocity is a vector. This distinction is not pedantry — it is the difference between “60 km/h” and “60 km/h due north.”

Relation to the Unit Tangent Vector

Recall T(t)=r′(t)/∣r′(t)∣. Therefore:

v(t)=∣v(t)∣,T(t)=v(t),T(t)

Velocity is speed times the unit tangent. This is the canonical decomposition of velocity — speed tells you how fast, T tells you which way.

Arc Length and Speed

Arc length satisfies dtds​=∣r′(t)∣=v(t). So:

s(t)=∫t0​t​∣r′(u)∣,du=∫t0​t​v(u),du

Speed is the rate of change of arc length. This is not a coincidence — it is the definition.

2. Acceleration

Definition

The acceleration vector is the derivative of velocity:

a(t)=v′(t)=r′′(t)=⟨x′′(t),,y′′(t),,z′′(t)⟩​

Unlike velocity, acceleration does not generally point along T. It can point in any direction — and decomposing it into the Frenet frame is the central result of this section.

Newton’s Second Law

For a particle of mass m:

F(t)=m,a(t)=m,r′′(t)

The vector equation encodes three scalar ODEs simultaneously. Knowing F as a function of r,v,t gives a second-order ODE system — the equation of motion. The trajectory r(t) is its solution.

3. The Tangential and Normal Components of Acceleration

This is the theorem that makes everything click. Acceleration splits into exactly two parts: one that changes your speed, and one that changes your direction.

Derivation

Start from v=v,T. Differentiate using the product rule:

a=v′=v′,T+v,T′

Now use the first Frenet–Serret formula: T′=dsdT​dtds​=κ,N⋅v. So:

a=v′,T+κv2,N

Define:

aT​=v′=dtdv​,aN​=κv2​

Then:

a=aT​,T+aN​,N​

This is exact — no approximation. Acceleration has zero component along B.

Geometric Interpretation

ComponentFormulaPhysical Meaning
aT​,Tv′,TChange in speed (speeding up or slowing down)
aN​,Nκv2,NChange in direction (centripetal, toward center of curvature)

Key observations:

  • If you drive at constant speed on a curved road: aT​=0, but aN​=κv2=0. You are accelerating — your direction changes.
  • If you drive in a straight line and speed up: aN​=0 (since κ=0), only aT​=0.
  • Acceleration is never along B. Torsion (twisting) does not produce force directly — it produces a change in the osculating plane.

Why aN​=κv2?

Write it as aN​=v2/ρ where ρ=1/κ is the radius of curvature. This is the familiar centripetal acceleration formula a=v2/r from circular motion — now generalized to any curve, using the instantaneous radius of the osculating circle. Beautiful.

4. Computing aT​ and aN​ Without the Frenet Frame

In practice you rarely know κ and N explicitly. Here are computational formulas:

aT​=∣v∣v⋅a​=∣r′∣r′⋅r′′​

aN​=∣v∣∣v×a∣​=∣r′∣∣r′×r′′∣​

Proof of aT​: Project a=aT​T+aN​N onto T: a⋅T=aT​(T⋅T)+aN​(N⋅T)=aT​

Since v=vT, we get a⋅v=aT​v, hence aT​=a⋅v/v.

Proof of aN​: Compute v×a: v×a=vT×(aT​T+aN​N)=v,aT​(T×T)+v,aN​(T×N)=v,aN​,B

So ∣v×a∣=v,aN​ (since ∣B∣=1), giving aN​=∣v×a∣/v.

Pythagoras check:

∣a∣2=aT2​+aN2​

since T⊥N. This gives a useful consistency check.

5. Worked Examples

Example 1: Circular Motion at Constant Speed

r(t)=(Rcosωt,,Rsinωt,,0)

where R>0 is the radius and ω>0 is the angular frequency.

Velocity: v(t)=(−Rωsinωt,,Rωcosωt,,0),v=∣v∣=Rω

Constant speed — no speeding up or slowing down.

Acceleration: a(t)=(−Rω2cosωt,,−Rω2sinωt,,0)=−ω2(Rcosωt,,Rsinωt,,0)=−ω2,r(t)

The acceleration points toward the origin — centripetal. Its magnitude: ∣a∣=Rω2.

Decomposition: aT​=dtdv​=0✓aN​=κv2=R1​(Rω)2=Rω2✓

The centripetal force required: F=m,aN​=mRω2=mv2/R.

Example 2: Projectile Motion

A ball launched from the origin with initial velocity v0​=⟨v0​cosθ,,v0​sinθ,,0⟩, under gravity g=⟨0,−g,0⟩:

a(t)=⟨0,,−g,,0⟩

Integrate once (velocity): v(t)=v0​+gt=⟨v0​cosθ,;v0​sinθ−gt,;0⟩

Integrate again (position): r(t)=v0​t+21​gt2=⟨v0​cosθ⋅t,;v0​sinθ⋅t−21​gt2,;0⟩

Speed at time t: v(t)=v02​cos2θ+(v0​sinθ−gt)2​

Tangential acceleration (rate of change of speed): aT​=dtdv​=v(t)(v0​sinθ−gt)(−g)​=v−g(vy​)​

At the apex (vy​=0): aT​=0 — speed is momentarily neither increasing nor decreasing. But aN​=g — the ball is still curving.

Normal acceleration at apex: aN​=∣a∣2−aT2​​=g2−0​=g

At the apex, the full gravitational acceleration is centripetal — exactly as expected from the geometry.

Example 3: Helix with Increasing Speed

r(t)=(cost2,,sint2,,t)

Velocity: v(t)=(−2tsint2,,2tcost2,,1) v=∣v∣=4t2+1​

Acceleration: a(t)=(−2sint2−4t2cost2,;2cost2−4t2sint2,;0)

aT​: aT​=vv⋅a​=4t2+1​(−2tsint2)(−2sint2−4t2cost2)+(2tcost2)(2cost2−4t2sint2)​

Expanding the numerator: =4t2+1​4tsin2t2+8t3sint2cost2+4tcos2t2−8t3cost2sint2​=4t2+1​4t​

Check: v=4t2+1​, so v′=4t/4t2+1​=aT​. ✓

The speed is increasing (for t>0) while the particle also curves — both aT​ and aN​ are nonzero.

Speed vs. Curvature Trade-off

High speed, low curvature:        Low speed, high curvature:
  a_N = κv² → large               a_N = κv² → moderate

  ─────────────────────            ╭──────────────╮
  (fast, gently curving road)      (slow, sharp turn)

Both can produce the same |a_N| — the driver feels the same lateral force.
This is why speed limits decrease at sharp curves.

Phase Diagram: Speeding Up vs. Slowing Down

  a_T > 0  →  speeding up   (a has component in direction of motion)
  a_T < 0  →  slowing down  (a opposes direction of motion)
  a_T = 0  →  constant speed (a ⊥ T, purely centripetal)

  Example — car on a mountain road:
  Ascending curve:   a_T < 0 (engine fights gravity), a_N ≠ 0 (turning)
  Descending curve:  a_T > 0 (gravity aids motion), a_N ≠ 0 (turning)
  At apex (flat):    a_T ≈ 0 momentarily, a_N from curvature

7. Integration: Finding Position from Acceleration

Given a(t) and initial conditions r(t0​)=r0​, v(t0​)=v0​:

v(t)=v0​+∫t0​t​a(u),du

r(t)=r0​+∫t0​t​v(u),du

Integration is componentwise: each equation is three scalar ODEs integrated independently.

Key point: The initial conditions (r0​,v0​) — six scalar values — completely determine the trajectory when a is given. This is the vector form of the initial value problem, and uniqueness follows from Picard–Lindelöf applied componentwise.

8. Kepler’s First Law (A Taste of What the Formalism Buys)

Consider a planet of mass m orbiting a star of mass M at the origin. Newton’s law gives:

r′′=−∣r∣3GM​,r

Claim: The angular momentum L=m(r×v) is conserved.

Proof: dtdL​=m(r′×v+r×v′)=m(v×v+r×a)=m(0+r×(−∣r∣3GM​r))=0

since r×r=0. Therefore L= const., which means the motion is planar (confined to the plane perpendicular to L). This is the first step in proving the orbit is a conic section — and it falls out of a two-line vector calculation.

This illustrates the power of the vector formalism: what would require pages of component algebra becomes transparent.

9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The concepts of velocity and acceleration in vector calculus map directly onto some of the most important algorithms and ideas in modern machine learning.

9.1 Gradient Descent as Motion in Parameter Space

Training a neural network by gradient descent defines a trajectory θ(t) in parameter space Rp (where p can be billions). The analog of Newton’s equations:

PhysicsML Optimization
Position r(t)Parameters θ(t)
Velocity v(t)=r′Rate of parameter change θ′
Force / AccelerationNegative gradient −∇θ​L
MassLearning rate η (inverse analog)

Vanilla gradient descent is literally Euler’s method for the ODE: dtdθ​=−∇θ​L(θ)

The “velocity” in parameter space is −∇θ​L — you move in the direction of steepest descent.

9.2 Momentum Methods — Literally Acceleration

SGD with momentum (Polyak, 1964) introduces an auxiliary “velocity” variable:

mt+1​=β,mt​−η,∇θ​L(θt​) θt+1​=θt​+mt+1​

This is a discrete heavy-ball ODE: θ′′+(1−β)θ′=−η,∇θ​L

where θ′′ is the “acceleration” in parameter space. The momentum term βmt​ is literally carrying forward the previous velocity — inertia. This is why momentum helps:

  • In a long ravine (loss landscape), the tangential component aT​ accumulates — you speed up in the ravine direction.
  • Noisy gradients partially cancel in the normal component aN​ — you don’t oscillate wildly across the ravine.

Nesterov accelerated gradient looks ahead before computing the gradient — equivalent to a better ODE discretization with faster convergence. Sutskever et al. (2013) showed this achieves the optimal O(1/t2) convergence rate for convex functions.

9.3 Adam and Adaptive Curvature

Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014) maintains:

  • mt​: exponential moving average of gradients (≈ velocity)
  • vt​: exponential moving average of squared gradients (≈ estimate of local curvature / aN​)

The update normalizes the velocity by the estimated curvature: θt+1​=θt​−η,v^t​​+ϵm^t​​

This is an adaptive normal acceleration correction — in directions of high curvature (oscillation), the effective step is reduced; in directions of low curvature (flat), it is increased. Adam is essentially adapting aN​ to the local geometry.

9.4 Neural ODEs

Neural ODEs (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2018) explicitly model the hidden state as a particle trajectory:

dtdh(t)​=fθ​(h(t),t)

Here fθ​ is a neural network that defines the velocity field — the analog of v(t). The forward pass is an ODE solve, not a sequence of layer applications. The backward pass uses the adjoint method — a continuous analog of backpropagation derived from the calculus of variations.

The acceleration h′′(t)=∂h∂fθ​​fθ​+∂t∂fθ​​ encodes how the hidden state changes direction in representation space — directly analogous to a=aT​T+aN​N.

9.5 Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs solve PDEs by embedding the equation of motion into the loss. For instance, learning the trajectory of a charged particle:

r′′=mq​(E+r′×B)

The loss penalizes violation of this ODE: L=​rθ′′​(t)−mq​(E(t)+rθ′​(t)×B(t))​2

where rθ​(t) is a neural network approximating the trajectory. The network must learn both the velocity and acceleration components correctly — aT​ (along the trajectory) and aN​ (centripetal, from the magnetic force qv×B, which is always perpendicular to v).

9.6 Robotics and Reinforcement Learning

In model-based RL, the agent learns a dynamics model: st+1​=f(st​,at​)

which is a discrete version of r′′=F/m. For robotic arm control:

  • Joint velocities v are the control inputs.
  • Joint accelerations a are constrained (actuator torque limits).
  • The tangential component aT​ changes the end-effector speed; the normal component aN​ changes its direction.

Trajectory optimization explicitly minimizes ∫0T​∣a(t)∣2,dt subject to dynamics constraints — minimizing the total acceleration effort, decomposed into aT2​+aN2​.

9.7 Attention and Transformer Dynamics

Recent theoretical work models the forward pass of a transformer as a continuous dynamical system (an ODE in layer depth). The residual stream update at each layer is:

xl+1​=xl​+Fl​(xl​)

as l→∞, this converges to x′=F(x) — a velocity field on the embedding manifold. The acceleration x′′ captures how attention heads curve the representation trajectory through token space. This framework (Continuous Depth Networks) enables analysis of representation stability using tools directly from this section.

10. Summary

Starting from position r(t), differentiation gives:

r(t);d/dt;​v(t);d/dt;​a(t)

The master decomposition of acceleration:

a=tangentialv′,T​​+normalκv2,N​​

with computational formulas:

aT​=∣v∣v⋅a​,aN​=∣v∣∣v×a∣​,∣a∣2=aT2​+aN2​

The physical content:

  • aT​ is what your speedometer feels — rate of speed change.
  • aN​ is what presses you into your seat on a turn — centripetal, always toward center of curvature.
  • No acceleration ever has a binormal component — torsion is invisible to Newton’s F=ma in 3D.

In machine learning, this framework reappears as: momentum in gradient descent (inertia in parameter space), adaptive curvature correction (Adam), continuous dynamics of neural networks (Neural ODEs), and constrained trajectory optimization in robotics.
