02.Space Curves

02.Space Curves

Apr 23, 202616 min read

“A curve is not a set of points — it is a map. The same set of points can be traversed slowly, quickly, backwards, or repeatedly. The parametrization carries information the image does not.” — personal note, Stewart §13.1–13.4, MIT 18.02

0. Motivation

In the previous note (Vector Functions) we defined r:D→R3 and noted that the tip of r(t) sweeps a space curve. Now we take that curve seriously as a geometric object in its own right.

The central questions are:

  1. How long is the curve? → Arc length
  2. How fast is it turning? → Curvature κ
  3. In which direction is it turning? → Principal normal N
  4. Is it twisting out of a plane? → Torsion τ and binormal B
  5. What is the best local coordinate frame? → Frenet–Serret frame T,N,B

These are the tools of differential geometry of curves, and they reappear — sometimes verbatim, sometimes in spirit — in the geometry of loss landscapes, attention mechanisms, and manifold learning in deep learning.

1. Space Curves — Formal Definition

Definition 1.1 (Parametric Curve). A parametric curve in R3 is a continuous map r:[a,b]→R3,r(t)=⟨f(t),g(t),h(t)⟩

Definition 1.2 (Space Curve / Image). The space curve C is the image: C=r(t):t∈[a,b]⊂R3

Definition 1.3 (Smooth Curve). r is smooth on (a,b) if r′(t) exists and r′(t)=0 for all t∈(a,b).

Why r′(t)=0 matters. At a point where r′(t0​)=0, the curve has no well-defined tangent direction — these are singular points or cusps. Requiring r′=0 ensures the curve is locally “non-degenerate,” which is necessary for curvature and torsion to be defined.

Example 1.4 (a cusp). r(t)=⟨t2,t3⟩ has r′(0)=⟨0,0⟩. At t=0 the curve has a cusp — it comes to a point and reverses direction. This is not a smooth point.

        |      /
        |     / ← curve approaching cusp from right
        |    *  ← cusp at origin (r'(0) = 0)
        |     \
        |      \ ← curve leaving cusp downward

2. Reparametrization

Definition 2.1. A reparametrization of r(t) is a composition r(ϕ(s)) where ϕ:[c,d]→[a,b] is a smooth bijection with ϕ′(s)=0.

  • If ϕ′>0: orientation-preserving — same traversal direction.
  • If ϕ′<0: orientation-reversing — traversed backwards.

Key insight. The geometry of the curve (lengths, angles, curvature) must be independent of parametrization. Quantities that depend on the parametrization (e.g., speed ∣r′(t)∣) are extrinsic to the curve but intrinsic to the motion.

The canonical reparametrization is by arc length s, because it gives the curve a “built-in ruler” independent of any particular traversal speed.

3. Arc Length (Revisited Rigorously)

3.1 Definition via Riemann Sums

Partition [a,b] into n subintervals: a=t0​<t1​<⋯<tn​=b.

The polygonal approximation has total length: Ln​=∑i=1n​∣r(ti​)−r(ti−1​)∣

Definition 3.1 (Arc Length). The arc length of C is: L=limn→∞​Ln​=∫ab​∣r′(t)∣,dt

provided this limit exists (which it does when r′ is continuous).

Proof of the integral formula. By the Mean Value Theorem applied component-wise: |\mathbf{r}(t_i) - \mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})| = |\mathbf{r}'(t_i^_)|,\Delta t_i + O((\Delta t_i)^2) for some t_i^_ \in (t_{i-1}, t_i). Summing and taking the limit gives the Riemann integral. ■

3.2 The Arc Length Function

s(t)=∫at​∣r′(u)∣,du​

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: dtds​=∣r′(t)∣≥0

This says: speed = rate of arc length accumulation. If ∣r′(t)∣=1 for all t (unit-speed parametrization), then s(t)=t−a.

3.3 Reparametrization by Arc Length

Since dtds​=∣r′(t)∣>0 (smooth curve), s(t) is strictly increasing, hence invertible. Write t=t(s). Then:

r~(s)=r(t(s))

has speed ∣r~′(s)∣=∣r′(t)∣⋅∣t′(s)∣=∣r′(t)∣⋅∣r′(t)∣1​=1.

r~(s) is a unit-speed curve. This is the most natural parametrization — the parameter s is literally distance along the curve.

Example 3.4 (helix reparametrized). r(t)=⟨cost,sint,t⟩, ∣r′(t)∣=2​, so s=2​,t, i.e., t=s/2​:

r~(s)=⟨cos2​s​, sin2​s​, 2​s​⟩

Now s measures true distance along the helix.

4. The Frenet–Serret Frame

This is the heart of the geometry. At each smooth point of a curve we build a canonical orthonormal basis adapted to the curve’s local shape.

4.1 Unit Tangent Vector T

T(t)=∣r′(t)∣r′(t)​​

  • Points in the direction of motion.
  • ∣T∣=1 always.
  • By Lemma 6.3 from Vector Functions: T⋅T′=0.

4.2 Curvature κ

Idea. Curvature measures how quickly the tangent direction is turning per unit arc length. It is a property of the curve, not the parametrization.

Definition 4.1 (Curvature). κ=​dsdT​​

Since dT/ds=T′(t)/∣r′(t)∣ (chain rule), we have:

κ(t)=∣r′(t)∣∣T′(t)∣​​

Computational formula (no need to find T explicitly):

κ=∣r′(t)∣3∣r′(t)×r′′(t)∣​​

Proof. Let v=∣r′∣. Then r′=vT. Differentiate: r′′=v′T+vT′ Take cross product with r′=vT: r′×r′′=vT×(v′T+vT′)=v2(T×T′) (since T×T=0). Then ∣r′×r′′∣=v2∣T×T′∣=v2∣T′∣ (since T⊥T′ and ∣T∣=1, so ∣T×T′∣=∣T′∣). Thus: κ=v∣T′∣​=v3∣r′×r′′∣​■

Curvature of a plane curve y=f(x) (Stewart §13.3): κ=(1+[f′(x)]2)3/2∣f′′(x)∣​

Special cases:

  • Straight line: κ=0 (tangent never turns).
  • Circle of radius R: κ=1/R (constant; smaller circles curve more).
  • Radius of curvature: ρ=1/κ — the radius of the osculating circle (the circle that best fits the curve at that point).
Large κ (tight curve):         Small κ (gentle curve):

      *                              *
    * * *                         *     *
   *  |  *                       *       *
      |                         *         *
   osculating circle is small   osculating circle is large
   κ = 1/R, small R → large κ

4.3 Principal Normal Vector N

Since T⊥T′, the vector T′(t) is perpendicular to T(t). Normalize it:

N(t)=∣T′(t)∣T′(t)​​

  • N points toward the center of curvature (the center of the osculating circle).
  • T⊥N always.
  • For a curve turning left in the xy-plane, N points left.

Intrinsic relation: dsdT​=κ,N

This is the first Frenet–Serret formula.

4.4 Binormal Vector B

B(t)=T(t)×N(t)​

  • ∣B∣=1 (since T⊥N, ∣T∣=∣N∣=1).
  • B⊥T, B⊥N.
  • T,N,B is a right-handed orthonormal frame at every point.

Geometric meaning. The osculating plane at a point is spanned by T and N. B is the normal to this plane — it points “out of the plane of bending.”

4.5 Torsion τ

Curvature measures bending; torsion measures twisting.

Definition 4.2 (Torsion). τ=−dsdB​⋅N

Computational formula: τ=∣r′×r′′∣2(r′×r′′)⋅r′′′​​

Geometric meaning:

  • τ=0: the curve lies in a plane (a plane curve).
  • τ=0: the curve spirals out of every plane — it truly lives in 3D.
  • The sign of τ indicates the direction of twisting (right-handed or left-handed helix).

5. The Frenet–Serret Formulas

This is the fundamental theorem of space curve theory.

Theorem 5.1 (Frenet–Serret). For a smooth unit-speed curve with κ>0: dsdT​=κ,N​ dsdN​=−κ,T+τ,B​ dsdB​=−τ,N​

In matrix form: dsd​​TmathbfNmathbfB​​=(0​κ​0 −κ​0​τ 0​−τ​0​)​TmathbfNmathbfB​​

The matrix is skew-symmetric — this encodes that T,N,B is an orthonormal frame rotating as we move along the curve. The evolution of the frame is governed by the Darboux vector ω=τT+κB, so T′=ω×T, N′=ω×N, B′=ω×B.

Proof of dT/ds=κN. By definition of κ and N: dsdT​=​dsdT​​⋅∣dT/ds∣dT/ds​=κ,N■

Proof of dB/ds=−τN. Since B=T×N: dsdB​=dsdT​×N+T×dsdN​=κN×N+T×dsdN​=T×dsdN​ Since ∣B∣=1, we have dB/ds⊥B. Also dB/ds⊥T (shown by differentiating T⋅B=0). Hence dB/ds∥N, so dB/ds=−τN for some scalar τ. ■

The Fundamental Theorem of Curve Theory. Given smooth functions κ(s)>0 and τ(s), there exists a unique (up to rigid motion) smooth curve in R3 with those curvature and torsion. κ and τ are the complete invariants of the curve.

6. Worked Examples

6.1 The Circular Helix — Full Analysis

r(t)=⟨acost, asint, bt⟩,a>0,b=0

Derivatives: r′(t)=⟨−asint, acost, b⟩,∣r′∣=a2+b2​=:c r′′(t)=⟨−acost, −asint, 0⟩ r′′′(t)=⟨asint, −acost, 0⟩

Unit tangent: T(t)=c1​⟨−asint,acost,b⟩

Curvature: r′×r′′=⟨absint, −abcost, a2⟩ ∣r′×r′′∣=a2b2+a4​=ab2+a2​=ac κ=c3ac​=c2a​=a2+b2a​​

Constant! The helix has constant curvature.

Torsion: (r′×r′′)⋅r′′′=⟨absint,−abcost,a2⟩⋅⟨asint,−acost,0⟩ =a2bsin2t+a2bcos2t=a2b τ=(ac)2a2b​=a2+b2b​​

Constant! The helix has constant torsion.

Interpretation:

  • If b>0: right-handed helix (τ>0), rises counterclockwise.
  • If b<0: left-handed helix (τ<0).
  • If b=0: reduces to a circle, κ=1/a, τ=0.
  • If a=0: reduces to a straight line, κ=0.

The helix is characterized (up to rigid motion) by having constant κ and τ.

r(t)=⟨t,t2,0⟩

This lies in the z=0 plane, so τ=0 (verify with the formula as an exercise).

7. Normal and Osculating Planes

Definition 7.1 (Osculating Plane). The osculating plane at r(t0​) is the plane spanned by T(t0​) and N(t0​), passing through r(t0​). Normal to this plane: B(t0​).

Equation: B(t0​)⋅(x−r(t0​))=0.

Definition 7.2 (Normal Plane). The normal plane at r(t0​) is perpendicular to T(t0​), containing N and B.

Equation: T(t0​)⋅(x−r(t0​))=0.

Definition 7.3 (Rectifying Plane). Spanned by T and B; contains the tangent line and the binormal.

8. Motion in Space — Physics Interpretation

If r(t) is the position of a particle:

velocity: v(t)=r′(t)=v,T(t),v=∣v∣=∣r′∣

acceleration: a(t)=r′′(t)=v′T+vT′=v′T+vdsdT​dtds​=v′T+κv2,N

a=tangentialv′​​T+centripetalκv2​​N​

The acceleration decomposes into:

  • Tangential component aT​=v′=dt2d2s​: changes the speed.
  • Normal component aN​=κv2: changes the direction (centripetal — always toward the center of curvature).

Component formulas (no need to find T and N): aT​=∣r′∣r′⋅r′′​,aN​=∣r′∣∣r′×r′′∣​

Verify: aN​=κv2=∣r′∣3∣r′×r′′∣​⋅∣r′∣2=∣r′∣∣r′×r′′∣​. ✓

Example 8.1. For the helix r(t)=⟨cost,sint,t⟩: v=∣r′∣=2​,v′=0(constant speed) a(t)=⟨−cost,−sint,0⟩ aT​=0,aN​=κv2=21​⋅2=1

The particle moves at constant speed on the helix — all acceleration is centripetal.

9.2 The Frenet Frame Rolling Along a Curve

Imagine a tiny coordinate system {T, N, B} that rides along the curve:

  position 1:              position 2:             position 3:
    B↑                       B↑                      B ↑(tilted — torsion!)
    |  N←                    |   N←                  |    N←
    | /                      |  /                    |   /
    |/ T→                    | / T→ (rotated)        |  / T→
    *                        *                       *
    
As κ increases, T-N plane rotates (bending).
As τ increases, B tilts — the osculating plane rotates (twisting).

10. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

10.1 Loss Landscape as a Surface; Optimizer Trajectory as a Space Curve

The training trajectory θ(t)∈Rp is a space curve in parameter space (very high-dimensional, but the geometry applies). The curvature κ of this trajectory tells us:

  • High κ: the optimizer is turning sharply — it has found a direction of high curvature in the loss landscape, indicating a saddle point or sharp minimum.
  • Low κ: the optimizer is moving in a nearly straight line — the gradient is consistent, loss is decreasing steadily.

The Hessian H=∇2L encodes the curvature of the loss surface, and second-order optimizers (Newton’s method, natural gradient) use this curvature to correct the trajectory — exactly as the Frenet frame uses κ and τ to describe the curve’s geometry.

10.2 Frenet–Serret in Sequence Models

In a sequence model (RNN, Transformer), the hidden state h(t) traces a curve in Rd. The Frenet frame T,N,B along this trajectory gives:

  • T(t): the direction of change in the hidden state — what the model is “attending to” at step t.
  • κ(t): how sharply the model is changing direction in state space — high curvature at semantic boundaries (e.g., transitions between topics in text).
  • τ(t): how much the model’s plane of attention is rotating — high torsion may indicate complex, multi-dimensional context shifts.

This perspective is exploited in geometric deep learning (Bronstein et al., 2021) and in interpretability research that studies the geometry of activation trajectories.

10.3 Arc Length as Complexity / Path Length Regularization

In ML, the path length of the optimizer trajectory: L=∫0T​∣θ′(t)∣,dt measures the total distance traveled in parameter space. Flat minima (low sharpness) correspond to regions where the trajectory length is short before convergence. Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) explicitly penalizes trajectories that travel into high-curvature regions of the loss landscape — it is, in spirit, a curvature-based regularizer.

10.4 The Osculating Circle and Attention Radius

The osculating circle at a point has radius ρ=1/κ and is the best local circular approximation to the curve. In attention mechanisms, the attention radius (how far back in the sequence the model attends) is analogous: at points of high curvature (sharp transitions), the model must attend locally; at low curvature (slow drift), the model can attend over longer ranges.

10.5 Curvature of Decision Boundaries

For a binary classifier with decision boundary f(x)=0, the curvature κ of this boundary (as a curve in R2 or a surface in Rn) controls:

  • Generalization: highly curved boundaries tend to overfit.
  • Adversarial robustness: inputs near high-curvature regions of the boundary are vulnerable to small perturbations that cross it.

Regularization methods like weight decay implicitly reduce the curvature of decision boundaries — a direct differential-geometric interpretation.

10.6 Geodesics on Data Manifolds

Real data (images, audio, text) lies on a low-dimensional manifold embedded in high-dimensional space. The geodesic between two points on this manifold is the shortest path along the manifold — the analogue of a straight line in curved space. Computing geodesics uses exactly the machinery of arc length and curvature developed here:

geodesic length=∫ab​gij​,x˙ix˙j​,dt

where gij​ is the Riemannian metric on the manifold. This is the foundation of Riemannian manifold optimization, used in training on curved parameter spaces (e.g., hyperbolic embeddings for hierarchical data).
