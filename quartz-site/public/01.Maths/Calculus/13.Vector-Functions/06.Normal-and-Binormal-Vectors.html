06.Normal and Binormal Vectors

“A curve in space carries its own coordinate system with it — one that breathes, twists, and tilts as we walk along it. This is the Frenet–Serret frame: the most natural house a curve can live in.”

0. Motivation & Setup

We already know the unit tangent vector T(t), which points in the direction of motion along a curve r(t). But motion in space is richer than a single direction. As you drive along a winding mountain road, three things happen simultaneously:

  1. You move forward — direction of T.
  2. The road curves — some direction orthogonal to T.
  3. The road may bank or twist out of the plane — a third direction.

These three orthogonal directions form the Frenet–Serret frame T,,N,,B, a moving orthonormal basis that travels with the curve.

Standing assumption throughout: r(t) is a smooth curve with r′(t)=0 everywhere, and r′′(t)∥r′(t) (so the curve is not locally straight).

1. Recall: The Unit Tangent Vector

T(t)=∣r′(t)∣r′(t)​

Key property: Since T is a unit vector, ∣T(t)∣=1 for all t, which implies:

T(t)⋅T(t)=1⟹dtd​[T⋅T]=0⟹2,T(t)⋅T′(t)=0

T′(t)⊥T(t)​

This is the pivotal observation: the derivative of any unit vector is always perpendicular to that vector. We use this fact repeatedly.

2. The Principal Normal Vector N

Definition

Since T′(t)⊥T(t), the vector T′(t) (when nonzero) points in the direction the curve is turning. We normalize it to get the principal unit normal vector:

N(t)=∣T′(t)∣T′(t)​​

Geometric Meaning

N(t) points toward the center of curvature — the center of the “best-fit circle” (osculating circle) at the point r(t). It lives in the osculating plane (the plane that best fits the curve locally), orthogonal to T.

  • On a circle traversed counterclockwise, N points radially inward (toward the center).
  • On a straight line, T′=0, so N is undefined — straight lines have no bending direction.

Connection to Curvature

Recall the curvature κ=​dsdT​​ where s is arc length. Since dsdT​=∣r′(t)∣T′(t)​, we have:

dsdT​=κ,N

This is the first Frenet–Serret formula (derived below). It says: as you walk along the curve at unit speed, the tangent changes at rate κ in the direction N.

3. The Binormal Vector B

Definition

With T and N in hand — both unit vectors, mutually orthogonal — we complete the right-handed orthonormal frame by taking the cross product:

B(t)=T(t)×N(t)​

B is the unit binormal vector.

It is automatically a unit vector: Since T and N are unit vectors orthogonal to each other, ∣B∣=∣T∣∣N∣sin(90°)=1⋅1⋅1=1.✓

Geometric Meaning

B is the normal to the osculating plane. As the curve evolves:

  • If B stays constant, the curve lies entirely in a plane (zero torsion).
  • If B is rotating, the curve is genuinely twisting through space.

The three planes defined by pairs of frame vectors have classical names:

PlaneSpanned byContains
Osculating planeT,,NThe curve’s “bending”
Normal planeN,,B⊥ to direction of motion
Rectifying planeT,,BThe curve’s “twisting”

4. The Full Frenet–Serret Apparatus

Torsion τ

Since B is a unit vector, B′⊥B. We claim B′⊥T as well:

Proof: B=T×N. Differentiate: B′=T′×N+T×N′

Now T′=κ∣r′∣,N (from dsdT​=κN, chain rule), so: T′×N=κ∣r′∣(N×N)=0

Thus B′=T×N′, which is orthogonal to T. Combined with B′⊥B, we conclude B′ is parallel to N. We define torsion τ by:

dsdB​=−τ,N

The sign is conventional — positive τ means a right-handed twist.

The Three Frenet–Serret Formulas

With respect to arc-length parameter s:

dsdT​=κ,N​

dsdN​=−κ,T+τ,B​

dsdB​=−τ,N​

Proof of the middle formula: Since N=B×T, differentiate using the product rule and substitute the first and third formulas.

In matrix form (Frenet–Serret system):

dsd​​TmathbfNmathbfB​​=(0​κ​0 −κ​0​τ 0​−τ​0​)​TmathbfNmathbfB​​

Note the antisymmetry of the matrix — a signature of the fact that the frame evolves by rotation (no stretching, since all vectors remain unit vectors).

5. Worked Examples

Example 1: Circle of Radius r in the xy-Plane

r(t)=(rcost,,rsint,,0)

Tangent: r′(t)=(−rsint,,rcost,,0),∣r′∣=r T(t)=(−sint,,cost,,0)

Normal: T′(t)=(−cost,,−sint,,0),∣T′∣=1 N(t)=(−cost,,−sint,,0)

Check: N points from the curve toward the origin (0,0,0) — indeed the center of the circle. ✓

Binormal: B(t)=T×N=(−sint,cost,0)×(−cost,−sint,0)=(0,0,sin2t+cos2t)=(0,0,1)

B is constant — the curve lies in the xy-plane, so the osculating plane never changes. Torsion τ=0. ✓

Curvature: κ=1/r. Tighter circles ⇒ larger curvature. ✓

Example 2: Helix

r(t)=(acost,,asint,,bt)

This is the prototypical curve with both nonzero curvature and nonzero torsion.

Step 1: r′(t)=(−asint,acost,b), ∣r′∣=a2+b2​=:c

T(t)=c1​(−asint,,acost,,b)

Step 2: T′(t)=c1​(−acost,,−asint,,0),∣T′∣=ca​

N(t)=(−cost,,−sint,,0)

N always points horizontally toward the z-axis — “inward,” as expected.

Step 3: B=T×N=c1​(−asint,acost,b)×(−cost,−sint,0)

Computing the cross product: B(t)=c1​(bsint,,−bcost,,a)

Curvature and Torsion of the Helix: κ=a2+b2a​,τ=a2+b2b​

  • When b=0: helix degenerates to a circle, κ=1/a, τ=0.
  • When a=0: straight line, κ=0.
  • Equal κ and τ when a=b — a “balanced” helix.

Intuition check: The helix has constant κ and τ — it’s the unique curve with this property (up to congruence). This reflects the helix’s translational symmetry along the z-axis.

7. Computing N and B Without Arc-Length Parametrization

In practice, curves are rarely given in arc-length form. For a general parameter t:

T(t)=∣r′∣r′​

N(t)=∣T′(t)∣T′(t)​

B(t)=T(t)×N(t)

For curvature and torsion in terms of r′ and r′′:

κ=∣r′∣3∣r′×r′′∣​

τ=∣r′×r′′∣2(r′×r′′)⋅r′′′​

These formulas are what you actually use in computation. The arc-length formulas are for theory.

8. The Fundamental Theorem of Space Curves

Theorem (Existence and Uniqueness): Given smooth functions κ(s)>0 and τ(s), there exists a unique space curve (up to rigid motion) with those curvature and torsion functions.

This is the analogue for curves of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — and it says: κ and τ completely characterize a curve’s shape. The Frenet–Serret formulas are then an ODE system whose solution is the frame (and thus the curve).

Proof sketch: The system dsd​​TmathbfNmathbfB​​=A(s)​TmathbfNmathbfB​​ is a linear ODE with smooth coefficients — existence and uniqueness follow from the Picard–Lindelöf theorem. The antisymmetry of A ensures orthonormality is preserved. Uniqueness up to rigid motion follows from the initial condition T0​,N0​,B0​,r(0).

9. Special Cases and Intuition

Straight line0undefinedNo bending, no frame

A planar curve has τ=0 if and only if it lies in a plane. This is the geometric test: compute τ and check.

10. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The Frenet–Serret frame is not merely a classical geometry artifact — it surfaces throughout modern machine learning in deep and sometimes unexpected ways.

10.1 Curve Geometry in Latent Spaces

Deep neural networks map data into high-dimensional latent spaces (think of the hidden layers of a VAE or the embedding space of a transformer). Trajectories in these spaces — sequences of token embeddings, latent diffusion paths, optimization paths — are curves in Rn.

The curvature κ of such a trajectory measures how sharply the latent representation is changing direction. A sudden spike in curvature during a language model’s generation trajectory can indicate:

  • A semantic shift (changing topic)
  • Hallucination onset (leaving the “manifold of plausible text”)
  • A difficult reasoning step

Researchers use curvature as a diagnostic tool for interpretability — analyzing the geometry of latent trajectories.

10.2 Riemannian Geometry of Neural Network Loss Landscapes

The loss landscape L(θ) of a neural network is a high-dimensional surface. The gradient descent trajectory θ(t) is a curve on this surface. Its Frenet–Serret frame tells us:

  • T: Direction of parameter update (steepest descent direction)
  • N: Direction in which the gradient is rotating — connected to the Hessian of the loss
  • κ: How quickly the gradient direction changes — large κ near saddle points or sharp valleys

The Natural Gradient method (Amari, 1998) and K-FAC approximate the Fisher information matrix, which is intimately related to the curvature of the statistical manifold the model lives on — a direct application of differential geometry.

10.3 Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Trajectory Modeling

When neural networks learn to predict physical trajectories (robot arms, drone paths, molecular dynamics), the Frenet–Serret formulas appear explicitly in the loss function or constraints:

  • A robot arm following a smooth path minimizes torsion τ (reduces mechanical stress).
  • Path planning algorithms enforce κ≤κmax​ (curvature constraints for vehicle turning radius).
  • Neural ODEs that model particle trajectories must learn the geometry of phase-space curves, where N and B encode centripetal and gyroscopic forces.

10.4 Recurrent Neural Networks as Dynamical Systems

A trained RNN defines a vector field ht+1​=f(ht​,xt​) on hidden state space. The trajectories of this dynamical system are curves. The Frenet frame of these hidden-state trajectories has been used to:

  • Identify fixed points (where T→0) and saddle points in the RNN dynamics
  • Measure sensitivity of the RNN to inputs via changes in κ
  • Understand long-range dependencies: trajectories with large torsion indicate the RNN is “twisting” through state space — often correlated with forgetting

This connects to the vanishing/exploding gradient problem: in the Frenet frame language, gradients vanish when the loss trajectory has near-zero curvature (the optimization isn’t turning anywhere useful).

10.5 Curve Fitting and Shape Analysis (Computer Vision)

In computer vision and shape analysis, the Frenet frame is used directly:

  • 3D shape descriptors: Curvature and torsion profiles (κ(s),τ(s)) are used as rotation-invariant signatures of 3D curves (blood vessels, protein backbones, road networks)
  • Deep learning on point clouds (PointNet, DGCNN): These architectures implicitly learn local geometric features, including curvature and frame information
  • Protein structure prediction (AlphaFold2): The backbone of a protein is a space curve. The Frenet frame of the Cα​ trace encodes secondary structure — helices have constant κ and τ, strands have τ≈0

10.6 Normalizing Flows and Manifold Learning

Normalizing flows learn diffeomorphic maps between distributions. The Jacobian of such a map encodes local geometric distortion. Along flow trajectories, N and B encode how the coordinate frame twists and bends — this is related to torsion of connections in the Riemannian geometry of the data manifold.

11. Summary and Big Picture

The Frenet–Serret frame T,N,B is the most natural coordinate system a curve carries with itself:

T=∣r′∣r′​,N=∣T′∣T′​,B=T×N

The three Frenet–Serret equations encode how this frame evolves:

dsdT​=κN,dsdN​=−κT+τB,dsdB​=−τN

Curvature κ measures bending; torsion τ measures twisting. Together, they completely characterize the shape of a space curve. This is a theorem — not merely a description.

In modern mathematics and its applications, this classical framework reappears whenever we study:

  • Paths in high-dimensional spaces (optimization, generative models)
  • Geometric structure of data manifolds
  • Physical trajectories constrained by curvature and torsion bounds
  • Biological structures like proteins, neurons, and DNA

The curve doesn’t just know where it’s going — it carries, at every point, a full description of how it’s going there. That’s the content of N and B.
