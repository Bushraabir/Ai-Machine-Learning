05.Arc Length and Curvature

05.Arc Length and Curvature

Apr 23, 202611 min read

“Curvature is the rate at which the curve turns — measured not against the clock, but against the road itself.”

0. Motivation

We already know r′(t) is the tangent vector. But two natural questions remain:

  1. How long is the curve? — this is arc length.
  2. How sharply does the curve bend? — this is curvature.

Both questions demand we measure intrinsic properties of the curve — properties that don’t depend on how fast we happen to traverse it. This forces us to re-parametrize by arc length, the natural “ruler” on a curve.

1. Arc Length

1.1 Definition

Let r(t)=⟨f(t),g(t),h(t)⟩ be a smooth curve on [a,b]. Partition [a,b] into n subintervals. The k-th chord has length ∣r(tk​)−r(tk−1​)∣. Taking the limit of the sum of chord lengths:

L=∫ab​∣r′(t)∣,dt=∫ab​[f′(t)]2+[g′(t)]2+[h′(t)]2​;dt​

Why ∣r′(t)∣? By the Mean Value Theorem applied component-wise, ∣r(tk​)−r(tk−1​)∣≈∣r′(tk∗​)∣,Δtk​. Summing and passing to the limit gives the integral. This is a clean Riemann sum argument.

Key insight (MIT 18.02). ∣r′(t)∣ is the speed of the parametrization — how fast the point moves. Arc length integrates speed over time to get distance. Same formula as d=∫v,dt from physics.

1.2 The Arc Length Function

Fix a basepoint a. Define:

s(t)=∫at​∣r′(u)∣,du

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

dtds​=∣r′(t)∣

So s measures how much curve has been traversed up to time t. The infinitesimal arc length element is:

ds=∣r′(t)∣,dt

1.3 Worked Example — Helix

r(t)=⟨cost,;sint,;t⟩,t∈[0,2π]

r′(t)=⟨−sint,;cost,;1⟩,∣r′(t)∣=sin2t+cos2t+1​=2​

L=∫02π​2​,dt=22​,π

The helix has constant speed 2​, so its length is simply speed × time = 2​⋅2π. One full turn rises by 2π in z while wrapping a circle of circumference 2π — Pythagoras gives (2π)2+(2π)2​=22​π. ✓

1.4 Parametrization Invariance

Arc length is independent of parametrization. If r~(τ)=r(t(τ)) with t′(τ)>0, then by the chain rule:

∣r~′(τ)∣=∣r′(t(τ))∣⋅t′(τ)

∫αβ​∣r~′(τ)∣,dτ=∫ab​∣r′(t)∣,dt

The curve length is a geometric fact — not an artifact of how we label points on it.

2. Parametrization by Arc Length

2.1 The Natural Parametrization

A curve is parametrized by arc length (or unit-speed) when:

∣r′(s)∣=1for all s

This is the parametrization where “parameter = distance traveled.” It strips away the arbitrary speed of any other parametrization and exposes the curve’s pure geometry.

How to find it: Given r(t), compute s(t)=∫at​∣r′(u)∣,du, invert to get t(s), then set r~(s)=r(t(s)).

Verification: ∣r~′(s)∣=∣r′(t(s))∣⋅∣t′(s)∣=∣r′(t)∣⋅∣r′(t)∣1​=1. ✓

2.2 Example — Circle

r(t)=⟨Rcost,;Rsint⟩,∣r′(t)∣=R

s=Rt⟹t=Rs​

r~(s)=⟨RcosRs​,;RsinRs​⟩

Check: ∣r~′(s)∣=​⟨−sinRs​,;cosRs​⟩​=1. ✓

3. Curvature

3.1 The Core Idea

The unit tangent vector T=r′/∣r′∣ always has magnitude 1, so it lives on the unit sphere. Curvature measures how rapidly T turns as we move along the curve.

But “rapidly” must be measured per unit of arc length, not per unit of time — otherwise a fast-moving particle on a straight line would appear to have curvature.

3.2 Definition

κ=​dsdT​​​

where s is arc length. Equivalently, by the chain rule dsdT​=ds/dtdT/dt​:

κ=∣r′(t)∣∣T′(t)∣​

Geometric reading: κ is large where the curve bends sharply (tight corner); κ=0 on a straight line; κ is constant on a circle.

Low curvature:              High curvature:
                                ╭──╮
────────────────             ───╯  ╰───
κ ≈ 0                           κ >> 0

3.3 Computational Formula (Stewart §13.3)

Computing T and then T′ is often messy. The following formula is far more practical:

κ=∣r′(t)∣3∣r′(t)×r′′(t)∣​​

Proof. Let v=∣r′∣=ds/dt. Then r′=vT. Differentiate:

r′′=v′T+vT′

Take the cross product with r′=vT:

r′×r′′=vT×(v′T+vT′)=v2(T×T′)

Since T⊥T′ (constant length — recall §7 of the previous note) and ∣T∣=1:

∣r′×r′′∣=v2∣T′∣

But κ=∣T′∣/v, so ∣T′∣=κv, giving:

∣r′×r′′∣=v2⋅κv=κv3=κ∣r′∣3

∴κ=∣r′∣3∣r′×r′′∣​□

3.4 Curvature of a Plane Curve y=f(x)

For r(x)=⟨x,f(x)⟩:

r′=⟨1,f′⟩,r′′=⟨0,f′′⟩

r′×r′′=⟨1,f′,0⟩×⟨0,f′′,0⟩=⟨0,0,f′′⟩⟹∣r′×r′′∣=∣f′′∣

∣r′∣3=(1+[f′]2)3/2

κ=(1+[f′(x)]2)3/2∣f′′(x)∣​​

At an inflection point f′′=0, curvature is zero — the curve momentarily “straightens.” At a local max/min where f′=0, curvature simplifies to ∣f′′∣ — pure second derivative.

4. The Frenet–Serret Frame

Curvature is part of a richer structure — the moving frame attached to a curve.

4.1 The Three Vectors

VectorDefinitionMeaning
T$\mathbf{r}’/\mathbf{r}’
N$\mathbf{T}’/\mathbf{T}‘
BT×NUnit binormal — axis of the osculating plane

These three form an orthonormal basis at every point of the curve — a “local coordinate system” that travels with the curve.

Why N⊥T? Since ∣T∣=1, we have T⋅T′=0, so T′⊥T always. N is just T′ normalized.

4.2 The Frenet–Serret Equations

dsdT​=κ,N

dsdN​=−κ,T+τ,B

dsdB​=−τ,N

where τ is the torsion — the rate at which the curve twists out of its osculating plane.

  • κ>0: curve bends
  • τ=0: curve is planar
  • τ=0: curve twists in 3D (like a helix)

4.3 Geometric Picture

                B (binormal)
                ↑
                │
                │   osculating plane
     N ←────── • ──────→ T (tangent)
    (normal)   P
               
   The curve bends in the T-N plane.
   Torsion measures how fast B rotates.

5. The Osculating Circle

At each point P on a curve, the osculating circle (from Latin osculari, “to kiss”) is the circle that:

  • Passes through P
  • Has the same tangent as the curve at P
  • Has the same curvature κ as the curve at P

Its radius of curvature is:

ρ=κ1​

Its center lies at P+ρ,N — a distance ρ along the normal direction.

Intuition. On a straight road, κ=0, ρ=∞: the “osculating circle” has infinite radius. On a sharp hairpin, κ is large, ρ is small. A circle of radius R has κ=1/R everywhere.

5.1 Example — Parabola

y=x2,f′=2x,f′′=2

κ(x)=(1+4x2)3/22​

At x=0 (vertex): κ=2, ρ=1/2.

At x→∞: κ→0 — the parabola flattens out.

          ─────┤─────
        ╱      │      ╲
       ╱   ⊙ ← osculating circle
      ╱    radius 1/2
     ╱─────┴─────╲

f′=cosx,f′′=−sinx

  • At x=0: κ=0 (inflection point — straightening).
  • Maximum curvature occurs at the peaks and troughs, as expected.

8. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

8.1 Loss Landscape Geometry — Curvature of Optimization Paths

During gradient descent, the parameter trajectory θ(t) is a curve in Rn. Its curvature κ measures how sharply the optimization path bends. High curvature near a saddle point means the optimizer is changing direction rapidly — a sign of instability.

The Hessian H=∇2L is the second derivative of the loss, encoding the curvature of the loss surface. Second-order optimizers (Newton’s method, L-BFGS) explicitly use H — they are computing the osculating paraboloid of L, the surface analogue of the osculating circle.

8.2 Natural Gradient — Arc Length in Parameter Space

The ordinary gradient ∇L measures steepness in Euclidean parameter space. But the natural gradient (Amari, 1998) accounts for the Riemannian metric on the statistical manifold of model distributions:

∇~L=F−1∇L

where F is the Fisher information matrix. This corrects for the fact that equal Euclidean steps in θ-space do not correspond to equal arc lengths in distribution space. The natural gradient is precisely the gradient after re-parametrizing by the arc length of the information geometry.

8.3 Attention and Sequence Curvature

In Transformer models, the hidden states h1​,h2​,…,hn​ form a discrete curve in representation space. Attention mechanisms can be interpreted as computing a weighted average of positions on this curve — a discretized integral operator analogous to ∫r(t),w(t),dt.

The curvature of the hidden-state trajectory correlates with how “surprising” or semantically complex the sequence is at that position.

8.4 Neural ODEs and Curvature Regularization

In a Neural ODE dtdh​=F(h,t), the trajectory h(t) is a curve in latent space. A natural regularizer is:

R=∫0T​∣h′′(t)∣2,dt

This penalizes large acceleration (equivalently, high curvature of the hidden-state trajectory), encouraging the network to learn smooth, gradual transformations. This is directly the squared curvature functional from differential geometry.

8.5 Geodesics and Shortest Path in Latent Space

In generative models (VAEs, diffusion models), the latent space has a geometry. Interpolating between two latent codes along the geodesic (the curve of minimal arc length) produces more natural interpolations than linear interpolation in Euclidean space. This requires computing:

L[γ]=∫01​∣γ′(t)∣g​,dt

where ∣⋅∣g​ is the Riemannian metric induced by the generative model — a direct application of our arc length formula.

8.6 Curvature of Decision Boundaries

In classification, the decision boundary is a surface (or curve in 2D). Its curvature measures how “wiggly” the boundary is:

  • High curvature → overfitting (the model memorized the training data with sharp twists)
  • Low curvature → smoother generalization

Regularization methods (weight decay, dropout, spectral normalization) implicitly reduce the curvature of decision boundaries.

  2. [Parametrization] Re-parametrize the line r(t)=⟨1+3t,2−t,4t⟩ by arc length starting at t=0.

  3. [Curvature] Prove that κ=0 everywhere on a line. What does this say about T′?
