0. Orientation

A power function is the simplest non-trivial function: one variable, one exponent, one parameter. Yet the behavior changes radically as we vary the exponent — integer, rational, real, even complex. Understanding this single family of functions is the foundation for:

  • Polynomials (finite sums of power functions)
  • Taylor series (infinite sums)
  • Scaling laws in physics and ML
  • Activation functions and loss landscapes in deep learning

Do not rush this. The function f(x)=xr is deceptively rich.

1. Definition

Definition. A power function is any function of the form f(x)=xr where r∈R is a fixed constant called the exponent (or power), and x is the variable.

Contrast with exponential functions: In f(x)=xr, the base is the variable and the exponent is fixed. In g(x)=ax, the exponent is the variable. This distinction is fundamental and constantly confused.

3. The Exponent Zoo — A Taxonomy

3.1 Positive Integer Powers (r=1,2,3,…)

These are the monomials: x,x2,x3,x4,…

Parity determines symmetry:

  • r even: f(−x)=f(x) — even function, symmetric about the y-axis.
  • r odd: f(−x)=−f(x) — odd function, symmetric about the origin.

Growth: xn→+∞ as x→+∞ for all n≥1.

Comparison of growth rates: x≪x2≪x3≪⋯ as x→∞

More precisely: limx→∞​xnxm​=0 whenever m<n. Higher powers dominate.

3.3 Rational Powers — Roots (r=1/n)

f(x)=x1/n=nx​

n odd (e.g., cube root x1/3):

  • Domain: R.
  • Odd function.
  • Always passes through (−1,−1),(0,0),(1,1).
  • Slope is infinite at x=0 (vertical tangent).

n even (e.g., square root x1/2):

  • Domain: [0,∞).
  • Not defined for x<0 (over R).
  • Concave down, increasing.

Key shape: Root functions grow slower than linear: limx→∞​xx1/n​=limx→∞​x1/n−1=0(n>1)

3.4 General Real Powers (r∈R, x>0)

For x>0, defined as f(x)=erlnx.

Behavior at the anchor points:

  • f(0+): →0 if r>0; →+∞ if r<0; =1 if r=0.
  • f(1)=1 for all r — every power function passes through (1,1).
  • f(∞): →∞ if r>0; →0 if r<0.

Observation. The point (1,1) is the universal fixed point of all power functions. It is the natural “anchor” — the place where all power functions agree.

Behavior hierarchy as x→∞: lnx≪xr≪ex for any r>0

This is a theorem, not an approximation. Exponentials eventually dominate all power functions; logarithms grow slower than all positive powers.

4. Calculus of Power Functions

4.1 The Power Rule — General Version

Theorem (Power Rule). For f(x)=xr with r∈R: dxd​[xr]=rxr−1 wherever f is differentiable.

Proof for r∈Z+ (from first principles):

f′(x)=limh→0​h(x+h)r−xr​

Expand (x+h)r by the Binomial Theorem: (x+h)r=xr+rxr−1h+(2r​)xr−2h2+⋯+hr

Subtract xr, divide by h: h(x+h)r−xr​=rxr−1+(2r​)xr−2h+⋯+hr−1

Take h→0: all terms with a factor of h vanish, leaving rxr−1. □

Proof for r∈R (using logarithmic differentiation):

Write f(x)=erlnx. Then: f′(x)=erlnx⋅xr​=xr⋅xr​=rxr−1□

Remark. This second proof reveals something deep: the power rule for general real exponents is really a consequence of the chain rule and the fundamental relationship between ex and lnx. The power rule is not “elementary” — it secretly involves exponential functions.

4.3 Integration

The antiderivative follows directly from the derivative:

Theorem (Power Rule for Integration). For r=−1: ∫xr,dx=r+1xr+1​+C

Why r=−1 is excluded: ∫x−1dx=ln∣x∣+C. The formula 0x0​ is undefined — the antiderivative of 1/x genuinely escapes the power function family and lands in the logarithm.

This is not a coincidence. The logarithm is the area function of 1/x: ln(a)=∫1a​t1​,dt

This is in fact one clean way to define the natural logarithm — as the unique antiderivative of x−1 satisfying ln(1)=0.

5. Monotonicity and Inverse Functions

5.1 Monotonicity

For x>0:

  • r>0: f is strictly increasing (larger x gives larger xr).
  • r<0: f is strictly decreasing (larger x gives smaller xr).
  • r=0: constant function f=1.

Proof. f′(x)=rxr−1. For x>0, xr−1>0 always. Sign of f′ equals sign of r. □

5.2 Inverse Functions

On the appropriate domain:

f(x)=xr⟹f−1(x)=x1/r

Proof. f(f−1(x))=(x1/r)r=xr/r=x1=x. □

Example: f(x)=x3 has inverse f−1(x)=x1/3. The graphs are reflections across the line y=x.

6. Power Functions vs. Exponential — A Critical Distinction

This deserves its own section because it is the most common conceptual error.

6.1 Growth Rate Comparison

Theorem. For any r>0 and any a>1: limx→∞​axxr​=0

That is, every exponential function ax eventually dominates every power function xr, no matter how large r is.

Proof. Apply L’Hôpital’s rule ⌈r⌉ times. Each application differentiates xr to reduce the exponent; differentiating ax merely multiplies by lna. After ⌈r⌉ steps, the numerator is constant while the denominator is still ∼ax. □

Consequence: polynomial≪power (positive)≪exponential

6.2 Near x=0: Power Functions Win

For x∈(0,1), the order reverses: ex≈1+x+… is nearly constant, while xr for r>1 plunges toward 0. This is why power functions are preferred for modeling small-x phenomena (percolation thresholds, phase transitions near critical points).

7. Scaling Laws — The Physical Content of Power Functions

The defining property of power functions is scale invariance.

Theorem. f(x)=xr satisfies the scaling relation: f(λx)=λrf(x) for all λ>0.

Proof. f(λx)=(λx)r=λrxr=λrf(x). □

This property — that scaling the input by λ scales the output by λr — is called homogeneity of degree r.

Converse (Stewart §1.2 flavor). If a continuous function f:(0,∞)→R satisfies f(λx)=λrf(x) for all λ,x>0, then f(x)=f(1)⋅xr. Power functions are the only scale-invariant functions.

Physical examples:

  • Area of a square: A=s2. Double the side (λ=2): area quadruples (22=4).
  • Volume of a sphere: V=34​πr3. Triple the radius: volume increases by 33=27.
  • Gravitational force: F∝r−2. Double the distance: force drops to 1/4.

8. Log-Log Plots and Power Law Detection

8.1 The Key Trick

If y=Cxr, take log of both sides: logy=rlogx+logC

Setting Y=logy and X=logx: Y=rX+logC

This is a straight line with slope r and intercept logC.

Consequence. A power law appears as a straight line on a log-log plot. The slope is the exponent. This is the standard empirical test for power laws.

Example 1: Full Analysis of f(x)=x2/3

Domain: R (cube root of x2, defined everywhere).

Symmetry: f(−x)=(−x)2/3=(x2)1/3=x2/3=f(x). Even function.

10. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

10.1 Power Laws in Neural Scaling

This is one of the most empirically striking facts in modern deep learning.

Observation (Kaplan et al., 2020 — OpenAI Scaling Laws): The test loss L of a language model follows a power law in the number of parameters N, training tokens D, and compute C:

L(N)≈(NNc​​)αN​,αN​≈0.076

On a log-log plot: a straight line. This is exactly the signature of a power function.

Why does this matter? It means:

  • Doubling model size gives a predictable, consistent improvement.
  • You can extrapolate performance before training — the exponent α is stable across scales.
  • The optimal allocation of compute between model size and data follows a power law (the Chinchilla scaling law, Hoffmann et al. 2022).

10.2 Activation Functions as Power Functions

Several key activation functions are power functions or closely related:

ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit): ReLU(x)=max(0,x)={x​x≥0 0​x<0​ This is a piecewise power function: x1 for x≥0. Its derivative is a step function (piecewise x0).

Leaky ReLU: LeakyReLU(x)={x​x≥0 αx​x<0​ Still piecewise linear (degree 1 power).

ELU and SELU approximate ex−1≈x+x2/2+… for x<0 — Taylor series using power functions.

Polynomial activations are used explicitly in:

  • Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs, 2024): replace fixed activations with learnable splines (piecewise polynomials).
  • Implicit Neural Representations (SIREN): use sin(ω0​x) whose Taylor expansion is a power series.

10.3 Loss Functions and Power Functions

MSE (Mean Squared Error): L(y^​,y)=n1​∑i​(yi​−y^​i​)2 The squaring is x2 — a power function. It penalizes large errors more than small ones, quadratically.

MAE (Mean Absolute Error): L(y^​,y)=n1​∑i​∣yi​−y^​i​∣ This is ∣x∣1 — a power function with r=1. Less sensitive to outliers than MSE.

Huber Loss interpolates: Lδ​(x)={21​x2​∣x∣≤δ δ(∣x∣−2δ​)​∣x∣>δ​ Quadratic (power 2) near zero, linear (power 1) in the tails. This is explicitly a piecewise power function.

General Lp loss: Lp​=∑i​∣yi​−y^​i​∣p

  • p=1: MAE (robust to outliers)
  • p=2: MSE (efficient, assumes Gaussian noise)
  • p→∞: Chebyshev norm (controls worst-case error)

The choice of p — the exponent of a power function — completely determines the geometry of the loss landscape.

10.4 Attention and Softmax — Power Functions in Disguise

The softmax function is: softmax(xi​)=∑j​exj​exi​​

For large inputs, this concentrates mass on the maximum. But consider the sparsemax and α-entmax generalizations used in modern attention:

α-entmax(z)=argmaxp∈Δ​[p⊤z+Hα​(p)]

For α=2: the output is piecewise linear — a power function of rank 1 in the inputs. The entire family of attention mechanisms interpolates between α=1 (softmax) and α=2 (sparsemax) by varying the exponent of a power-function-based entropy term.

10.5 Weight Initialization and Power Laws in Spectra

Xavier/Glorot initialization sets weights W∼U[−n​1​,n​1​]. The 1/n​ is n−1/2 — a power function of the layer width.

He initialization (for ReLU networks): W∼N(0,2/n), standard deviation ∝n−1/2.

In both cases, the initialization scale follows a power law in network width. This ensures variance is preserved across layers — a direct consequence of the scaling property f(λx)=λrf(x).

Eigenvalue spectra of weight matrices in trained deep networks often follow power laws (Martin & Mahoney, 2019): ρ(λ)∼λ−α Networks with α∈(2,4) tend to generalize better. This is active research — the exponent of the spectral power law as a diagnostic of model quality.

10.6 Gradient Flow and Power Function Dynamics

Consider the gradient flow ODE for loss L(θ)=θr: θ˙=−dθdL​=−rθr−1

Solution: Depends on r.

  • r=2 (quadratic loss): θ˙=−2θ⟹θ(t)=θ0​e−2t. Exponential convergence.
  • r=4 (quartic loss): θ˙=−4θ3⟹θ(t)=θ0​(1+8θ02​t)−1/2. Power law convergence — much slower.

Key insight. The shape of the loss landscape — which is determined by the power r — controls the speed of gradient-based optimization. Flat (high r) losses are hard to optimize; quadratic (r=2) losses are ideal. This is why second-order methods (Newton, curvature) try to locally transform the landscape toward r=2.
