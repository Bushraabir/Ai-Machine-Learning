“A rational function is a fraction whose numerator and denominator are polynomials. Its soul lives in its singularities — the places where the denominator vanishes.”

§1. Definition & Basic Setup

Definition 1.1 (Rational Function). A rational function is any function of the form r(x)=q(x)p(x)​ where p(x) and q(x) are polynomials and q(x)≡0.

Domain. The natural domain of r is all real x where q(x)=0: dom(r)=x∈R:q(x)=0.

Convention. We always assume p and q share no common factors — i.e., r is in lowest terms. If they do share a factor (x−a), cancelling it removes a removable discontinuity (a hole), not a vertical asymptote.

§2. Zeros and Poles

Definition 2.1 (Zero). x=a is a zero of r if r(a)=0, i.e., p(a)=0 and q(a)=0.

Definition 2.2 (Pole). x=a is a pole of r if q(a)=0 and p(a)=0 (after full cancellation of common factors). The order of the pole is the multiplicity of (x−a) as a factor of q.

Behaviour at a pole of order n: limx→a​∣r(x)∣=+∞. The parity of n determines whether r changes sign:

  • Odd order pole: r(x)→+∞ from one side, −∞ from the other. Graph crosses the vertical asymptote “direction-wise.”
  • Even order pole: r(x)→+∞ from both sides (or −∞ from both sides).

Example 2.1. r(x)=(x−1)2(x+2)1​

  • Pole of order 2 at x=1 (even): r→+∞ from both sides.
  • Pole of order 1 at x=−2 (odd): r→+∞ from one side, −∞ from the other.

§3. Asymptotes — The Skeleton of the Graph

Asymptotes encode the global behaviour of r. There are three types.

3.1 Vertical Asymptotes

x=a is a vertical asymptote if q(a)=0 and the factor (x−a) does not cancel with p(x).

limx→a+​r(x)=±∞and/orlimx→a−​r(x)=±∞.

3.2 Horizontal Asymptotes

Let degp=m, degq=n, with leading coefficients am​ and bn​.

limx→±∞​r(x)={0​if m<n bn​am​​​if m=n ±∞​if m>n​

When m<n or m=n: horizontal asymptote y=0 or y=am​/bn​. When m>n: no horizontal asymptote (but possibly an oblique one).

3.3 Oblique (Slant) Asymptotes

Occur exactly when degp=degq+1. Perform polynomial long division: r(x)=q(x)p(x)​=(ax+b)+q(x)remainder(x)​. Since deg(remainder)<degq, the remainder term →0 as x→±∞, so: r(x)≈ax+bas x→±∞. The line y=ax+b is the oblique asymptote.

Example 3.1. r(x)=x−1x2+3​ Long division: x2+3=(x−1)(x+1)+4, so r(x)=x+1+x−14​. Oblique asymptote: y=x+1. Vertical asymptote: x=1.

§4. Holes vs. Vertical Asymptotes — A Critical Distinction

When a factor (x−a) appears in both p and q:

r(x)=(x−a)n⋅q~​(x)(x−a)m⋅p~​(x)​,q~​(a)=0.

  • If m≥n: factor cancels fully or partially. The singularity at x=a is removable — a hole in the graph.
  • If m<n: a factor remains in denominator. Vertical asymptote at x=a.

Example 4.1 — Hole. r(x)=x−2x2−4​=x−2(x−2)(x+2)​=x+2,x=2. The graph is the line y=x+2 with a single point punched out at (2,4).

The limit exists: limx→2​r(x)=4, but r(2) is undefined. This is a removable discontinuity.

§5. Graphing Strategy — A Complete Procedure

To sketch r(x)=p(x)/q(x) in lowest terms:

  1. Domain: Solve q(x)=0; exclude those x.
  2. Holes: Factor both p and q; cancel common factors; note the x-values removed.
  3. Zeros: Solve p(x)=0 (with q(x)=0). Note multiplicities.
  4. y-intercept: Compute r(0) if 0 is in the domain.
  5. Vertical asymptotes: Uncancelled zeros of q; determine sign behaviour (odd/even order).
  6. Horizontal or oblique asymptote: Compare degrees.
  7. Sign chart: Between zeros and poles, determine the sign of r.
  8. End behaviour: Confirm consistency with asymptotes.
  9. Sketch.

As x→±∞: r(x)→x. The graph hugs the line y=x asymptotically.

§7. Limits and Continuity

Proposition 7.1. Every rational function r=p/q is continuous on its domain.

Proof. Both p and q are polynomials, hence continuous everywhere. On the set x:q(x)=0, r is a quotient of continuous functions with nonzero denominator, hence continuous. □

Limits at poles. Near a simple pole x=a, write q(x)=(x−a)q~​(x) with q~​(a)=0:

r(x)=(x−a)q~​(x)p(x)​→0±⋅q~​(a)p(a)​=±∞.

The sign depends on the sign of p(a)/q~​(a) and the direction of approach.

§8. Calculus of Rational Functions

8.1 Differentiation

Using the quotient rule: r′(x)=[q(x)]2p′(x)q(x)−p(x)q′(x)​.

The result is again a rational function (with [q(x)]2 in the denominator). Critical points of r occur where the numerator p′q−pq′=0.

Example 8.1. r(x)=x1​, so p=1, q=x: r′(x)=x20⋅x−1⋅1​=−x21​<0∀x=0. r is strictly decreasing on each interval of its domain. No local extrema.

8.2 Integration via Partial Fractions

Integrating rational functions reduces to partial fraction decomposition — the systematic algebraic splitting of r into simpler summands.

The master strategy. If degp≥degq, first perform long division to write: r(x)=s(x)+q(x)rem(x)​,deg(rem)<degq. Then decompose the proper fraction rem/q.

Common misconception: Graphs never cross vertical asymptotes (by definition — r is undefined there). But horizontal asymptotes are a statement about limits at infinity — the graph absolutely can cross the horizontal asymptote for finite x.

Example: r(x)=1sinx⋅e−x2​ is not rational, but illustrates the principle. For rational functions, a simple example:

§10. Partial Fractions and the Residue — Bridge to Complex Analysis

The coefficients in the partial fraction decomposition for simple poles have a beautiful interpretation. For a simple pole at x=a:

A=limx→a​(x−a),r(x)=q′(a)p(a)​.

This is called the residue of r at a. In complex analysis, the residue theorem turns this algebraic observation into a powerful integration technique over complex contours. The partial fractions you compute in Calculus II are the real-line shadow of that theory.

§11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

Rational functions appear in ML in places that are easy to overlook but structurally important.

11.1 Rational Approximation of Activation Functions

The GELU activation, used in GPT and BERT, is defined as: GELU(x)=x⋅Φ(x) where Φ is the Gaussian CDF — not closed form. In practice, it is approximated by a rational function for fast GPU evaluation: GELU(x)≈1+e−1.702xx​ or via a degree-(3,2) Padé approximant. The Padé approximant is the canonical rational-function approximation to a smooth function — analogous to Taylor series but often superior near singularities.

Padé approximant of order (m,n) to f at x=0: Rm,n​(x)=1+b1​x+⋯+bn​xna0​+a1​x+⋯+am​xm​ chosen so that Rm,n​ and f agree to order m+n in their Taylor expansion.

11.2 Rational Activations (RAU)

Recent work (Molina et al., 2019) proposes replacing fixed activations with learnable rational functions: σ(x)=Q(x)P(x)​,P,Q polynomials with learnable coefficients. These Rational Activation Units (RAU) can approximate ReLU, Leaky ReLU, GELU, etc., and the network learns the best activation shape during training. The key constraint: Q(x)>0 must be enforced (e.g., Q(x)=∣q(x)∣+ϵ) to prevent poles inside the domain.

11.3 Transformer Attention is a Rational Function

Scaled dot-product attention: Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(d​QK⊤​)V The softmax of a linear expression is a rational function componentwise: softmax(z)i​=∑j​ezj​ezi​​. This is a ratio of exponentials — technically an exponential rational function, but its algebraic structure (numerator/denominator both positive, output sums to 1) mirrors the rational function setting. The gradient of softmax inherits the quotient-rule structure: ∂zj​∂softmax(z)i​​=softmax(z)i​(δij​−softmax(z)j​).

11.4 Rational Interpolation in Gaussian Processes

Kernel functions in Gaussian Processes (GPs) are sometimes rational: k(x,x′)=1+(lx−x′​)2σ2​ This is the rational quadratic kernel — a scale mixture of squared-exponential kernels. Its rational structure gives heavier tails than the Gaussian kernel, better modelling functions with varying lengthscales.

11.5 Stability Analysis of Recurrent Networks

The hidden-state update of a linear RNN is: ht​=Wht−1​+Uxt​. Unrolling and taking the z-transform gives a transfer function: H(z)=(zI−W)−1U, which is a matrix of rational functions of z (the matrix inverse of a polynomial matrix is rational). The poles of H(z) are the eigenvalues of W. Stability requires all poles inside the unit disk ∣z∣<1 — a condition on the zeros of det(zI−W), which is a polynomial. The theory of poles of rational functions is the exact tool used in signal processing and control theory to analyse such systems.
