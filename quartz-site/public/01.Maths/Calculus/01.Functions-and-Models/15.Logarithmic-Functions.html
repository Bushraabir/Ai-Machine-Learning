0. Motivation — Why Should We Care?

Before the machinery, a word of honesty: logarithms are not a trick. They are the inverse of the most natural function in analysis — the exponential — and they appear wherever growth, decay, entropy, or information lives.

In 1614, John Napier introduced logarithms to turn multiplication into addition (a computational miracle before computers). Today they appear in:

  • Information theory: Shannon entropy H=−∑pi​logpi​
  • Signal processing: decibels, Fourier analysis
  • Physics: thermodynamic entropy S=kB​lnΩ
  • Machine learning / deep learning: cross-entropy loss, log-likelihood, softmax, log-sum-exp trick — we will return to all of these

1. Definition — The Right One

There are two equivalent entry points. I prefer the integral definition because it requires no prior knowledge of e and makes differentiability immediate.

1.1 The Natural Logarithm via an Integral

lnx;:=;∫1x​t1​,dt,x>0​

Why is this a good definition?

We want a function L satisfying L′(x)=1/x with L(1)=0. The First Fundamental Theorem of Calculus hands us exactly that. Every property of ln — its domain, sign, monotonicity, the logarithm laws — follows from this integral and from properties of Riemann integrals.

Geometric picture. lnx is the signed area under the hyperbola y=1/t from t=1 to t=x.

  • x>1: area is positive, so lnx>0.
  • x=1: zero-width strip, so ln1=0.
  • 0<x<1: we integrate backwards, so lnx<0.

1.2 The Number e — Emerging from the Definition

Define e as the unique number satisfying lne=1, i.e.,

∫1e​t1​,dt=1.

It can be shown this number exists (by IVT: ln is continuous, ln1=0, ln is unbounded above) and is unique (strict monotonicity). Numerically, e≈2.71828…

2. Fundamental Properties

All of the following follow from the integral definition. I will prove each.

2.1 Domain and Range

ln:(0,∞)→R is a bijection.

  • Domain (0,∞): 1/t is only integrable on intervals avoiding t=0.
  • Range R:
    • lnx→+∞ as x→∞ (compare with ∫1x​t1​,dt≥∫1x​x1​,dt=xx−1​… or use the harmonic series argument).
    • lnx→−∞ as x→0+ (substitute u=1/t).

2.2 Logarithm Laws

Theorem. For a,b>0 and r∈Q:

\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b \tag{L1} \ln!\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right) = \ln a - \ln b \tag{L2} \ln(a^r) = r\ln a \tag{L3}

Proof of (L1). Fix a>0 and let f(x)=ln(ax). Then f′(x)=axa​=x1​=(lnx)′. So f(x)=lnx+C for some constant. Setting x=1: f(1)=lna=ln1+C=C. Therefore ln(ax)=lnx+lna. Setting x=b gives (L1). ■

(L2) follows from (L1): ln(a/b)+lnb=ln!(ba​⋅b)=lna.

(L3) for integer r follows by induction from (L1); the rational and real cases require a bit more care.

2.3 Derivative and Monotonicity

dxd​lnx=x1​>0for all x>0.

So ln is strictly increasing. Also, dx2d2​lnx=−1/x2<0, so ln is strictly concave on (0,∞).

Remark (concavity matters in ML). The strict concavity of ln is exactly why Jensen’s inequality gives ln(E[X])≥E[lnX] — a fact that underlies the EM algorithm and variational inference.

2.4 The Chain Rule with ln

dxd​ln∣u(x)∣=u(x)u′(x)​

This is the workhorse for integration: ∫uu′​,dx=ln∣u∣+C.

Example. ∫x2+12x​,dx=ln(x2+1)+C. (Here u=x2+1>0, so no absolute value needed.)

3. General Logarithms

3.1 Change of Base

For any base b>0, b=1, define:

logb​x:=lnblnx​

This is not a separate function — it is a rescaled natural logarithm. The base b=10 gives log10​ (common log), and b=e gives ln itself.

Derivative: dxd​logb​x=xlnb1​

In practice: unless forced otherwise, always work in base e. The extra factor of lnb in denominators is noise.

3.2 The Exponential Function — The Inverse

Since ln:(0,∞)→R is a bijection, its inverse exp=ln−1:R→(0,∞) exists. We denote exp(x)=ex. From inverse function differentiation:

dxd​ex=ex.

The mutual inverse relationship: ln(ex)=x∀x∈R,elnx=x∀x>0.

4. Asymptotic Behavior — Two Important Limits

These are indispensable and constantly appear in analysis and ML.

Theorem. \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x^p} = 0 \quad \text{for any } p > 0 \tag{A1} \lim_{x \to 0^+} x^p \ln x = 0 \quad \text{for any } p > 0 \tag{A2}

Proof of (A1). For x>1: lnx=∫1x​tdt​≤∫1x​tp/2−1,dt=p2​(xp/2−1)≤p2​xp/2. So 0≤xplnx​≤p2​x−p/2→0. ■

Verbal summary: Logarithms grow more slowly than any positive power of x. Any power of x damps out ∣lnx∣ at the origin.

Growth rate hierarchy (slowest to fastest):
ln(ln x) ≪ ln x ≪ x^ε ≪ x ≪ x^2 ≪ e^x ≪ e^(e^x)
for any fixed ε > 0.

5. Integration Involving ln

5.1 The Fundamental Antiderivative

∫x1​,dx=ln∣x∣+C

The absolute value handles x<0: for x<0, let u=−x, then du=−dx and ∫x1​dx=∫u−du​=−lnu=ln∣x∣.

5.2 Integration by Parts: ∫lnx,dx

∫lnx,dx=xlnx−x+C

Proof. Set u=lnx, dv=dx. Then du=x1​dx, v=x. ∫lnx,dx=xlnx−∫x⋅x1​,dx=xlnx−x+C.■

5.3 Logarithmic Integration Pattern

∫f(x)f′(x)​,dx=ln∣f(x)∣+C

Example. ∫tanx,dx=∫cosxsinx​,dx=−ln∣cosx∣+C=ln∣secx∣+C.

6. Logarithmic Differentiation

A technique for differentiating products, quotients, or functions with variable exponents.

Strategy: Take ln of both sides, differentiate implicitly, solve for y′.

Example 1. Let y=xx for x>0. lny=xlnx⟹yy′​=lnx+1⟹y′=xx(lnx+1).

Example 2. Let y=(2x−1)3x2x+1​​.

lny=2lnx+21​ln(x+1)−3ln(2x−1) yy′​=x2​+2(x+1)1​−2x−16​

Multiply through by y to get y′. Much cleaner than the quotient rule.

7. Taylor Series and Approximations

Near x=0:

ln(1+x)=x−2x2​+3x3​−⋯=∑n=1∞​n(−1)n+1​xn,∣x∣≤1,;x=−1

Proof sketch. We know dxd​ln(1+x)=1+x1​=∑n=0∞​(−1)nxn for ∣x∣<1. Integrating term by term and using ln(1)=0 gives the series.

First-order approximation: For small x, ln(1+x)≈x. This is everywhere in ML. The log-likelihood gradient, the natural gradient, the derivation of KL divergence — all lean on this.

8. Connections to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is where the abstract machinery becomes viscerally useful. I want to show why logarithms, specifically, appear — not just that they do.

8.1 Cross-Entropy Loss

In a K-class classification problem, let p=(p1​,…,pK​) be the true distribution (a one-hot vector in practice) and p^​=(p^​1​,…,p^​K​) be the model’s predicted probabilities (output of a softmax). The cross-entropy loss is:

L=−∑k=1K​pk​lnp^​k​

For a one-hot label where pc​=1 for correct class c:

L=−lnp^​c​

Why ln? Because we are doing maximum likelihood estimation. The likelihood of data xi​,yi​i=1N​ under the model is: LMLE​=∏i=1N​p^​yi​​(xi​). Maximizing this is equivalent to minimizing −lnLMLE​=−∑i​lnp^​yi​​(xi​). The product became a sum because of property (L1). This is why gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood.

Key calculus here. ∂zc​∂​(−lnp^​c​)=p^​c​−1 (after passing through the softmax — a beautiful calculation involving dxd​ln and the chain rule). This is why the gradient of cross-entropy + softmax is so clean.

8.2 The Log-Sum-Exp Trick — Numerical Stability

The softmax is: p^​k​=∑j​ezj​ezk​​

For large logits zk​ (e.g., zk​=1000), ezk​ overflows to inf on a computer. The fix: use the identity

ln!∑j​ezj​=c+ln!∑j​ezj​−c

(follows from (L1): ln(ec⋅∑ezj​−c)=c+ln∑ezj​−c). Choose c=maxj​zj​. Now the largest term inside the sum is e0=1, and everything is numerically safe.

This is applied logarithm arithmetic — property (L1) saves numerical computation.

8.3 KL Divergence

The Kullback–Leibler divergence from distribution q to p:

DKL​(p∣q)=∑k​pk​lnqk​pk​​=∑k​pk​(lnpk​−lnqk​)

Properties:

  • DKL​≥0 always (Gibbs’ inequality, proved using concavity of ln and Jensen’s inequality: −ln is convex, so −Ep​[ln(q/p)]≥−lnEp​[q/p]=0).
  • DKL​=0 iff p=q.
  • Not symmetric — it is not a metric, but it measures “information lost when using q to approximate p.”

In variational autoencoders (VAEs), the loss is ELBO = reconstruction loss + DKL​(qϕ​(z∣x)∣p(z)). The KL term regularizes the latent space toward the prior. Its gradient is computable exactly when both distributions are Gaussian, using the formula for Gaussian KL — which requires ln of the determinants of covariance matrices.

8.4 Entropy and Information

Shannon entropy: H(p)=−∑k​pk​lnpk​

This measures the average uncertainty (or information content) in a distribution. The ln makes it additive for independent events (from (L1)): H(p⊗q)=H(p)+H(q).

In decision tree learning (CART, ID3), splitting criteria use information gain =H(parent)−∑H(children). The logarithm is not cosmetic — it is the unique (up to scalar) function making information additive. (Shannon’s theorem.)

8.5 Log-Likelihood and Gradient Descent

Training a neural network by gradient descent on negative log-likelihood:

θ∗=argminθ​[−∑i=1N​lnpθ​(yi​∣xi​)]

The gradient is:

∇θ​[−lnpθ​(y∣x)]=−∇θ​lnpθ​(y∣x)=−pθ​(y∣x)∇θ​pθ​(y∣x)​

(by the chain rule: dxd​lnf(x)=f(x)f′(x)​)

This score function ∇θ​lnpθ​ appears in policy gradient methods (REINFORCE), the Fisher information matrix, and natural gradient descent. Every one of these rests on the single formula (lnf)′=f′/f.

8.6 Logarithmic Learning Rate Schedules

Practitioners use schedules like: ηt​=η0​/ln(t+2)

This decays very slowly (slower than 1/t, faster than constant) — precisely because lnt grows slowly (recall (A1)). The asymptotic hierarchy of §4 is not academic — it tells you which schedule converges, and how fast.

Example 9.5 — Cross-Entropy Gradient (ML Application)

Suppose p^​=σ(z)=1/(1+e−z) (sigmoid, binary classification). Loss: L=−ylnp^​−(1−y)ln(1−p^​). Compute ∂L/∂z:

∂z∂L​=−p^​y​p^​′+1−p^​1−y​p^​′

where p^​′=p^​(1−p^​) (standard sigmoid derivative). Substituting:

=−y(1−p^​)+(1−y)p^​=p^​−y.

The loss gradient is simply prediction minus label — a beautiful fact enabled entirely by dxd​lnx=1/x eating the denominator.

12. Personal Marginalia

Why is the natural log “natural”? Because dxd​lnb​x=xlnb1​, and only b=e gives the clean coefficient 1. Any other base clutters every derivative and integral with a constant factor. Nature (and mathematicians) despise clutter.

The logarithm is the original data scientist’s transform. Raw data spanning orders of magnitude — incomes, earthquake energies, neural firing rates, word frequencies (Zipf’s law) — becomes Gaussian-ish after a log transform. Why? Because many natural phenomena are log-normally distributed: their logarithm is normal. The moment you take a log, you’re doing exponential-family statistics.

Jensen’s inequality + concavity of ln = half of modern probabilistic machine learning. The ELBO in variational inference, the EM algorithm, contrastive learning bounds — all are applications of ln(E[X])≥E[lnX] combined with the definition of KL divergence. One concavity fact, infinite consequences.
