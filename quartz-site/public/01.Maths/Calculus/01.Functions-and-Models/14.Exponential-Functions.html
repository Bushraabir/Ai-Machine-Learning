1. Motivation — Why Exponentials Are Special

Every function we typically write — polynomials, rationals, power functions — grows at most polynomially. Exponentials are categorically different: they grow faster than any polynomial.

Claim: For any n∈N and a>1: limx→∞​axxn​=0.

Proof sketch: Apply L’Hôpital’s Rule n times. Each differentiation reduces the power of x by 1 in the numerator, while the denominator remains a multiple of ax. After n steps: limx→∞​(lna)n⋅axn!​=0. □

This single fact is why exponentials dominate computational complexity, population models, neural network expressivity bounds, and learning rate schedules.

2. Formal Definition

2.1 Integer Exponents First

For a>0 and n∈Z: an=n timesa⋅a⋯a​​(n>0),a0=1,a−n=an1​.

2.2 Rational Exponents

For p/q∈Q in lowest terms with q>0: ap/q:=(a1/q)p=(qa​)p.

This requires a>0 (or careful handling for even q).

2.3 Real Exponents — The Honest Definition

The extension to irrational exponents requires real analysis. There are two clean approaches:

Approach 1 (Dedekind cut / supremum). For irrational x and a>1, define: ax:=supar∣r∈Q,,r<x. This works because ar:r∈Q,r<x is bounded above (since as>ar for rational s>r, and we can find rational s>x).

Approach 2 (via ex and ln, preferred). First define ex via its power series (§10), then define ln as its inverse, then: ax:=exlna.

Personal note: Approach 2 is used in Stewart §7.3 and is the standard in analysis courses. It converts all questions about ax into questions about ex and ln, which are cleaner to handle. I’ll develop ex first, then return to ax in §8.

3. The Number e — Three Equivalent Definitions

The number e≈2.71828… is one of the most important constants in mathematics. Here are three equivalent characterizations — each illuminates a different facet.

Definition 1 (Limit / Compound Interest)

e:=limn→∞​(1+n1​)n

Origin: Continuously compounded interest. If $$1isinvestedat100%annualinterest,compoundedntimesperyear,thevalueafter1yearis(1 + 1/n)^n.Asn \to \infty(continuouscompounding),thelimitise$.

Proof that the limit exists. Let an​=(1+1/n)n. Taking logarithms: \ln a_n = n\ln!\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right).$ Let $h = 1/n \to 0$: \ln a_n = \frac{\ln(1+h)}{h} \to \frac{d}{dh}\ln(1+h)\Big|{h=0} = \frac{1}{1+h}\Big|{h=0} = 1.$$ So lnan​→1, i.e., an​→e1=e. □

Definition 2 (The Integral Definition of ln)

Define first: lnx:=∫1x​t1​,dt(x>0), then e is the unique number satisfying lne=1, i.e.: ∫1e​t1​,dt=1.

This is the MIT 18.02 / Apostol approach. It has the virtue of being constructive — we define ln by a definite integral and derive all its properties from that definition, then define ex as the inverse.

Definition 3 (Power Series / ODE)

ex:=∑n=0∞​n!xn​=1+x+2!x2​+3!x3​+⋯

Setting x=1: e=1+1+1/2+1/6+1/24+⋯

Equivalently: ex is the unique solution to the initial value problem: dxdy​=y,y(0)=1.

The defining property of ex is that it equals its own derivative. Every other property follows from this.

5. The Natural Exponential ex — The Calculus Perspective

5.1 Differentiation

dxd​ex=ex​

Proof via the limit definition: dxd​ex=limh→0​hex+h−ex​=exlimh→0​heh−1​.

We need h→0lim​heh−1​=1.

Using the series definition: eh=1+h+h2/2+⋯, so: heh−1​=1+2h​+6h2​+⋯→1 as h→0.□

Alternatively, this limit is exactly dhd​eh​h=0​=e0=1 by definition of the derivative.

5.2 Chain Rule

dxd​ef(x)=ef(x)⋅f′(x)

Examples: dxd​ex2=2xex2 dxd​e−x=−e−x dxd​esinx=esinxcosx

5.3 Integration

∫ex,dx=ex+C

∫eax+b,dx=a1​eax+b+C

Important integral (Gaussian): ∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π​

Proof uses polar coordinates in R2 — a famous technique from MIT 18.02: Let I=∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx. Then: I2=∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​e−(x2+y2),dx,dy. Convert to polar coordinates x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, dx,dy=r,dr,dθ: I2=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2r,dr,dθ=2π⋅21​=π. Therefore I=π​. □

Deep importance: e−x2 is the shape of the Gaussian (normal) distribution. The factor 1/π​ (or 1/2π​ after rescaling) is the normalizing constant that makes it a probability distribution. This is central to probabilistic ML.

Key Geometric Facts

  1. The y-intercept is always 1: a0=1 for any base a>0.
  2. Tangent line at the origin for ex: Since (ex)′=ex and e0=1, the tangent at x=0 is y=1+x — the best linear approximation.
  3. Horizontal asymptote: limx→−∞​ex=0. The x-axis is a horizontal asymptote from the left.
  4. No zeros: ex>0 always. The graph never touches the x-axis.
  5. Concavity: (ex)′′=ex>0 everywhere — ex is always concave up.

The symbol ≪ means “grows strictly slower” (formally: the ratio tends to 0 as x→∞).

8. General Exponentials ax and Change of Base

Definition via e

For a>0: ax=exlna.

Derivative

dxd​ax=dxd​exlna=exlna⋅lna=axlna.

So ex is special because lne=1 — the factor lna vanishes to 1, giving (ex)′=ex.

Integral

∫ax,dx=lnaax​+C(a=1).

Change of Base Formula

loga​x=lnalnx​.

Proof: Let y=loga​x, so ay=x. Taking ln: ylna=lnx, so y=lnalnx​. □

Consequence: All logarithms are scalar multiples of ln. In pure mathematics, we only ever need ln.

9. Exponential Growth and Decay Models

The Fundamental ODE

The equation dtdy​=ky (rate of change proportional to current value) has unique solution:

y(t)=y0​ekt​

where y0​=y(0) is the initial value.

Proof: Separating variables: ydy​=k,dt⟹ln∣y∣=kt+C1​⟹y=eC1​ekt=Cekt. Setting t=0: y0​=C. □

Doubling Time and Half-Life

Doubling time (for k>0): Time T such that y(T)=2y0​: 2y0​=y0​ekT⟹kT=ln2⟹T=kln2​.

Half-life (for k<0, write k=−λ, λ>0): Time τ such that y(τ)=y0​/2: τ=λln2​.

Examples

PhenomenonModelk or λ
Radioactive decayN(t)=N0​e−λtλ>0
Population growthP(t)=P0​ertr>0
Newton’s law of coolingT(t)−T∞​=(T0​−T∞​)e−ktk>0
Continuously compounded interestA(t)=Pertr= interest rate
RC circuit dischargeV(t)=V0​e−t/RCλ=1/RC

Example — Carbon-14 Dating

14C has half-life τ=5730 years, so λ=5730ln2​.

If a sample has 60 of its original 14C remaining: 0.6=e−λt⟹t=λ−ln(0.6)​=ln2ln(5/3)⋅5730​≈4100 years.

10. Taylor Series of ex

ex=n=0∑∞​n!xn​=1+x+2!x2​+3!x3​+⋯​

Derivation. The n-th derivative of ex is ex, so at x=0: f(n)(0)=1 for all n. Taylor’s formula gives: ex=∑n=0∞​n!f(n)(0)​xn=∑n=0∞​n!xn​.

Convergence. By the ratio test: limn→∞​​xn/n!xn+1/(n+1)!​​=limn→∞​n+1∣x∣​=0<1. Convergent for all x∈R (and indeed all z∈C). □

Important Special Cases

e=∑n=0∞​n!1​=1+1+21​+61​+241​+⋯≈2.71828

e−1=∑n=0∞​n!(−1)n​=1−1+21​−61​+⋯=e1​≈0.36788

11. Complex Exponential — Full Circle with Euler

For z=x+iy∈C, define ez by the same power series: ez=∑n=0∞​n!zn​.

The series converges absolutely for all z∈C.

Euler’s Formula (derived in the Trigonometric Functions note): eiy=cosy+isiny.

General formula: ex+iy=ex(cosy+isiny).

This means ∣ex+iy∣=ex (modulus) and arg(ex+iy)=y (argument).

Polar Form of Complex Numbers

Every nonzero z∈C can be written: z=reiθ,r=∣z∣,θ=argz.

Multiplication becomes: z1​z2​=r1​r2​ei(θ1​+θ2​) — multiply moduli, add arguments.

De Moivre’s Theorem

(eiθ)n=einθ⟹(cosθ+isinθ)n=cos(nθ)+isin(nθ).

Application. Express cos(3θ) in terms of cosθ: (cosθ+isinθ)3=cos3θ−3cosθsin2θ+i(⋯). Taking real part: cos(3θ)=cos3θ−3cosθ(1−cos2θ)=4cos3θ−3cosθ.

12. Hyperbolic Functions

The hyperbolic functions arise from splitting ex into even and odd parts:

coshx=2ex+e−x​(even part of ex) sinhx=2ex−e−x​(odd part of ex) tanhx=coshxsinhx​=ex+e−xex−e−x​

The Hyperbolic Identity

cosh2x−sinh2x=1

Proof: (2ex+e−x​)2−(2ex−e−x​)2=4(ex+e−x)2−(ex−e−x)2​=44​=1.□

Compare with cos2+sin2=1. The point (cosht,sinht) lies on the unit hyperbola x2−y2=1, just as (cost,sint) lies on the unit circle.

Derivatives

(coshx)′=sinhx,(sinhx)′=coshx,(tanhx)′=1−tanh2x=sech2x.

Note the absence of minus signs — unlike trig functions! The derivative cycle cosh→sinh→cosh has no sign flip.

Connection to Trig

cos(ix)=cosh(x),sin(ix)=isinh(x).

This is why hyperbolic and circular trig functions share identical identity structures — they are the same function, one evaluated at real arguments, one at imaginary arguments.

13. Connections to AI / ML / Deep Learning

Exponential functions are perhaps the most pervasive mathematical object in machine learning. They are not incidental — they appear because of deep structural reasons.

13.1 Activation Functions: Softmax

The softmax function maps a vector z=(z1​,…,zK​)∈RK to a probability distribution:

softmax(z)i​=∑j=1K​ezj​ezi​​

Why exponentials? We need a function R→(0,∞) that:

  1. Is always positive (to be a valid probability)
  2. Is differentiable everywhere
  3. Preserves order (zi​>zj​⇒outputi​>outputj​)
  4. Amplifies differences (makes the distribution “sharp”)

ex is the canonical choice satisfying all four properties. Moreover, softmax is the gradient of the log-sum-exp function: LSE(z)=ln!(∑i=1K​ezi​),∇z​,LSE(z)=softmax(z).

Numerical stability: In practice, softmax(z)i​=∑j​ezj​−maxj​zj​ezi​−maxj​zj​​ — subtracting the maximum prevents overflow of ezi​ for large zi​.

13.2 The Sigmoid as a Special Case

The sigmoid is softmax for K=2: σ(x)=ex+e0ex​=ex+1ex​=1+e−x1​.

Properties flowing from ex:

  • σ′(x)=σ(x)(1−σ(x)) — proved by differentiating 1+e−x1​.
  • σ(−x)=1−σ(x) — symmetry around (0,1/2).
  • σ(x)∈(0,1) always — maps all of R into the open unit interval.

The vanishing gradient problem occurs because σ′(x)→0 exponentially fast as ∣x∣→∞ — the gradient saturates because e−x→0 or e−x→∞ rapidly.

13.3 Exponential Family Distributions

The exponential family unifies nearly all common probability distributions used in ML:

p(x∣η)=h(x)exp!(η⊤T(x)−A(η))

where:

  • η: natural parameters
  • T(x): sufficient statistics
  • A(η)=ln∫h(x)exp(η⊤T(x)),dx: log-partition function (normalizing constant)
DistributionFits into exponential family
Gaussian N(μ,σ2)Yes
BernoulliYes
PoissonYes
GammaYes
DirichletYes
Student-tNo

Why this matters: Exponential family distributions have:

  • Conjugate priors (Bayesian inference is analytically tractable)
  • Sufficient statistics of fixed dimension
  • A(η) is convex — guarantees unique MLE

Personal note: The log-partition function A(η) is a cumulant generating function. Its gradient is E[T(x)] and its Hessian is Cov[T(x)] — this is where all the properties of Gaussian and Bernoulli mean-variance relationships come from. The exponential is not optional here; it is the structural necessity.

13.4 Cross-Entropy Loss and Maximum Likelihood

For a classification problem with K classes, the cross-entropy loss is: L=−∑i=1K​yi​lnp^​i​=−lnp^​ytrue​​, where p^​i​=softmax(z)i​.

This is the negative log-likelihood under a categorical distribution: L=−ln∑j​ezj​ezytrue​​​=ln!(∑j​ezj​)−zytrue​​=LSE(z)−zytrue​​.

Gradient: ∂zi​∂L​=softmax(z)i​−1[i=ytrue​].

This clean gradient (prediction minus truth) comes entirely from the exponential’s differentiation property.

13.5 Attention Mechanism in Transformers

The scaled dot-product attention in a Transformer:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QK⊤​)V

The softmax converts raw dot-product scores into attention weights. Each row of the attention matrix is a probability distribution over positions.

Why 1/dk​​? For large dk​, dot products grow as O(dk​), pushing softmax into saturation (exponentially small gradients). Dividing by dk​​ keeps inputs in the linear regime of softmax.

The exponential does the heavy lifting: Large logit differences get exponentially amplified into nearly-one-hot attention distributions. This is the mechanism by which the model “focuses” sharply on specific tokens.

13.6 Learning Rate Schedules

Exponential decay: ηt​=η0​⋅γt=η0​⋅etlnγ,0<γ<1.

Cosine annealing with warm restarts (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) combines trig and exponential. The base schedule: ηt​=ηmin​+21​(ηmax​−ηmin​)(1+cos!(Ti​Tcur​​π)).

Why exponential decay? The loss landscape near a local minimum is approximately quadratic. Newton’s method converges in one step for quadratics, but gradient descent converges geometrically — at rate rt for some ∣r∣<1. This geometric convergence is exponential decay.

13.7 Batch Normalization and Layer Normalization

The softplus function is a smooth approximation to ReLU: softplus(x)=ln(1+ex).

Note: softplus′(x)=σ(x) (sigmoid). The exponential inside creates smooth behavior at x=0 while recovering linear behavior for large ∣x∣.

13.8 Natural Gradient and Fisher Information

The Fisher information matrix for a model pθ​: F(θ)=Epθ​​![∇θ​lnpθ​(x),(∇θ​lnpθ​(x))⊤].

The natural gradient replaces the standard gradient ∇L with F(θ)−1∇L, adapting to the geometry of the probability simplex.

For exponential family models, F(θ)=∇2A(θ) — the Hessian of the log-partition function. This means the natural gradient is geometrically natural in the space of probability distributions — curvature comes entirely from the exponential structure of the model.

13.9 Generative Models: VAEs and Diffusion

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs): The ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund): LELBO​=Eqϕ​(z∣x)​[lnpθ​(x∣z)]−DKL​(qϕ​(z∣x)∣p(z)).

The KL divergence between Gaussians (both exponential family): DKL​(N(μ,σ2)∣N(0,1))=21​(μ2+σ2−lnσ2−1).

The lnσ2 term comes from the log-normalizing constant of the Gaussian — a direct consequence of ∫e−x2,dx=π​.

Diffusion Models: The forward process adds Gaussian noise: q(xt​∣x0​)=N(xt​;,αˉt​​,x0​,,(1−αˉt​)I),αˉt​=∏s=1t​αs​=e∑s=1t​lnαs​.

The product of exponential decay factors is an exponential — the noise schedule is fundamentally an exponential function.
