0. Why Polynomials? A Personal Note

Before we dive in, I want to say something that textbooks rarely bother with: polynomials are the canonical approximation object of mathematics. Everything from Taylor series to neural network activation functions, from numerical integration to loss landscapes — if you peel away the sophistication, there’s a polynomial underneath. Learning them rigorously is not a detour. It is the road.

1. Definition and Anatomy

1.1 Formal Definition

Definition. A polynomial of degree n over a field F (typically R or C) is a function p:F→F of the form p(x)=an​xn+an−1​xn−1+⋯+a1​x+a0​=∑k=0n​ak​xk where ak​∈F for all k, and an​=0. The ak​ are called coefficients, an​ is the leading coefficient, and a0​ is the constant term.

Pedantic note: The zero polynomial p(x)=0 is special — it has no well-defined degree (some authors assign degree −∞ or −1). This matters in polynomial ring theory.

1.3 The Space of Polynomials Pn​

The set of all polynomials of degree ≤n forms a vector space Pn​ over R, with basis 1,x,x2,…,xn. This basis — called the monomial basis — has dimension n+1.

Remark (MIT 18.02 flavor). This is the same linear algebra you use for vectors in Rn+1, but the “vectors” are now functions. Recognizing polynomials as elements of a vector space is the first step toward functional analysis and, ultimately, neural function spaces.

2. Algebraic Properties

2.1 Ring Structure

Polynomials form a commutative ring R[x] under ordinary addition and multiplication.

Addition: (p+q)(x)=∑k​(ak​+bk​)xk — add coefficients termwise.

Multiplication (Cauchy product): (p⋅q)(x)=∑k=0m+n​(∑j=0k​aj​bk−j​)xk

Fact. deg(p⋅q)=deg(p)+deg(q).

Proof sketch: The leading term of p⋅q is an​bm​xn+m; since an​=0 and bm​=0, this term is nonzero, so the degree is exactly n+m. □

2.2 Division Algorithm

Theorem (Polynomial Division). For any p,d∈R[x] with d=0, there exist unique polynomials q,r∈R[x] such that p(x)=d(x)⋅q(x)+r(x),deg(r)<deg(d)

This mirrors integer division: p is the dividend, d the divisor, q the quotient, and r the remainder.

Example. Divide p(x)=x3−2x+1 by d(x)=x−1:

So x3−2x+1=(x−1)(x2+x−1)+0. Here r=0, so (x−1) divides p exactly — this means x=1 is a root.

2.3 Remainder and Factor Theorems

Theorem (Remainder). When p(x) is divided by (x−c), the remainder is p(c).

Proof. By the division algorithm, p(x)=(x−c)q(x)+r where r is a constant (since deg(r)<deg(x−c)=1). Substituting x=c: p(c)=0⋅q(c)+r=r. □

Corollary (Factor Theorem). (x−c) divides p(x) ⟺ p(c)=0, i.e., c is a root of p.

3. Roots and the Fundamental Theorem

3.1 The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Theorem (FTA). Every non-constant polynomial p∈C[x] of degree n has exactly n roots in C, counted with multiplicity.

Consequence. Over C: p(x)=an​(x−r1​)m1​(x−r2​)m2​⋯(x−rk​)mk​,m1​+m2​+⋯+mk​=n

Over R, complex roots come in conjugate pairs, so the real factorization uses irreducible quadratics.

Proof (sketch, Liouville’s theorem route). If p had no root in C, then 1/p(z) would be entire and bounded (since ∣p(z)∣→∞ as ∣z∣→∞), hence constant by Liouville’s theorem — contradiction. □

3.2 Multiplicity

Definition. c is a root of p of multiplicity m if (x−c)m∣p(x) but (x−c)m+1∤p(x).

Geometric interpretation:

  • Multiplicity 1: p crosses the x-axis at c.
  • Multiplicity 2: p touches (is tangent to) the x-axis at c.
  • Multiplicity ≥3 (odd): crosses with a flatter profile.
  • Multiplicity ≥3 (even): touches and flattens.

4. Calculus of Polynomials

4.1 Differentiation

Power Rule. For p(x)=∑k=0n​ak​xk:

p′(x)=∑k=1n​k⋅ak​xk−1=nan​xn−1+(n−1)an−1​xn−2+⋯+a1​

Proof. By definition, dxd​[xk]=limh→0​h(x+h)k−xk​. Expanding via the binomial theorem: (x+h)k=xk+kxk−1h+O(h2). Dividing by h and taking the limit gives kxk−1. □

Observation. Differentiation maps Pn​→Pn−1​ — it is a linear map that reduces degree by 1. This has profound consequences in differential equations and operator theory.

4.3 Critical Points and Shape

For p′(c)=0: c is a critical point. The second derivative test:

  • p′′(c)>0⇒ local minimum
  • p′′(c)<0⇒ local maximum
  • p′′(c)=0⇒ inconclusive (higher-order test needed)

End behavior: Governed entirely by the leading term an​xn.

n even, an​>0Both ends →+∞“U shape”
n even, an​<0Both ends →−∞”∩ shape”
n odd, an​>0Left →−∞, Right →+∞Rising
n odd, an​<0Left →+∞, Right →−∞Falling

5. Taylor Polynomials — The Deep Connection

5.1 The Taylor Polynomial

Definition. The n-th degree Taylor polynomial of f centered at a is Tn​(x)=∑k=0n​k!f(k)(a)​(x−a)k

This is the unique polynomial of degree ≤n that matches f and all its first n derivatives at a.

Theorem (Taylor’s Theorem with Lagrange Remainder, Stewart §11.10). If f is (n+1)-times differentiable on an interval containing a and x, then f(x)=Tn​(x)+Rn​(x),Rn​(x)=(n+1)!f(n+1)(c)​(x−a)n+1 for some c between a and x.

Proof sketch: Apply the Mean Value Theorem repeatedly — formally, integrate by parts n times starting from f(x)=f(a)+∫ax​f′(t),dt. Each integration introduces a term matching a Taylor coefficient. □

5.2 Taylor Polynomial as a Polynomial Approximation

The message of Taylor’s theorem: locally, every smooth function looks like a polynomial. This is not a metaphor — it is a precise quantitative statement.

Example. Approximate sin(x) near x=0:

T1​(x)=x T3​(x)=x−6x3​ T5​(x)=x−6x3​+120x5​

         sin(x)
    ─────────────────
    Near x=0, T₁ is fine.
    Need T₃ for x ∈ [-π/2, π/2].
    Need T₅ for x ∈ [-π, π].
    The polynomial "fills in" more of the curve.

Error bound: ∣Rn​(x)∣≤(n+1)!M​∣x−a∣n+1 where M=sup∣f(n+1)∣. Note the factorial in the denominator — this is why Taylor polynomials converge so fast.

6. Special Polynomial Families

6.1 Legendre Polynomials Pn​(x)

Defined on [−1,1], orthogonal under the inner product ⟨f,g⟩=∫−11​f(x)g(x),dx.

P0​=1,P1​=x,P2​=21​(3x2−1),P3​=21​(5x3−3x)

Why care? They are the optimal basis for approximation in L2[−1,1] — analogous to Fourier series for periodic functions.

6.2 Chebyshev Polynomials Tn​(x)

Defined by Tn​(cosθ)=cos(nθ). They minimize the maximum error for polynomial interpolation.

T0​=1,T1​=x,T2​=2x2−1,T3​=4x3−3x

Key property. Among all monic polynomials of degree n, 2n−11​Tn​(x) has the smallest sup-norm on [−1,1]. This is the minimax optimality property.

6.3 Bernstein Polynomials Bn,k​(x)

Bn,k​(x)=(kn​)xk(1−x)n−k,x∈[0,1]

Weierstrass Approximation Theorem (1885). Every continuous function on [a,b] can be uniformly approximated by polynomials. Bernstein’s constructive proof: pn​(x)=∑k=0n​f(k/n)Bn,k​(x)→f(x) uniformly.

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is where the abstract becomes urgently practical.

8.1 Polynomial Regression: The Simplest Model

Setup. Given data (xi​,yi​)i=1N​, fit a polynomial: y^​=w0​+w1​x+w2​x2+⋯+wd​xd

The design matrix is: Φ=(1​x1​​x12​​⋯​x1d​ 1​x2​​x22​​⋯​x2d​ ⋮​​​​⋮ 1​xN​​xN2​​⋯​xNd​​)

Minimizing MSE loss: L(w)=∣Φw−y∣2, the closed-form solution is w∗=(ΦTΦ)−1ΦTy.

Key tension:

  • Low d: underfitting — the polynomial lacks capacity.
  • High d: overfitting — the polynomial memorizes noise.

This is the bias-variance tradeoff in its purest form. Regularization (ridge, LASSO) penalizes large coefficients, enforcing smoothness — again, a polynomial story.

8.2 Universal Approximation and the Weierstrass Theorem

The Universal Approximation Theorem for neural networks (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991) says: a single hidden layer network with enough neurons can approximate any continuous function on a compact set.

The deep connection: Weierstrass’s theorem (1885) says the same thing about polynomials. Neural networks are, in a sense, the nonlinear generalization of polynomial approximation — trading the fixed polynomial basis 1,x,x2,… for a learned, adaptive basis of sigmoidal functions.

The polynomial is the intellectual ancestor of the neural network.

8.3 Taylor Approximations in Optimization

Gradient Descent is a first-order method. It replaces the loss L(θ) with its linear Taylor approximation and takes a step in the steepest descent direction: θt+1​=θt​−η∇L(θt​)≈θt​−η∂θ∂T1​​

Newton’s Method (second-order optimization) uses the quadratic Taylor approximation: L(θ)≈L(θt​)+∇L⊤δ+21​δ⊤Hδ

Minimizing in δ: θt+1​=θt​−H−1∇L, where H is the Hessian.

Adam, RMSProp — all adaptive optimizers can be viewed as approximate second-order methods that implicitly capture curvature information, i.e., they approximate the quadratic polynomial T2​ of the loss.

In homomorphic encryption (privacy-preserving ML), all activations must be exact polynomials because only polynomial operations are supported over encrypted data.

8.5 Spectral Methods and Graph Neural Networks

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) — especially spectral GNNs — define convolutions on graphs via polynomials of the graph Laplacian L:

Signal filtering: gθ​(L)=∑k=0K​θk​Lk

This is literally a polynomial in the Laplacian matrix. ChebNet uses Chebyshev polynomials for this filter; GCN (Kipf & Welling 2017) uses a first-degree polynomial T1​. The entire spectral GNN literature is polynomial approximation theory on graphs.

8.6 Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)

At initialization, infinitely wide neural networks behave as kernel machines with the Neural Tangent Kernel. As width →∞, training dynamics reduce to a linear system — a degree-1 polynomial approximation of the parameter space. The loss converges as: L(t)=L(0)e−λmin​(Θ)t where Θ is the NTK matrix. The polynomial structure of the model is what makes this linearization tractable.
