01.3D Coordinate System

01.3D Coordinate System

Apr 22, 202616 min read

“The introduction of coordinates is an act of violence.” — Hermann Weyl

And yet — what beautiful violence it is.

§0. Why Bother? A Note Before We Begin

Before I write a single formula, let me say this plainly: the 3D coordinate system is not merely a bookkeeping device. It is the lingua franca of modern science and engineering. Every point in a neural network’s weight space, every pixel in a rendered scene, every atom in a protein fold — all of them live in some version of what we’re about to build. If you want to understand deep learning at a non-superficial level, you must be fluent here. Not acquainted. Fluent.

§1. The Construction: Building R3 from Scratch

1.1 The Three Mutually Perpendicular Axes

Fix a point O in space, called the origin. Through O, draw three lines, each perpendicular to the other two. Label them the x-axis, y-axis, and z-axis.

The right-hand rule (non-negotiable convention): Point your right hand’s fingers along the positive x-axis and curl them toward the positive y-axis. Your thumb points in the direction of the positive z-axis.

        z
        |
        |
        |_______ y
       /
      /
     x

This defines a right-handed coordinate system. Left-handed systems exist (and are used in some graphics APIs like DirectX), but in mathematics and physics, right-handed is the default. Confusing them is a classical source of sign errors.

1.2 The Three Coordinate Planes

The three axes determine three planes:

PlaneContainsEquation
xy-planex-axis and y-axisz=0
yz-planey-axis and z-axisx=0
xz-planex-axis and z-axisy=0

These three planes divide all of R3 into eight octants. The first octant is where x>0, y>0, and z>0. Unlike in 2D (where the four quadrants are labeled I–IV), the octants in 3D have no standard numbering — only the first octant is universally named.

1.3 Coordinates as an Isomorphism

Every point P in 3D space corresponds to a unique ordered triple (a,b,c)∈R3, where:

  • a = signed distance from the yz-plane (along the x-axis)
  • b = signed distance from the xz-plane (along the y-axis)
  • c = signed distance from the xy-plane (along the z-axis)

This correspondence P↔(a,b,c) is a bijection. It is the content of the Cartesian project: geometry ↔ algebra.

Example 1.1. The point P=(2,−3,5): start at the origin, move 2 units in the positive x-direction, 3 units in the negative y-direction, then 5 units in the positive z-direction. You have arrived at P.

Example 1.2. Where is Q=(0,4,0)? On the y-axis, 4 units above the origin (in the y-direction). All points of the form (0,b,0) lie on the y-axis.

§2. Distance in R3

2.1 The Distance Formula

Theorem (Distance Formula). The distance between P1​=(x1​,y1​,z1​) and P2​=(x2​,y2​,z2​) is:

∣P1​P2​∣=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2+(z2​−z1​)2​

Proof. Apply the Pythagorean theorem twice. The horizontal displacement (in the xy-plane) from P1​ to the point (x2​,y2​,z1​) directly beneath P2​ has length dxy​=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​. Then the distance from P1​ to P2​ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs dxy​ and ∣z2​−z1​∣:

∣P1​P2​∣2=dxy2​+(z2​−z1​)2=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2+(z2​−z1​)2

Taking square roots gives the result. ■

Example 2.1. Distance from A=(1,2,−1) to B=(3,0,4): ∣AB∣=(3−1)2+(0−2)2+(4−(−1))2​=4+4+25​=33​

2.2 The Midpoint Formula

The midpoint M of the segment P1​P2​ is:

M=(2x1​+x2​​,,2y1​+y2​​,,2z1​+z2​​)

This is immediate: midpoint coordinates are arithmetic means. The proof is just the 1D midpoint formula applied coordinate-by-coordinate.

§3. The Equation of a Sphere

3.1 Derivation

A sphere with center C=(h,k,l) and radius r>0 is the set of all points equidistant from C:

(x,y,z)∈R3:(x−h)2+(y−k)2+(z−l)2​=r

Squaring both sides:

(x−h)2+(y−k)2+(z−l)2=r2​

This is the standard form of the sphere equation. Think of it as the 3D analogue of the circle equation in R2.

3.2 Completing the Square (The Inverse Problem)

Given a general second-degree equation, recognize when it describes a sphere by completing the square in all three variables.

Example 3.1. Identify the surface: x2+y2+z2+4x−6y+2z+6=0

Group and complete the square: (x2+4x+4)+(y2−6y+9)+(z2+2z+1)=−6+4+9+1=8

(x+2)2+(y−3)2+(z+1)2=8

This is a sphere with center (−2,3,−1) and radius 8​=22​.

What can go wrong? If after completing the square the right-hand side is 0, you get a single point. If it’s negative, there are no real solutions — the “sphere” is imaginary.

§4. Vectors in R3

4.1 What Is a Vector?

A vector v is an object with both magnitude and direction. In coordinate form, we represent it as:

v=⟨v1​,v2​,v3​⟩

Distinguish carefully: the position vector of a point P=(a,b,c) is OP=⟨a,b,c⟩. The point is a location; the vector is a displacement. This distinction matters.

4.2 Vector Arithmetic

If u=⟨u1​,u2​,u3​⟩ and v=⟨v1​,v2​,v3​⟩, and c∈R:

u+v=⟨u1​+v1​,,u2​+v2​,,u3​+v3​⟩

cv=⟨cv1​,,cv2​,,cv3​⟩

∣v∣=v12​+v22​+v32​​

The unit vector in the direction of v (assuming v=0):

v^=∣v∣v​

4.3 Standard Basis Vectors

The three vectors:

i=⟨1,0,0⟩,j=⟨0,1,0⟩,k=⟨0,0,1⟩

form the standard basis for R3. Every vector decomposes uniquely:

v=v1​i+v2​j+v3​k

This is not just notation — it’s the statement that i,j,k spans R3 and is linearly independent. (You’ll make this precise in linear algebra, if you haven’t already.)

§5. The Dot Product

5.1 Definition and Geometric Interpretation

Definition. The dot product (inner product) of u and v is:

u⋅v=u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u3​v3​

Theorem. u⋅v=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ, where θ∈[0,π] is the angle between u and v.

Proof. Apply the law of cosines to the triangle formed by u, v, and u−v:

∣u−v∣2=∣u∣2+∣v∣2−2∣u∣∣v∣cosθ

Expanding the left side using the coordinate definition:

∣u−v∣2=(u1​−v1​)2+(u2​−v2​)2+(u3​−v3​)2=∣u∣2−2(u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u3​v3​)+∣v∣2

Comparing: u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u3​v3​=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ. ■

Corollary (Orthogonality Test). u⊥v⟺u⋅v=0.

5.2 Projection

The scalar projection of b onto a:

compa​b=∣a∣a⋅b​

The vector projection of b onto a:

proja​b=∣a∣2a⋅b​a

Example 5.1. Find the angle between u=⟨1,2,2⟩ and v=⟨0,1,−1⟩:

u⋅v=0+2−2=0

Since u⋅v=0, these vectors are perpendicular: θ=π/2.

§6. The Cross Product

6.1 Definition

The cross product u×v is defined only in R3 (and R7, but let’s not go there). It is computed via the formal determinant:

u×v=​i​j​k u1​​u2​​u3​ v1​​v2​​v3​​​

=(u2​v3​−u3​v2​)i−(u1​v3​−u3​v1​)j+(u1​v2​−u2​v1​)k

6.2 Geometric Meaning

Theorem. ∣u×v∣=∣u∣∣v∣sinθ, and u×v is orthogonal to both u and v, with direction given by the right-hand rule.

Immediate consequences:

  • ∣u×v∣ equals the area of the parallelogram spanned by u and v.
  • u∥v⟺u×v=0.

Anti-commutativity: u×v=−(v×u). The cross product is not commutative. Order matters — reversing it flips the sign (i.e., flips the direction of the resulting vector by 180°).

Example 6.1. u=⟨1,0,0⟩, v=⟨0,1,0⟩:

u×v=​i​j​k 1​0​0 0​1​0​​=(0−0)i−(0−0)j+(1−0)k=k

As expected: i×j=k. The right-hand rule confirms the direction.

§7. Lines and Planes in R3

7.1 Lines

A line through point P0​=(x0​,y0​,z0​) with direction vector d=⟨a,b,c⟩:

Vector equation: r(t)=r0​+td=⟨x0​+at,,y0​+bt,,z0​+ct⟩, t∈R

Parametric equations: x=x0​+at,y=y0​+bt,z=z0​+ct

Symmetric equations (if a,b,c=0): ax−x0​​=by−y0​​=cz−z0​​

If, say, c=0, then z=z0​ is a separate equation and the others form the symmetric form.

7.2 Planes

A plane through P0​=(x0​,y0​,z0​) with normal vector n=⟨a,b,c⟩:

n⋅(r−r0​)=0

a(x−x0​)+b(y−y0​)+c(z−z0​)=0

Or in the general (linear) form:

ax+by+cz+d=0

where d=−(ax0​+by0​+cz0​).

Key insight: The normal vector ⟨a,b,c⟩ is readable directly from the coefficients. This is one of the most useful facts in multivariable calculus — when you see a plane’s equation, you immediately know its normal direction.

Example 7.1. Find the equation of the plane through P(1,2,3), Q(2,3,1), R(3,1,2).

Find two vectors in the plane: PQ​=⟨1,1,−2⟩, PR=⟨2,−1,−1⟩

Normal: n=PQ​×PR=​i​j​k 1​1​−2 2​−1​−1​​

=(−1−2)i−(−1+4)j+(−1−2)k=⟨−3,−3,−3⟩

Simplify: n=⟨1,1,1⟩. Plane: (x−1)+(y−2)+(z−3)=0, i.e., x+y+z=6.

7.3 Distance from a Point to a Plane

For the plane ax+by+cz+d=0 and point P1​=(x1​,y1​,z1​):

D=a2+b2+c2​∣ax1​+by1​+cz1​+d∣​

This formula is the 3D analogue of the familiar 2D point-to-line distance formula.

§8. Cylinders and Quadric Surfaces

8.1 Cylinders

A cylinder in 3D is a surface generated by lines (rulings) parallel to a given axis, passing through a curve (the directrix) in a coordinate plane. The key observation:

An equation in R3 that lacks one variable defines a cylinder whose rulings are parallel to that variable’s axis.

EquationSurfaceAxis
x2+y2=r2circular cylinderz-axis
y=x2parabolic cylinderz-axis
a2x2​+c2z2​=1elliptic cylindery-axis

Example 8.1. z=sin(x) in R3: a sinusoidal cylinder extending infinitely in the y-direction. No matter what y is, the cross-section in any plane y=k is the curve z=sin(x).

8.2 Quadric Surfaces

These are the 3D analogues of conic sections — defined by second-degree equations in x,y,z. The six canonical types:

SurfaceStandard EquationNotes
Ellipsoida2x2​+b2y2​+c2z2​=1All traces are ellipses; sphere if a=b=c
Elliptic Paraboloidz=a2x2​+b2y2​Opens upward; vertex at origin
Hyperbolic Paraboloidz=b2y2​−a2x2​Saddle shape; critical point is a saddle
Hyperboloid (1 sheet)a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=1Connected; “hourglass without the pinch”
Hyperboloid (2 sheets)−a2x2​−b2y2​+c2z2​=1Two disconnected pieces
Elliptic Conea2x2​+b2y2​=c2z2​Two nappes meeting at origin

How to identify: Find the traces — cross-sections with coordinate planes and planes parallel to them. The pattern of traces (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) identifies the surface type.

Example 8.2. Identify 4x2+y2−z2=4.

Rewrite: x2+4y2​−4z2​=1. This is a hyperboloid of one sheet with a=1, b=2, c=2.

Trace in z=k: x2+4y2​=1+4k2​ — ellipses for all k.

Trace in x=0: 4y2​−4z2​=1 — a hyperbola.

§9. Coordinate Systems Beyond Cartesian

9.1 Cylindrical Coordinates (r,θ,z)

Essentially polar coordinates in the xy-plane, plus z unchanged.

Conversion: x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ,z=z r=x2+y2​,θ=arctan!(xy​),z=z

Useful when the geometry has rotational symmetry about the z-axis.

Example 9.1. The cylinder x2+y2=9 becomes r=3 in cylindrical coordinates. Far simpler.

9.2 Spherical Coordinates (ρ,θ,ϕ)

  • ρ≥0: distance from origin
  • θ∈[0,2π): azimuthal angle in xy-plane (same as cylindrical)
  • ϕ∈[0,π]: polar angle from positive z-axis (colatitude)

Conversion: x=ρsinϕcosθ,y=ρsinϕsinθ,z=ρcosϕ ρ=x2+y2+z2​

Warning on notation: Physics often swaps θ and ϕ relative to mathematics. Always check the convention in your source.

Example 9.2. The sphere x2+y2+z2=a2 becomes ρ=a. The upper hemisphere z≥0 is ϕ∈[0,π/2], ρ=a.

§10. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This section deserves serious treatment, not a footnote. Let me be precise.

10.1 Weight Space as Rn: The Fundamental Analogy

Every neural network’s parameters — weights and biases — can be concatenated into a single vector w∈Rn, where n can be in the billions for large language models. The 3D coordinate system is the kindergarten version of this.

When you understand that:

  • A point in R3 is (x,y,z)
  • Moving from P1​ to P2​ is a displacement vector v=P2​−P1​

…you have already internalized the idea that:

  • A set of model parameters is a point in Rn
  • Gradient descent is a displacement −α∇w​L(w) in that high-dimensional space

10.2 The Loss Surface as a Hypersurface

The loss function L:Rn→R defines a hypersurface in Rn+1 — the exact analogue of a surface z=f(x,y) in R3.

Concepts from 3D geometry map directly:

3D GeometryDeep Learning
z=f(x,y), a surfaceL(w), the loss landscape
Local minimum: ∇f=0, fxx​,fyy​>0Converged model: ∇w​L=0
Saddle point (hyperbolic paraboloid shape)Saddle point in loss landscape (common, problematic)
Gradient ∇f=⟨fx​,fy​⟩ points uphillGradient ∇w​L points toward higher loss
Normal to a surfaceGradient of constraint in constrained optimization

The hyperbolic paraboloid (z=y2−x2) is literally the shape of a saddle point in a 2D parameter space. In deep learning, saddle points (not local minima) are believed to be the dominant critical points in high-dimensional loss landscapes. Understanding their geometry is active research.

10.3 Dot Products: The Engine of Neural Networks

Every neuron in a feedforward layer computes:

a=σ(w⋅x+b)=σ(w1​x1​+w2​x2​+⋯+wn​xn​+b)

This is a dot product. The geometric meaning: w⋅x=∣w∣∣x∣cosθ, where θ is the angle between the weight vector and input vector. The neuron activates strongly when the input is aligned with the weight direction. This is the 3D intuition, lifted to n dimensions.

Attention in Transformers: The attention score between query q and key k is:

score(q,k)=dk​​q⋅k​

This is exactly a scaled dot product — measuring angular similarity in Rdk​. The dk​​ normalization prevents dot products from becoming too large in high dimensions (where random vectors tend to have larger dot products).

10.4 Distance and Similarity in Feature Space

In many ML tasks, we embed data points into Rn (feature space / embedding space) and use distance to measure similarity. The Euclidean distance formula from §2 is the default:

d(x,y)=∣x−y∣=∑i=1n​(xi​−yi​)2​

Used in:

  • k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN): classify by the k closest training points
  • k-Means Clustering: assign points to nearest centroid
  • Word Embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe): semantic similarity is geometric proximity. The famous example: King​−Man+Woman≈Queen​ — vector arithmetic in a high-dimensional analogue of R3.

Cosine similarity (preferred when magnitude is irrelevant):

sim(u,v)=∣u∣∣v∣u⋅v​=cosθ

This is exactly the geometric content of the dot product theorem from §5.1, applied at scale.

10.5 Cross Products and Normal Vectors in 3D ML

In 3D deep learning (point cloud processing, 3D object detection, robotics), cross products are used directly:

  • Computing surface normals for 3D mesh learning
  • PointNet and its successors process point clouds in R3, requiring 3D geometry fluency
  • SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) relies on rigid body transformations encoded as rotation matrices and cross products

10.6 Planes as Decision Boundaries

A linear classifier in Rn defines a hyperplane — the n-dimensional analogue of a plane in R3:

w⋅x+b=0

Compare with §7.2: ax+by+cz+d=0. Same structure. The vector w is the normal to this hyperplane; classification is determined by which side of the hyperplane x falls on.

Support Vector Machines (SVM) explicitly maximize the margin (minimum distance) from training points to this hyperplane — using precisely the distance formula from §7.3.

The distance from a support vector xi​ to the decision boundary is:

D=∣w∣∣w⋅xi​+b∣​

Recognize this? It is the point-to-plane distance formula, in n dimensions.

Every abstraction in deep learning — loss surface, attention mechanism, embedding space — is a generalization of something you learned in 3D. The 3D coordinate system is not a stepping stone you leave behind. It is the intuitive foundation on which all of high-dimensional geometry rests.
