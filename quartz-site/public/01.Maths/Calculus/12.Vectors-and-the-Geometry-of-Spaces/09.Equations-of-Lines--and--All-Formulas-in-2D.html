0. Why Lines Matter (A Personal Note)

Before we get formal: lines are not a trivial topic dressed up for introductory students. They are:

  • The local approximation of every differentiable function (tangent line = first-order Taylor)
  • The building block of linear regression — the workhorse of supervised learning
  • The 1D slice of a hyperplane, which is the decision boundary in SVMs and logistic regression
  • The directional derivative along a line encodes gradient information in multivariable calculus

So when you’re computing the gradient of a loss function in PyTorch, you are — whether you know it or not — working with the same slope formula you’ll prove below.

1. The Coordinate Plane: Setting the Stage

Let R2 denote the Euclidean plane with the standard orthonormal basis e1​,e2​. A point is an ordered pair P=(x,y).

         y

Every geometric object in the plane is ultimately a set of points satisfying some algebraic condition. A line is the simplest such object: a degree-1 algebraic condition.

2. Slope: The Central Character

2.1 Definition

Definition. Let P1​=(x1​,y1​) and P2​=(x2​,y2​) be two distinct points with x1​=x2​. The slope of the line through P1​ and P2​ is:

m=ΔxΔy​=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​

Geometric meaning: m measures the rise per unit run — how much y changes for a unit change in x.

Example. P1​=(1,2), P2​=(4,8): m=4−18−2​=36​=2

2.2 Slope is Well-Defined (it doesn’t depend on which two points you choose)

Lemma. If P1​,P2​,P3​ are distinct collinear points (no two with the same x-coordinate), then x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​=x3​−x2​y3​−y2​​

Proof sketch. Collinearity means there exist constants a,b with yi​=axi​+b for each i. Then both ratios equal a. □

This is the rigorous reason we can speak of the slope of a line, not just the slope between two particular points.

3. The Five Forms of a Line

3.1 Slope-Intercept Form

y=mx+b​

where m = slope, b = y-intercept (the value of y when x=0).

When to use: When you know the slope and y-intercept directly. Most natural for function perspective.

Example. Line with slope 3 crossing y-axis at −2: y=3x−2

At x=0: y=−2 ✓. At x=1: y=1. At x=2: y=4.

3.2 Point-Slope Form

y−y0​=m(x−x0​)​

where (x0​,y0​) is a known point on the line and m is the slope.

When to use: When you know one point and the slope. This is the form that generalizes directly to the tangent line formula in calculus.

Derivation. For any point (x,y) on the line, the slope from (x0​,y0​) to (x,y) must equal m: x−x0​y−y0​​=m⟹y−y0​=m(x−x0​)

Example. Line through (2,5) with slope −1: y−5=−1(x−2)⟹y=−x+7

3.3 Two-Point Form

y−y1​=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​(x−x1​)​

Derivation. Compute m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​, then plug into point-slope form with (x1​,y1​).

Example. Line through (1,3) and (5,11): m=5−111−3​=2⇒y−3=2(x−1)⟹y=2x+1

3.4 Standard (General) Form

Ax+By+C=0​

where A,B,C∈R and (A,B)=(0,0).

Remarks:

  • Every line in R2 — including vertical lines — has this representation.
  • Slope-intercept form fails for vertical lines; standard form does not.
  • If B=0: slope is m=−A/B, y-intercept is −C/B.
  • If B=0: vertical line x=−C/A.

The coefficient vector (A,B) is the normal vector to the line — this is the key insight for machine learning (see §7).

Example. 3x−2y+6=0. Here A=3,B=−2,C=6.
Slope: m=−3/(−2)=3/2. y-intercept: −6/(−2)=3.
Equivalent: y=23​x+3.

3.5 Intercept Form

ax​+by​=1​

where a is the x-intercept and b is the y-intercept, both nonzero.

Derivation. The line passes through (a,0) and (0,b). Apply the two-point form and simplify.

Example. Line cutting x-axis at 4 and y-axis at 3: 4x​+3y​=1⟹3x+4y=12

4. Special Lines

4.1 Horizontal Lines

y=k​

Slope =0. Parallel to x-axis. Standard form: 0⋅x+1⋅y−k=0.

4.2 Vertical Lines

x=h​

Slope is undefined. Cannot be written in slope-intercept form. Standard form: 1⋅x+0⋅y−h=0.

Note. In the vector/ML world, a vertical line x=h in 2D is a degenerate hyperplane with normal (1,0). There’s nothing exceptional about it once you adopt the standard form.

5. Relationships Between Lines

5.1 Parallel Lines

Theorem. Two distinct lines ℓ1​ and ℓ2​ in R2 are parallel if and only if they have equal slopes (or both are vertical).

ℓ1​∥ℓ2​⟺m1​=m2​

Proof. Lines are parallel iff they have no intersection. Suppose y=m1​x+b1​ and y=m2​x+b2​. Setting equal: (m1​−m2​)x=b2​−b1​. If m1​=m2​, this has no solution (since b1​=b2​) or infinitely many (same line). If m1​=m2​, unique solution x=(b2​−b1​)/(m1​−m2​). □

Example. y=2x+1 and y=2x−5 are parallel (both slope 2, different intercepts).

5.2 Perpendicular Lines

Theorem. Two non-vertical lines ℓ1​,ℓ2​ with slopes m1​,m2​ are perpendicular if and only if:

m1​⋅m2​=−1​

Equivalently, m2​=−1/m1​ (the negative reciprocal).

Proof. Translate so intersection is at origin. Direction vectors of ℓ1​ and ℓ2​ are v1​=(1,m1​) and v2​=(1,m2​). Perpendicularity means v1​⋅v2​=0: 1⋅1+m1​m2​=0⟹m1​m2​=−1□

Example. ℓ1​:y=3x+1 has slope 3. A perpendicular line has slope −1/3, e.g., y=−31​x+4.

         y
         |   / ← ℓ₁ (slope 3)
         |  /
─────────┼──────── x
         | \
         |  \ ← ℓ₂ (slope -1/3)

6. Key Distance & Geometric Formulas

6.1 Distance Between Two Points

d(P1​,P2​)=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​​

Proof. Direct application of the Pythagorean theorem on the right triangle with legs ∣x2​−x1​∣ and ∣y2​−y1​∣.

6.2 Midpoint Formula

M=(2x1​+x2​​,,2y1​+y2​​)​

Proof. The midpoint is the average position: M=21​(P1​+P2​) in vector notation.

6.3 Distance from a Point to a Line

Theorem. The perpendicular distance from point P0​=(x0​,y0​) to the line Ax+By+C=0 is:

d=A2+B2​∣Ax0​+By0​+C∣​​

Proof. The line has unit normal n^=A2+B2​(A,B)​. Pick any point Q=(x1​,y1​) on the line (so Ax1​+By1​+C=0). The signed distance from P0​ to the line is the projection of QP0​​ onto n^:

d=​QP0​​⋅n^​=A2+B2​∣A(x0​−x1​)+B(y0​−y1​)∣​=A2+B2​∣Ax0​+By0​+C∣​

(using Ax1​+By1​=−C). □

Example. Distance from (3,4) to the line 3x−4y+5=0: d=9+16​∣3(3)−4(4)+5∣​=5∣9−16+5∣​=5∣−2∣​=52​

This formula is the margin formula in Support Vector Machines (see §7.3).

6.4 Point of Intersection of Two Lines

Given ℓ1​:A1​x+B1​y+C1​=0 and ℓ2​:A2​x+B2​y+C2​=0, solve the 2×2 linear system:

(A1​​B1​ A2​​B2​​)(x y​)=(−C1​ −C2​​)

Unique solution exists iff the determinant A1​B2​−A2​B1​=0, i.e., the lines are not parallel.

6.5 Angle Between Two Lines

The acute angle θ between lines with slopes m1​ and m2​:

tanθ=​1+m1​m2​m1​−m2​​​​

Derivation. If α1​,α2​ are the inclination angles (mi​=tanαi​), then θ=α1​−α2​ or its supplement. Apply the tangent subtraction formula.

Special case: θ=90° iff 1+m1​m2​=0, recovering the perpendicularity condition.

7. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

This section is the reason these formulas are worth internalizing deeply — not just as geometry, but as computational infrastructure.

7.1 Linear Regression: The Line as a Model

Setup. Given data points (xi​,yi​)i=1n​, fit a line y^​=mx+b minimizing the mean squared error (MSE):

L(m,b)=n1​∑i=1n​(yi​−mxi​−b)2

Setting ∂L/∂m=0 and ∂L/∂b=0 yields the normal equations:

m=n∑xi2​−(∑xi​)2n∑xi​yi​−∑xi​∑yi​​,b=yˉ​−mxˉ

Every gradient descent step is a move along a line in parameter space.

7.2 Gradient Descent Along a Line

In 1D: if the loss is L(θ) and we step along the direction −∇L, the update is:

θt+1​=θt​−η⋅dθdL​​θt​​

This is literally: move from the current point θt​ along the line with slope −dL/dθ by step size η. The local linear approximation is L(θ)≈L(θt​)+L′(θt​)(θ−θt​) — the tangent line.

7.3 Support Vector Machines: The Margin is a Point-to-Line Distance

An SVM finds the hyperplane (line in 2D) w⋅x+b=0 that separates two classes with maximum margin. In 2D this is w1​x+w2​y+b=0. The margin is:

margin=∣w∣2​=w12​+w22​​2​

The distance from a support vector (x0​,y0​) to the decision boundary is exactly the point-to-line formula §6.3:

d=w12​+w22​​∣w1​x0​+w2​y0​+b∣​=1⋅∣w∣1​

Maximizing the margin = minimizing ∣w∣ subject to the classification constraints.

            y
            |     ●  ← class +1
            |   ●
────────────┼──────────── x
     ○      |  /← decision boundary: w·x + b = 0
       ○    | /
            |/

The margin is twice the point-to-line distance from the nearest data point.

7.4 Neural Networks: Neurons Compute Linear Thresholds

A single neuron with weights w1​,w2​ and bias b computes:

output=σ(w1​x1​+w2​x2​+b)

where σ is a nonlinearity. The pre-activation w1​x1​+w2​x2​+b=0 is a line in the input space — the neuron’s linear decision boundary before the nonlinearity.

In a neural network, stacking these creates piecewise-linear decision boundaries, which are just compositions of shifted, rotated lines.

7.5 Tangent Lines and Backpropagation

For a scalar function f(x), the tangent line at x0​ is:

T(x)=f(x0​)+f′(x0​)(x−x0​)

This is the first-order Taylor expansion. In backpropagation, the chain rule propagates these local linear approximations layer by layer. Every activation function is approximated by its tangent line at the current input during the backward pass.

9. Worked Examples

Example 1: Full line problem

Find the line through (1,−3) perpendicular to 2x−5y+1=0.

Step 1. Slope of given line: A=2,B=−5, so m1​=−A/B=−2/(−5)=2/5.

Step 2. Slope of perpendicular: m2​=−1/m1​=−5/2.

Step 3. Point-slope form through (1,−3): y−(−3)=−25​(x−1)⟹y=−25​x+25​−3=−25​x−21​

Step 4. Standard form: multiply by 2: 5x+2y+1=0.

Check perpendicularity: normal vectors (2,−5) and (5,2): dot product =10−10=0 ✓

Example 2: Distance to a line

Find the distance from the origin to the line 5x+12y−26=0.

d=25+144​∣5(0)+12(0)−26∣​=169​26​=1326​=2

Example 3: Tangent line (the calculus bridge)

Find the tangent line to f(x)=x2 at x=3.

f(3)=9, f′(x)=2x, f′(3)=6. Point-slope: y−9=6(x−3)⟹y=6x−9

This is exactly the point-slope formula from §3.2 — calculus doesn’t change the geometry, only how we find m.

10. Exercises (For Rigorous Practice)

  1. Prove that the perpendicular distance from (x0​,y0​) to y=mx+b equals 1+m2​∣y0​−mx0​−b∣​ directly from §6.3.

  2. Show that if three lines are mutually perpendicular (each pair), at least two must be parallel. (Hint: the perpendicularity condition is an equivalence relation issue — think carefully.)

  3. Derive the formula for the angle bisectors of two intersecting lines ℓ1​ and ℓ2​.

  4. Linear regression: Given points (0,1),(1,2),(2,4), find the best-fit line by minimizing MSE analytically.

  5. SVM setup: Given positive examples at (2,3),(3,2) and negative at (−1,0),(0,−1), sketch the maximum-margin separating line (do not solve — just geometric reasoning).
