“Torque is the rotational analogue of force — but the geometry hidden inside the cross product is where the real story lives.”

§1. Motivation — Why Torque?

Consider trying to open a heavy door. You instinctively push at the edge, not near the hinge. You push perpendicular to the door, not parallel to it. Both of these intuitions are encoded, simultaneously and elegantly, in a single mathematical object: the torque vector.

Torque answers the question: given a force applied at a position in space, how much does it tend to rotate a body about some axis?

§2. Definition

Let r be the position vector from the pivot point (origin of rotation) to the point where force is applied, and let F be the applied force. Then the torque is:

τ=r×F​

This is a cross product — so torque is a vector, not a scalar.

2.1 Unpacking the Cross Product

Recall that for r=⟨r1​,r2​,r3​⟩ and F=⟨F1​,F2​,F3​⟩:

r×F=​i​j​k r1​​r2​​r3​ F1​​F2​​F3​​​

=i(r2​F3​−r3​F2​)−j(r1​F3​−r3​F1​)+k(r1​F2​−r2​F1​)

2.2 Magnitude and Geometric Interpretation

∣τ∣=∣r∣∣F∣sinθ

where θ is the angle between r and F.

Key insight: Torque is maximised when r⊥F (i.e., θ=90°), and zero when r∥F (force points directly toward or away from the pivot — no rotation).

2.3 Direction — The Right-Hand Rule

The direction of τ=r×F follows the right-hand rule:

  • Point fingers of your right hand along r
  • Curl them toward F
  • Your thumb points in the direction of τ
         τ (out of page ⊙)
         ↑
         |
    r    |        F
  ───────●─────────→
       pivot

In 2D (rotation in the xy-plane), torque is purely in the k^ direction:

τ=(r1​F2​−r2​F1​),k^

A positive τz​ means counterclockwise rotation; negative means clockwise.

§3. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Wrench on a Bolt (2D)

A force F=⟨0,10,0⟩ N is applied at position r=⟨0.3,0,0⟩ m (a wrench of length 0.3 m pointing along the x-axis).

τ=r×F=​i​j​k 0.3​0​0 0​10​0​​=k(0.3⋅10−0⋅0)=3,k^ N\cdotpm

τ=3,k^ N·m — rotation is counterclockwise about the z-axis.

Example 2 — Force at an Angle

Force F=⟨5,5,0⟩ N applied at r=⟨2,0,0⟩ m.

τ=​i​j​k 2​0​0 5​5​0​​=k^(2⋅5−0⋅5)=10,k^ N\cdotpm

Notice: only the perpendicular component of F (the j^​ part) contributes to torque! The radial component (along i^) is wasted — it pushes toward the pivot and creates no rotation.

§4. Torque as a Signed Scalar (2D Special Case)

In planar problems (forces and positions in the xy-plane), it’s common to work with the scalar torque:

τ=rx​Fy​−ry​Fx​=∣r∣∣F∣sinθ

This is exactly the z-component of r×F.

This is also the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by r and F — a lovely geometric fact.

§5. Newton’s Second Law for Rotation

Just as F=ma governs linear dynamics, torque governs rotational dynamics:

τnet​=I,α

where:

  • I = moment of inertia (rotational analogue of mass, depends on mass distribution)
  • α=ω˙ = angular acceleration

And the angular momentum L=Iω satisfies:

τ=dtdL​

This is the rotational analogue of F=dtdp​.

§6. Equilibrium and Static Torque

A rigid body is in rotational equilibrium iff:

∑i​τi​=0

That is, all torques about any chosen pivot must sum to zero.

Example — Seesaw:

       m₁g↓        m₂g↓
         |            |
   ──────●────────────●──── plank
         |←   r₁  →|←  r₂  →|
                  △
                pivot

Equilibrium: m1​gr1​=m2​gr2​ (torques balance about the pivot).

§7. Connection to the Cross Product — A Deeper Look

The torque τ=r×F is fundamentally an antisymmetric operation:

r×F=−F×r

This reflects the physical fact that the order matters: the same force applied at the same point creates torques in opposite senses depending on the reference direction.

Decomposition

Any force F can be split into components:

F=F⊥​+F∥​

where F∥​ is along r and F⊥​ is perpendicular to r.

Then: r×F=r×F⊥​+=0r×F∥​​​=r×F⊥​

Only the perpendicular component produces torque. The parallel component is rotational dead weight.

§8. Moment Arm — An Equivalent Formulation

The moment arm d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of F.

∣τ∣=∣F∣⋅d

This is equivalent to ∣r∣∣F∣sinθ since d=∣r∣sinθ.

           ←────── line of action of F ────→  →F
                          ↕ d (moment arm)
           ●─────────────────────────────────────
         pivot        r

This formulation is often cleaner for problems where the line of action is easy to identify geometrically.

§9. Torques and Differential Forms (Preview)

For the advanced reader: torque is naturally expressed using the exterior product ∧. In 3D, r×F is dual (via the Hodge star) to the 2-form r∧F, which represents an oriented area element.

This is why torque is sometimes called a pseudovector (or axial vector) — it behaves differently from a polar vector under reflections. In even dimensions, the cross product doesn’t exist, but the exterior product always does.

§10. Torque in AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

This is where classical mechanics and modern computation collide in surprising ways.

10.1 Gradient Descent as a “Torque” Analogy

In gradient descent, the gradient ∇θ​L plays the role of a force that “pushes” parameters. The learning rate η scales the step — analogous to the moment arm scaling the torque. The update:

θ←θ−η,∇θ​L

is structurally similar to angular impulse: a force applied at a lever arm produces a rotation. The further the parameters are from a minimum (longer lever arm), the larger the induced rotation in parameter space.

10.2 Rigid Body Simulation in Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

Physics-Informed Neural Networks often embed torque equations directly as constraints in the loss function. When simulating articulated bodies (robot arms, molecular dynamics):

Lphysics​=∣τnet​−Iα∣2

The network is penalised for predicting motions that violate τ=Iα.

10.3 Robotics and Reinforcement Learning

In robot manipulation (a central problem in deep RL), the policy πθ​(a∣s) outputs joint torques directly as actions. The agent must learn:

  • Which joint to torque
  • By how much
  • In which direction

Algorithms like PPO and SAC are routinely benchmarked on torque-controlled MuJoCo environments (e.g., HalfCheetah-v4, Ant-v4).

State s_t ──→ π_θ ──→ [τ₁, τ₂, ..., τₙ] (joint torques)

                     Physics Simulator

                    Next state s_{t+1}, reward r_t

10.4 Attention Mechanism — A Structural Analogy

In transformer self-attention, the output is a weighted combination:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QK⊤​)V

The dk​​1​ scaling factor plays the role of a moment arm — it controls how strongly the “rotational effect” (attention) of each query-key pair is expressed. Without this scaling, large dk​ causes dot products to grow large, driving softmax into saturation (tiny gradients, no effective learning) — analogous to applying force directly through the pivot.

10.5 Angular Momentum in Optimisers

The momentum term in SGD with momentum:

vt+1​=β,vt​+∇θ​L θt+1​=θt​−η,vt+1​

is precisely angular momentum carried forward in time. Just as L=Iω persists unless acted on by torque, the velocity vt​ persists and accumulates — enabling the optimiser to “coast” through flat loss landscapes.

Adam extends this further by normalising by the second moment of gradients — adaptive torque control, in effect.

10.6 Graph Neural Networks and Equivariance

SE(3)-equivariant GNNs (e.g., SchNet, DimeNet, NequIP) are designed for molecular property prediction. They must handle torques and angular forces correctly — the network output must transform as a pseudovector under rotations and reflections of the input molecule. Torque’s status as an axial vector is not a curiosity; it is an architectural constraint.
