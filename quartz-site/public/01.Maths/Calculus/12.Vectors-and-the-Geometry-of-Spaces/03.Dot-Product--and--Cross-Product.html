“The dot product asks: how much do these two arrows agree? The cross product asks: what is born from their disagreement?” — Personal margin note

0. Orientation — Two Products, Two Philosophies

The dot product and cross product are both ways to “multiply” vectors, but they answer fundamentally different geometric questions and produce fundamentally different outputs:

Dot Product u⋅vCross Product u×v
OutputScalarVector
Lives inRn, any dimensionR3 only
MeasuresAlignment / projectionArea & perpendicularity
Zero whenVectors are orthogonalVectors are parallel
Max whenVectors are parallelVectors are orthogonal
Commutative?Yes: u⋅v=v⋅uNo: u×v=−v×u

Think of the dot product as measuring the shadow one vector casts on another. The cross product creates a new vector perpendicular to both, whose length encodes the area they sweep out.

Part I: The Dot Product

1. Definitions — Algebraic and Geometric

1.1 Algebraic Definition

Let u=⟨u1​,u2​,…,un​⟩ and v=⟨v1​,v2​,…,vn​⟩ in Rn. Then:

u⋅v=i=1∑n​ui​vi​=u1​v1​+u2​v2​+⋯+un​vn​​

The output is a single real number — a scalar.

Note on notation. Some texts write ⟨u,v⟩ for the inner product, especially in functional analysis or quantum mechanics. In Rn with the standard basis, these coincide. The dot product u⋅v is also written as the matrix product uTv when vectors are column matrices.

1.2 The Geometric Formula — The Central Theorem

Theorem (Stewart §12.3). For any u,v∈Rn: u⋅v=∣u∣,∣v∣cosθ​ where θ∈[0,π] is the angle between u and v.

Proof. Place both vectors with their tails at the origin. The vector u−v forms the third side of the triangle. By the Law of Cosines:

∣u−v∣2=∣u∣2+∣v∣2−2∣u∣∣v∣cosθ

Now expand ∣u−v∣2 algebraically: ∣u−v∣2=∑i=1n​(ui​−vi​)2=∑ui2​−2∑ui​vi​+∑vi2​=∣u∣2−2(u⋅v)+∣v∣2

Equating both expressions and canceling ∣u∣2+∣v∣2 from both sides: −2(u⋅v)=−2∣u∣∣v∣cosθ⟹u⋅v=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ□

This proof is beautiful. It is the bridge between pure computation (component-wise sum) and pure geometry (angle). Memorize the strategy: expand ∣u−v∣2 two ways and match.

2. Properties of the Dot Product

Let u,v,w∈Rn and c∈R.

  1. Commutativity: u⋅v=v⋅u (clear from definition)
  2. Distributivity: u⋅(v+w)=u⋅v+u⋅w
  3. Scalar pullout: (c,u)⋅v=c(u⋅v)=u⋅(c,v)
  4. Self-dot = squared norm: v⋅v=∣v∣2
  5. Zero vector: 0⋅v=0
  6. Positive definiteness: v⋅v≥0, with equality iff v=0

Properties 1–3 make the dot product a bilinear form. Properties 4–6, combined with 1, make it an inner product (on real vector spaces). These axioms generalize to infinite-dimensional spaces — the foundation of Hilbert spaces in functional analysis.

3. Reading cosθ — What the Dot Product Detects

From u⋅v=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ, the sign of the dot product tells you the qualitative relationship between the vectors:

                  u
                 /
                / θ < 90°
               /____________ v
    u·v > 0: acute angle, vectors "lean toward each other"


       u __________ v
    u·v = 0: θ = 90°, orthogonal — no alignment at all


    u ____________
                  \
                   \ θ > 90°
                    v
    u·v < 0: obtuse angle, vectors "pull away from each other"

The extreme cases:

  • u∥v, same direction (θ=0): u⋅v=∣u∣∣v∣ — maximum
  • u∥v, opposite direction (θ=π): u⋅v=−∣u∣∣v∣ — minimum
  • u⊥v (θ=π/2): u⋅v=0 — no projection

4. Projection — The Workhorse Application

4.1 Scalar Projection

The scalar projection (or component) of u onto v is the signed length of the shadow of u along v^:

compv​,u=∣v∣u⋅v​=∣u∣cosθ

4.2 Vector Projection

The vector projection is the actual shadow vector along v:

projv​,u=(∣v∣2u⋅v​)v=v⋅vu⋅v​,v

4.3 Orthogonal Decomposition

Every vector u decomposes uniquely into a part along v and a part perpendicular to v:

u=parallel to vprojv​,u​​+⊥,to v(u−projv​,u)​​

Verify the perpendicular part is indeed orthogonal to v: (u−projv​,u)⋅v=u⋅v−∣v∣2u⋅v​(v⋅v)=u⋅v−u⋅v=0✓

         u
        /|
       / |
      /  |  ← perpendicular part (u - proj_v u)
     / θ |
    /    |
   +-----+-----------> v
         proj_v u

This decomposition is the root of the Gram-Schmidt process, which orthogonalizes a basis, which underlies QR decomposition, PCA, and essentially all of numerical linear algebra.

5. The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Theorem. |\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}|,|\mathbf{v}|}

Proof (elegant algebraic method). Consider f(t)=∣u−tv∣2≥0 for all t∈R.

Expanding: f(t)=∣u∣2−2t(u⋅v)+t2∣v∣2≥0

This is a quadratic in t that is always non-negative, so its discriminant must be ≤0: Δ=4(u⋅v)2−4∣u∣2∣v∣2≤0 ⟹(u⋅v)2≤∣u∣2∣v∣2⟹∣u⋅v∣≤∣u∣∣v∣□

Corollary. The geometric formula cosθ=∣u∣∣v∣u⋅v​ is valid: Cauchy-Schwarz guarantees the right side lives in [−1,1], so θ=arccos(⋯) is well-defined.

Corollary. The Triangle Inequality: ∣u+v∣≤∣u∣+∣v∣.

Cauchy-Schwarz is one of the most used inequalities in all of mathematics. It appears in probability (correlation coefficients are bounded by 1), quantum mechanics (uncertainty principle), and optimization.

6. Worked Examples — Dot Product

Example 1 — Computing the Angle

Find the angle between a=⟨1,−2,3⟩ and b=⟨2,1,1⟩:

a⋅b=2−2+3=3 ∣a∣=1+4+9​=14​,∣b∣=4+1+1​=6​ cosθ=14​⋅6​3​=84​3​=221​3​ θ=arccos!(221​3​)≈70.9°

Example 2 — Projection and Decomposition

Project u=⟨4,2,0⟩ onto v=⟨1,1,0⟩:

u⋅v=4+2+0=6,∣v∣2=2 projv​,u=26​⟨1,1,0⟩=⟨3,3,0⟩ perpendicular part=⟨4,2,0⟩−⟨3,3,0⟩=⟨1,−1,0⟩

Check: ⟨1,−1,0⟩⋅⟨1,1,0⟩=1−1=0 ✓

Example 3 — Physical Work

A force F=⟨5,3,−2⟩ N displaces an object by d=⟨1,0,4⟩ m. Work done:

W=F⋅d=5+0−8=−3 J

Negative work means the force opposed the displacement (like friction doing negative work on a sliding object).

Part II: The Cross Product

7. Definition — Three Equivalent Forms

The cross product is defined only in R3. (A generalization exists in R7, but that’s exotic mathematics.)

7.1 Determinant Form (Most Practical)

u×v=​i​j​k u1​​u2​​u3​ v1​​v2​​v3​​​​

Expanding along the first row:

u×v=i(u2​v3​−u3​v2​)−j(u1​v3​−u3​v1​)+k(u1​v2​−u2​v1​)

=⟨u2​v3​−u3​v2​,;u3​v1​−u1​v3​,;u1​v2​−u2​v1​⟩

Memory device. For the i-component, “cover” the first column and take the 2×2 determinant of what remains. Alternate signs: +,−,+.

7.2 Geometric Definition

u×v=(∣u∣∣v∣sinθ),n^​

where θ∈[0,π] is the angle between u and v, and n^ is the unit vector perpendicular to both, determined by the right-hand rule.

Right-hand rule: Point fingers of right hand along u, curl them toward v. Thumb points in direction of u×v.

        ^ n = u × v (thumb)
        |
        |
   u ---+---> (fingers curl from u toward v)
         \
          v

7.3 Standard Basis Cross Products

i×j=k,j×k=i,k×i=j j×i=−k,k×j=−i,i×k=−j i×i=j×j=k×k=0

Mnemonic (cyclic):

    i → j → k → i → j → k → ...
    Going forward: positive
    Going backward: negative

8. Properties of the Cross Product

Let u,v,w∈R3 and c∈R.

  1. Anti-commutativity: u×v=−(v×u)
  2. Distributivity over addition: u×(v+w)=u×v+u×w
  3. Scalar pullout: (c,u)×v=c(u×v)=u×(c,v)
  4. Self-cross = zero: u×u=0 (since sin0=0)
  5. Perpendicularity: (u×v)⋅u=0 and (u×v)⋅v=0
  6. NOT associative: (u×v)×w=u×(v×w) in general

Property 1 is the critical one. The cross product is anti-commutative, not commutative. Reversing the order flips the direction of the result. This is why order matters in 3D geometry — and in physics (torque, angular momentum).

Proof of Property 5 (perpendicularity to u): (u×v)⋅u=(u2​v3​−u3​v2​)u1​+(u3​v1​−u1​v3​)u2​+(u1​v2​−u2​v1​)u3​ Expanding: u1​u2​v3​−u1​u3​v2​+u2​u3​v1​−u1​u2​v3​+u1​u3​v2​−u2​u3​v1​=0 □

9. Geometric Content — Area and Volume

9.1 Area of a Parallelogram

Area=∣u×v∣=∣u∣∣v∣sinθ

This is the area of the parallelogram with sides u and v.

         v
        /----/
       /    /   Area = ||u × v||
      /----/
           u

Area of the triangle with sides u and v: 21​∣u×v∣.

9.2 Scalar Triple Product and Volume of a Parallelepiped

The scalar triple product (or box product):

[u,v,w]=u⋅(v×w)=​u1​​u2​​u3​ v1​​v2​​v3​ w1​​w2​​w3​​​

This gives the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by u, v, w.

V=∣u⋅(v×w)∣

Key property: u⋅(v×w)=v⋅(w×u)=w⋅(u×v) — cyclic permutations preserve the value.

Coplanarity test: u,v,w are coplanar ⟺ u⋅(v×w)=0.

10. Lagrange’s Identity

Theorem. ∣u×v∣2=∣u∣2∣v∣2−(u⋅v)2

Proof (component-wise): Denote u×v=⟨a,b,c⟩ where a=u2​v3​−u3​v2​, etc. Compute a2+b2+c2 and expand. Meanwhile: ∣u∣2∣v∣2−(u⋅v)2=(u12​+u22​+u32​)(v12​+v22​+v32​)−(u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u3​v3​)2 Expanding both and matching terms confirms equality. □

Geometric meaning: Using u⋅v=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ:

∣u×v∣2=∣u∣2∣v∣2(1−cos2θ)=∣u∣2∣v∣2sin2θ

So ∣u×v∣=∣u∣∣v∣sinθ. The geometric formula falls out of Lagrange’s identity — the two definitions (determinant and geometric) are equivalent.

11. The BAC-CAB Rule

A crucial identity for the vector triple product:

A×(B×C)=B(A⋅C)−C(A⋅B)​

Mnemonic: BAC minus CAB. The result is a linear combination of B and C — which makes sense, since B×C is perpendicular to both B and C, so A×(B×C) lies in the plane spanned by B and C.

This identity appears constantly in electromagnetism (∇×(∇×E)) and fluid mechanics.

13. Dot vs. Cross — Side-by-Side Comparison

SAME INPUT: u = <1, 0, 0>,  v = <0, 1, 0>  (perpendicular unit vectors)

DOT PRODUCT:                    CROSS PRODUCT:
u · v = 0                       u × v = k = <0, 0, 1>
→ Zero because θ = 90°          → Maximum magnitude (sin 90° = 1)
→ Captures: no alignment        → Captures: maximal area swept

SAME INPUT: u = <1, 0, 0>,  v = <2, 0, 0>  (parallel vectors)

DOT PRODUCT:                    CROSS PRODUCT:
u · v = 2                       u × v = <0, 0, 0>
→ Maximum (cos 0° = 1)          → Zero (sin 0° = 0)
→ Captures: perfect alignment   → Captures: no area, degenerate

The duality: the dot product maximizes when vectors agree, the cross product maximizes when they disagree. They are “complementary sensors.”

14. Connections to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

14.1 The Dot Product is Everywhere in Neural Networks

Every forward pass in a neural network is fundamentally repeated dot products. A single neuron computes: z=w⋅x+b=∑i​wi​xi​+b

The dot product measures how much the input vector x aligns with the learned weight vector w. A neuron activates strongly precisely when its weight vector points in nearly the same direction as the input — the geometric picture from §3.

Efficiency: Modern hardware (GPUs/TPUs) is physically optimized to compute dot products in parallel (matrix multiplication = batched dot products). The dot product’s simplicity is what makes deep learning computationally feasible at scale.

14.2 Cosine Similarity — Semantic Search and Embeddings

From the geometric formula, cosine similarity isolates direction and discards magnitude: cosim(u,v)=∣u∣∣v∣u⋅v​

This is the dot product of the normalized vectors, i.e., unit vectors. It measures purely directional agreement.

Applications:

  • Word2Vec / GloVe: The famous king - man + woman ≈ queen result is vector addition/subtraction; nearness is measured by cosine similarity.
  • BERT embeddings: Sentences mapped to R768 vectors; semantic similarity is cosine similarity.
  • Vector databases (Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma): Store embeddings of documents; a user query is embedded and the top-k results are the k vectors with highest cosine similarity. The entire retrieval step in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines is a batched dot product search.

14.3 Self-Attention — Dot Products Scaled Up

The Transformer’s attention mechanism: Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QK⊤​)V

QK⊤ is a matrix of pairwise dot products: entry (i,j) is qi​⋅kj​ — how much query token i attends to key token j. This is exactly cosine-like: the higher the dot product, the more attention is paid.

The dk​​ scaling prevents dot products from becoming too large in high dimensions (where even near-orthogonal vectors accumulate non-negligible dot products), which would saturate softmax and kill gradients. This is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality being respected in practice.

14.4 Gradient as a Dot Product — Directional Derivatives

The directional derivative of a scalar field f:Rn→R in direction u^: Du^​f=∇f⋅u^

This is a dot product. The gradient ∇f (a vector in parameter space) is the direction of steepest ascent. The rate of change in any other direction u^ is the projection of ∇f onto u^ — exactly ∣∇f∣cosθ.

Gradient descent works because moving opposite to ∇f (i.e., choosing u^=−∇f^​) maximizes the decrease in f: D−∇f^​​f=∇f⋅(−∇f^​)=−∣∇f∣ This is the minimum possible directional derivative (by Cauchy-Schwarz).

The entire theory of gradient descent is the statement: the dot product is minimized when the angle is π.

14.5 The Cross Product in 3D Deep Learning

Cross products appear when learning from or generating 3D geometric data:

  • Surface normals in 3D vision: Given two tangent vectors on a surface mesh, their cross product gives the normal — used in lighting models, physics simulations, and 3D generative models.
  • NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields): Ray marching through 3D scenes; computing intersections and normals uses cross product geometry.
  • Rotation representations: Angular velocity ω of a rotating body gives instantaneous linear velocity v=ω×r. Robot learning agents modeling 3D kinematics use this constantly.
  • Graph Neural Networks on point clouds: Local coordinate frames at each point are defined using cross products of estimated tangent vectors.

14.6 PCA — Projection via Eigenvectors and Dot Products

Principal Component Analysis (the most fundamental dimensionality reduction technique):

  1. Center the data: subtract the mean from every data vector.
  2. Compute the covariance matrix Σ=n1​X⊤X — every entry is a dot product.
  3. Find eigenvectors v1​,v2​,… of Σ.
  4. Project data onto the top eigenvectors: zi​=x⋅vi​ — a dot product.

PCA is Gram-Schmidt (orthogonal decomposition, §4.3) applied to data, driven by dot products.

15. Summary

Dot Product

u⋅v=∑ui​vi​=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ

  • Scalar output. Works in Rn.
  • Zero ⟺ orthogonal. Measures alignment.
  • Powers: projections, angles, work, attention mechanisms, gradient descent, similarity search.

Cross Product

\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = \det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\u_1&u_2&u_3\v_1&v_2&v_3\end{pmatrix} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\sin\theta;\hat{\mathbf{n}}

  • Vector output. Only in R3.
  • Zero ⟺ parallel. Measures area, perpendicular axis.
  • Powers: surface normals, torques, plane equations, 3D vision.

The Deep Connection

∣u∣2∣v∣2=(u⋅v)2+∣u×v∣2

(Lagrange’s identity.) In words: the “total interaction” between two vectors splits into a scalar part (dot product — alignment) and a vector part (cross product — perpendicularity). They are complementary, and together they capture the complete relationship between u and v.

16. Exercises

  1. Prove that u×(v×w)+v×(w×u)+w×(u×v)=0 (Jacobi identity). Use BAC-CAB.

  2. Prove that (u×v)⋅(w×z)=(u⋅w)(v⋅z)−(u⋅z)(v⋅w).

  3. Show that the distance from point P to the line through A with direction d^ is ∣AP−(AP⋅d^)d^∣, or equivalently ∣d∣∣AP×d∣​.

  4. ML connection: If w and x are both unit vectors (normalized), show that w⋅x=cosθ. What is the maximum value of the dot product, and when is it achieved? What does this mean for a neuron with normalized weight and input vectors?

  5. Prove the polarization identity: u⋅v=41​(∣u+v∣2−∣u−v∣2). (The dot product is determined by the norm — this is why knowing all distances in a vector space is equivalent to knowing the inner product.)

17. Looking Forward

  • Lines and Planes (Stewart §12.5): Normal vectors to planes are found via the cross product. The plane through P0​ with normal n is n⋅(r−r0​)=0 — a dot product equation.
  • Calculus of Vector Fields (MIT 18.02, Week 8–9): Flux integrals ∬F⋅dS are dot products integrated over surfaces. Curl uses the cross product: ∇×F.
  • Linear Algebra: The dot product is the standard inner product; generalizing it yields Hilbert spaces, used in functional analysis and quantum mechanics.
  • Deep Learning Theory: Kernel methods generalize the dot product to nonlinear similarity. The kernel trick embeds data into a high-dimensional feature space and computes dot products there implicitly.
