Prerequisites: conic sections, completing the square, dot products, basic matrix algebra

1. The Setup — What Is a Quadratic Surface?

A quadratic surface (sometimes called a quadric surface) is the solution set of a single polynomial equation of degree 2 in three variables:

F(x,y,z)=Ax2+By2+Cz2+Dxy+Exz+Fyz+Gx+Hy+Iz+J=0

In matrix form — and this is the form that will later connect deeply to machine learning — we can write this as:

xTAx+bTx+c=0

where x=(x,y,z)T, A is a 3×3 symmetric matrix encoding the degree-2 terms, b encodes the linear terms, and c is the constant.

Why symmetric? Cross terms like Dxy appear symmetrically as 2D​ in both the (1,2) and (2,1) entries of A. The Spectral Theorem then guarantees A is diagonalisable over R, which means we can always rotate coordinates to eliminate cross terms. This is the geometric content behind “principal axes.”

When D=E=F=G=H=I=0 (no cross terms, centered at origin), we get the standard forms, and that’s where we begin.

2. The Six Standard Quadric Surfaces

Think of these as the “canonical representatives” — every quadric, after a change of coordinates (rotation + translation), reduces to one of them (or degenerates).

2.1 Ellipsoid

a2x2​+b2y2​+c2z2​=1

The mental image: A stretched sphere. When a=b=c=r you recover the sphere x2+y2+z2=r2.

Cross-sections (traces):

  • Fix z=k, ∣k∣<c: get the ellipse a2(1−k2/c2)x2​+b2(1−k2/c2)y2​=1. It shrinks to a point as ∣k∣→c.
  • Traces in the xy-, xz-, yz-planes are all ellipses.

Concrete example: 9x2​+4y2​+z2=1 — semi-axes a=3, b=2, c=1.

ML connection: The level sets of a multivariate Gaussian N(μ,Σ) are ellipsoids. Specifically, the set x:(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ)=c2 is an ellipsoid whose axes align with the eigenvectors of Σ and have lengths proportional to λi​​. PCA literally finds those axes.

2.2 Hyperboloid of One Sheet

a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=1

The mental image: A cooling tower. It’s connected — you can walk anywhere on it without lifting your pen.

Key traces:

  • Horizontal (z=k): ellipses a2(1+k2/c2)x2​+b2(1+k2/c2)y2​=1. Note: larger as ∣k∣ grows. The “waist” is at z=0.
  • Vertical (x=0): hyperbola b2y2​−c2z2​=1.

Sign count: Two positive, one negative on the left-hand side. That single minus sign is what “punches a hole” in the ellipsoid and opens it up.

Concrete example: x2+y2−z2=1 (circular cross-sections, a=b=c=1). Rotating around the z-axis traces out the familiar hourglass-adjacent shape seen in architecture.

2.3 Hyperboloid of Two Sheets

−a2x2​−b2y2​+c2z2​=1

equivalently c2z2​−a2x2​−b2y2​=1.

The mental image: Two separate bowl-shaped pieces, one with z≥c, one with z≤−c. Disconnected.

Key observation: At z=0: LHS =−x2/a2−y2/b2≤0<1, so no intersection with the xy-plane. The surface only appears where ∣z∣≥c.

Sign count: One positive, two negative. Mnemonic: “number of sheets = number of positive signs.”

Concrete example: z2−x2−y2=1.

2.4 Elliptic Paraboloid

z=a2x2​+b2y2​

The mental image: A bowl sitting at the origin, opening upward.

Key traces:

  • Horizontal (z=k>0): ellipses. At z=0: just the vertex (0,0,0).
  • Vertical (y=0): parabola z=x2/a2 opening upward.

Concrete example: z=x2+4y2​. The y-direction is “wider,” so contours are elongated horizontally.

ML connection — this is huge: The loss surface of a strongly convex objective is locally modeled by an elliptic paraboloid. Gradient descent on L(θ)=21​θTHθ (for positive definite Hessian H) descends elliptical level curves toward the unique minimum. The ratio of eigenvalues of H is the condition number, and it controls how “elongated” those ellipses are — and hence how slowly gradient descent converges without preconditioning.

2.5 Hyperbolic Paraboloid (Saddle Surface)

z=a2x2​−b2y2​

The mental image: A Pringles chip. Goes up in the x-direction, down in the y-direction from the origin.

Key traces:

  • Fix z=k>0: hyperbola with the x-axis as transverse axis.
  • Fix z=k<0: hyperbola with the y-axis as transverse axis.
  • Fix z=0: pair of lines y=±ab​x.
  • Fix x=0: downward parabola z=−y2/b2.
  • Fix y=0: upward parabola z=x2/a2.

The saddle point at the origin: it is simultaneously a local minimum along x and a local maximum along y. This is the canonical picture of a critical point that is neither a local max nor a local min.

Concrete example: z=x2−y2. The Hessian is (2​0 0​−2​), indefinite — one positive, one negative eigenvalue.

ML connection — critical for deep learning: Saddle points dominate the loss landscape of neural networks. In high dimensions, a critical point is almost certainly a saddle (the probability that all eigenvalues of the Hessian are positive shrinks exponentially). This explains why SGD with momentum, Adam, and second-order methods behave differently near saddles versus near minima. The escape from a saddle involves moving in a direction of negative curvature — exactly the direction of the negative-eigenvalue eigenvector of the Hessian.

2.6 Cone (Degenerate Case)

a2x2​+b2y2​=c2z2​

The mental image: Two cones glued tip-to-tip at the origin.

This is the “boundary” or degenerate case separating the one-sheet and two-sheet hyperboloids. The hyperboloid of one sheet a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=1 approaches the cone as the right-hand side →0.

Key traces:

  • Horizontal: ellipses (or circles for a=b).
  • Through the axis: pairs of lines.

3. The Trace Method — How to Identify These Things

The professional technique is: intersect with coordinate planes and planes parallel to them.

Whenever I see an unfamiliar quadric equation, I immediately set x=0, y=0, z=0 in turn, then look at z=k for a general k. Four traces almost always determine the surface uniquely.

Worked example. Identify 4x2−y2+2z2+4=0.

Rewrite: −1x2​+4y2​−2z2​=1, i.e., 4y2​−x2−2z2​=1.

  • Signs: one positive (y2), two negative (x2,z2).
  • At x=0,z=0: y2=4, so y=±2. Two isolated points — the “tips” of the two sheets.
  • At y=k, ∣k∣>2: x2+z2/2=k2/4−1>0 — an ellipse. ✓
  • Conclusion: Hyperboloid of two sheets opening along the y-axis.

4. Completing the Square — Shifting the Center

Real-world quadrics aren’t always centered at the origin. The strategy:

x2−4x+y2+z2−2z=4

Complete the square in x and z:

(x2−4x+4)+y2+(z2−2z+1)=4+4+1

(x−2)2+y2+(z−1)2=9

Ellipsoid (sphere, actually) centered at (2,0,1) with radius 3. Substitute u=x−2, v=y, w=z−1 to reduce to standard form.

The translation just shifts the center — it changes no qualitative feature of the surface.

5. The Principal Axes Theorem — The Linear Algebra Behind It All

Every quadric xTAx+bTx+c=0 (with A symmetric) can be reduced to standard form via:

  1. Eigendecomposition of A: A=QΛQT where Q is orthogonal (rotation) and Λ=diag(λ1​,λ2​,λ3​).
  2. Change of variables x=Qy: eliminates all cross terms.
  3. Complete the square in y: eliminates linear terms, shifts center.

After these steps, the equation looks like:

λ1​y12​+λ2​y22​+λ3​y32​=k

The signs of λ1​,λ2​,λ3​ and k determine which quadric you have:

Signs of λi​kSurface
All positive+Ellipsoid
Two positive, one negative+Hyperboloid of one sheet
One positive, two negative+Hyperboloid of two sheets
All positive (or mixed)0Elliptic cone / degenerate
Two positive, one zero+Elliptic paraboloid
One positive, one negative, one zero0Hyperbolic paraboloid

This is exactly what PCA does. Given a data covariance matrix Σ, PCA rotates to the eigenbasis — the principal axes of the ellipsoidal level sets of the Gaussian density. The eigenvalues λi​ tell you the variance along each new axis.

6. Connections to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

This section is the reason a modern analyst cares about quadric surfaces beyond their intrinsic beauty.

6.1 Loss Landscapes Are Locally Quadric

By Taylor expansion, any smooth loss L(θ) near a critical point θ∗ satisfies:

L(\theta) \approx L(\theta^_) + \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta^_)^T H(\theta - \theta^*)

where H=∇2L(θ∗) is the Hessian. The level sets L(θ)=c are quadric surfaces in parameter space. Specifically:

  • H≻0 (all eigenvalues positive) ⇒ elliptic paraboloid ⇒ local minimum.
  • H≺0 (all eigenvalues negative) ⇒ inverted elliptic paraboloid ⇒ local maximum.
  • H indefinite ⇒ saddle (hyperbolic paraboloid near the critical point).

In neural networks with millions of parameters, saddles are ubiquitous. The dimension is so high that the probability of a true local minimum (all eigenvalues positive) is negligible — most critical points are saddles.

6.2 Condition Number and Gradient Descent

For a quadratic loss L(θ)=21​θTHθ with H=diag(λ1​,λ2​) (in 2D for simplicity):

L(θ)=21​(λ1​θ12​+λ2​θ22​)

The level curves are ellipses with semi-axes 2c/λ1​​ and 2c/λ2​​. The condition number κ=λmax​/λmin​ measures elongation.

  • κ≈1 (nearly circular): gradient descent converges in few steps.
  • κ≫1 (highly elongated ellipsoid): gradient descent zigzags, converging slowly.

This is why batch normalization, Adam, and second-order methods (like Newton’s method) matter — they reshape the loss surface to have better condition number.

6.3 Gaussian Distributions and Mahalanobis Distance

The probability density of N(μ,Σ) is:

p(x)∝exp!(−21​(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ))

The exponent is a quadratic form. Its level sets — i.e., the sets where p(x)=const — are ellipsoids in Rn with axes aligned to eigenvectors of Σ and lengths proportional to λi​(Σ)​.

The Mahalanobis distance dM​(x,μ)=(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ)​ measures how many “standard deviations” away x is from μ in the geometry of Σ. In anomaly detection, points with large Mahalanobis distance are flagged as outliers — you’re literally asking “is this point outside the bounding ellipsoid?“

6.4 Support Vector Machines — The Kernel Trick and Quadric Decision Boundaries

A linear SVM finds a hyperplane wTx+b=0 separating classes. But with the polynomial kernel K(x,x′)=(x⋅x′+1)2, the implicit feature map lifts to a higher-dimensional space where the decision boundary becomes a quadric surface in the original space.

For example, in R2, a quadratic kernel SVM can produce decision boundaries that are ellipses, hyperbolas, or parabolas — all conic sections, which are the 2D analogues of our quadric surfaces.

6.5 Hessian Geometry in Second-Order Optimization

Methods like L-BFGS, Gauss-Newton, and natural gradient descent use or approximate the Hessian to account for curvature. The update rule:

θt+1​=θt​−H−1∇L(θt​)

geometrically transforms the ellipsoidal level sets into spheres (in the H−1-metric), making the landscape locally isotropic and allowing one-step convergence on quadratic objectives. Understanding that loss surfaces are locally quadric surfaces is the conceptual foundation for all of this.

9. Proof: Classification via Eigenvalue Signs

Theorem. Let Q(x)=xTAx with A symmetric. Then there exists an orthogonal change of variables x=Py (where P is orthogonal) such that Q(x)=λ1​y12​+λ2​y22​+λ3​y32​ where λ1​,λ2​,λ3​ are the eigenvalues of A.

Proof sketch.

  1. Since A is real symmetric, by the Real Spectral Theorem, A=PΛPT where P is orthogonal and Λ=diag(λ1​,λ2​,λ3​).
  2. Substitute x=Py: then xTAx=(Py)T(PΛPT)(Py)=yTPTPΛPTPy=yTΛy since PTP=I.
  3. This equals λ1​y12​+λ2​y22​+λ3​y32​. ■

Corollary. The quadric xTAx=1 is:

  • An ellipsoid iff all λi​>0.
  • A hyperboloid of one sheet iff exactly one λi​<0 (and the others >0).
  • A hyperboloid of two sheets iff exactly two λi​<0.
  • Empty iff all λi​ have the same sign and the equation has no solutions.

10. Practice Problems

Work these before looking up answers. The goal is fluency, not memorisation.

Problem 1. Identify: 4x2​+y2−9z2​=1. Describe traces.

Problem 2. The equation x2−2x+4y2+z2+2z=3. Complete the square and identify the surface.

Problem 3. What surface does z2=x2+4y2 define? Describe the traces.

Problem 4 (ML flavored). A loss function in 2D near a critical point has Hessian H=(6​0 0​−2​). What kind of quadric surface does the local approximation L≈21​θTHθ describe? What does this mean for gradient descent?

Problem 5. Show that the hyperboloid of one sheet a2x2​+b2y2​−c2z2​=1 contains the two families of lines:

(ax​−cz​)=t(1−by​),(ax​+cz​)=t1​(1+by​)

(Hint: multiply the two equations together and compare to the hyperboloid equation. This is a ruled surface — every point lies on two lines entirely contained in the surface.)

11. Key Takeaways — The Things I’d Write on the Blackboard

  1. Sign count determines type. Count positive terms on the standard-form left side. 3+ = ellipsoid, 2+ = 1-sheet, 1+ = 2-sheet. For paraboloids: one variable is linear (the “height”), count signs of the quadratic terms.

  2. Cross terms ⇒ rotate. Any off-diagonal term in A means the axes of the quadric are tilted. Diagonalize A to find the true axes.

  3. The saddle is the central object in optimization. More so than local minima in high-dimensional ML.

  4. Ellipsoids are the geometry of Gaussians. If you understand ellipsoids, you understand PCA, Mahalanobis distance, and multivariate statistics.

  5. Condition number = aspect ratio of the ellipsoid. Bad conditioning = elongated ellipsoid = slow gradient descent.
