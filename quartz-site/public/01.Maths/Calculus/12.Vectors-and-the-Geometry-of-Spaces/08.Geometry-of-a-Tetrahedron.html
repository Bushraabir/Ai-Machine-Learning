“The tetrahedron is the atom of geometry — the irreducible simplex from which all of three-dimensional space can be built.”

§1. What Is a Tetrahedron?

A tetrahedron is a polyhedron with 4 triangular faces, 4 vertices, 6 edges. It is the 3-dimensional simplex Δ3 — the simplest convex solid that fully encloses a volume in R3.

Euler’s formula check: V−E+F=4−6+4=2✓

The tetrahedron is self-dual: its dual polyhedron is another tetrahedron (swap vertices ↔ faces).

§2. Setting Up Coordinates

2.1 General Tetrahedron

Given four vertices a,b,c,d∈R3, all other geometric quantities derive from these positions.

2.2 The Regular Tetrahedron — Canonical Coordinates

The most natural embedding of a regular tetrahedron (all edges equal) is obtained by placing vertices at alternating corners of a unit cube:

v1​=(1,1,1),v2​=(1,−1,−1),v3​=(−1,1,−1),v4​=(−1,−1,1)

Verify edge length: ∣v1​−v2​∣=∣(0,2,2)∣=0+4+4​=22​

All (24​)=6 pairs give the same length 22​. ✓

§3. Volume

3.1 The Scalar Triple Product Formula

For a tetrahedron with one vertex at a and the other three at b,c,d, define edge vectors from a:

u=b−a,v=c−a,w=d−a

Then:

V=61​∣u⋅(v×w)∣​

Why 1/6? The parallelepiped spanned by u,v,w has volume ∣u⋅(v×w)∣. A pyramid is 31​ of the prism with the same base and height; the tetrahedron is 21​ of that pyramid — so overall 31​⋅21​=61​.

3.2 The Determinant Form

Equivalently, since u⋅(v×w)=det[u v w]:

V=61​​det(u1​​v1​​w1​ u2​​v2​​w2​ u3​​v3​​w3​​)​

Or equivalently, using all four vertices directly:

V=61​​det(b1​−a1​​c1​−a1​​d1​−a1​ b2​−a2​​c2​−a2​​d2​−a2​ b3​−a3​​c3​−a3​​d3​−a3​​)​

Geometric meaning of the determinant: It is the signed volume of the parallelepiped — positive if (u,v,w) form a right-handed system, negative otherwise.

3.3 Worked Example

Let a=(0,0,0), b=(1,0,0), c=(0,1,0), d=(0,0,1) (the unit tetrahedron).

u=(1,0,0),v=(0,1,0),w=(0,0,1)

det(1​0​0\0​1​0\0​0​1​)=1

V=61​⋅∣1∣=61​

This is 61​ of the unit cube — exactly one sixth, since the cube decomposes into exactly 6 congruent tetrahedra.

§4. Surface Area and Face Normals

4.1 Area of One Triangular Face

For a face with vertices p,q,r:

Area=21​∣(q−p)×(r−p)∣

The cross product (q−p)×(r−p) is the outward normal to that face (up to sign).

4.2 Outward Face Normals

For each face, the outward unit normal n^i​ points away from the opposite vertex. This is used in surface integrals, flux computations, and mesh rendering.

Convention: For face opposite vertex d, the normal should satisfy n^⋅(a−d)<0 (points away from d).

4.3 Total Surface Area — Regular Tetrahedron

Each face is an equilateral triangle with side a, area 43​​a2. Four faces:

S=4⋅43​​a2=3​,a2

§5. Key Metric Properties — Regular Tetrahedron

Let edge length be a. All proofs below use the canonical cube embedding scaled so edge =a.

5.1 Circumradius R (circumscribed sphere)

The circumsphere passes through all 4 vertices. By symmetry its centre is the centroid G.

R=∣v1​−G∣=4a6​​

5.2 Inradius r (inscribed sphere)

The insphere is tangent to all 4 faces from inside. It satisfies:

r=S3V​=3​,a23⋅122​​a3​=26​a​=12a6​​

Key ratio: rR​=a6​/12a6​/4​=3

So the circumradius is always exactly 3 times the inradius for a regular tetrahedron. This is a characterisation of tetrahedra among the Platonic solids.

5.3 Height h (apex to base)

h=a32​​

Note: R+r=h for the regular tetrahedron (centroid divides the altitude in ratio 3:1).

§6. The Centroid and Barycentric Coordinates

6.1 Centroid

G=4a+b+c+d​

The centroid divides each median (vertex to centroid of opposite face) in ratio 3:1 from the vertex.

6.2 Barycentric Coordinates

Any point p∈R3 can be written as:

p=λ1​a+λ2​b+λ3​c+λ4​d,λ1​+λ2​+λ3​+λ4​=1

The λi​ are the barycentric coordinates of p.

Geometric meaning of λi​: λi​=VVi​​ where Vi​ is the volume of the sub-tetrahedron formed by p and the face opposite vertex i.

p is inside the tetrahedron ⟺ all λi​>0.

§7. Dihedral Angles

The dihedral angle along an edge is the angle between the two faces sharing that edge.

For the regular tetrahedron, all 6 edges have the same dihedral angle:

θdihedral​=arccos!(31​)≈70.53°

Derivation: Let the edge be v1​v2​. The normals to the two adjacent faces are:

  • n^123​∝(v2​−v1​)×(v3​−v1​)
  • n^124​∝(v2​−v1​)×(v4​−v1​)

The dihedral angle satisfies cosθ=n^123​⋅n^124​. By the canonical coordinates this evaluates to 31​, so:

θ=arccos!(31​)

Note: The supplement π−arccos(1/3)≈109.47° is the tetrahedral bond angle in chemistry (e.g., methane CH₄).

§8. The Solid Angle at Each Vertex

The solid angle Ω subtended at each vertex of a regular tetrahedron (the amount of the unit sphere “seen” from that vertex):

Ω=arccos!(2723​)≈0.55 sr

The total solid angle over all 4 vertices is 4Ω≈2.2 sr (less than 4π≈12.57 sr — the full sphere — reflecting the open space between faces).

§9. Decomposition and the Simplex

9.1 The Tetrahedron as a Simplex

The tetrahedron is the 3-simplex Δ3:

\Delta^3 = \left{(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3,\lambda_4) \in \mathbb{R}^4 ;\middle|; \sum_{i=1}^4 \lambda_i = 1,\ \lambda_i \geq 0\right}

This is a 3-dimensional object living in a 3-dimensional affine subspace of R4.

Its faces correspond to subsets where some λi​=0:

  • 4 triangular faces: one λi​=0
  • 6 edges: two λi​=0
  • 4 vertices: three λi​=0

9.2 Decomposition of the Cube

The unit cube [0,1]3 decomposes into exactly 5 tetrahedra (non-overlapping, filling the cube):

Cube → 5 tetrahedra:
  1 central regular tetrahedron
+ 4 corner tetrahedra (right-angle type)

Alternatively, into 6 congruent tetrahedra (all sharing the main diagonal).

This decomposition is exploited in finite element methods and mesh generation.

§10. The Cayley–Menger Determinant

The volume of a tetrahedron with vertices at mutual distances dij​=∣vi​−vj​∣ can be computed without coordinates via:

288,V2=​0​1​1​1​1 1​0​d122​​d132​​d142​ 1​d122​​0​d232​​d242​ 1​d132​​d232​​0​d342​ 1​d142​​d242​​d342​​0​​

This is the Cayley–Menger determinant. It is purely metric — only pairwise distances enter.

Consequence: The shape of a tetrahedron (up to rigid motion and reflection) is completely determined by its 6 edge lengths.

Check for regular tetrahedron (all dij​=a): All off-diagonal entries =a2, giving 288V2=2a6, so V=122​a3​=122​​a3. ✓

§11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

The tetrahedron is not merely a topic in solid geometry — it appears, often unexpectedly, at the foundations of modern ML.

11.1 Simplices and Probability Simplices

The probability simplex Δn−1=p∈Rn:pi​≥0,∑pi​=1 is the direct generalisation of the tetrahedron.

For n=4: the set of all probability distributions over 4 classes is a 3-simplex — a tetrahedron. Each vertex is a one-hot distribution (total certainty about one class); interior points are mixed distributions.

        p₁=1                    "Class 1 certain"
         ▲
        /|\
       / | \
      /  |  \
     ▼───┼───▼
   p₂=1  |  p₃=1
         ▼
        p₄=1
   
   Interior = all mixed distributions
   over 4 classes

Softmax maps logits z∈Rn to the interior of Δn−1:

softmax(z)i​=∑j​ezj​ezi​​∈Δn−1

Every classification head of a neural network outputs a point in a probability simplex.

11.2 Barycentric Interpolation in Neural Rendering and Graphics

NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) and rasterization-based rendering pipelines use barycentric coordinates constantly — every fragment shader interpolates vertex attributes (colour, normal, UV) using the λi​ of the pixel’s position within the triangle/tetrahedron.

The barycentric coordinate formula from §6.2 is the engine behind every real-time 3D renderer and neural rendering pipeline.

11.3 Tetrahedral Meshes in Physics-Informed Neural Networks

FEM (Finite Element Method) discretises a 3D domain into tetrahedra. The volume formula (§3) and barycentric coordinates (§6) determine the element stiffness matrices. PINNs that operate on unstructured 3D grids inherit exactly this tetrahedral structure.

The quality of a tetrahedral mesh — measured by the aspect ratio R/r=3 for a regular tetrahedron — directly controls numerical conditioning of the resulting linear systems that neural solvers aim to bypass or approximate.

11.4 Simplex Methods in Optimisation

The Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm (used in hyperparameter tuning before gradient-based methods dominated) maintains a tetrahedron (in 3D) or n-simplex (in nD) in parameter space, and iteratively reflects, expands, or contracts it toward a minimum.

The operations — reflection through the face centroid, expansion along the axis — are exactly the affine operations on barycentric coordinates.

11.5 Tetrahedral Geometry in Molecular ML

Graph neural networks for molecular property prediction (e.g., SchNet, DimeNet++, GemNet) must represent 3D molecular geometry. Tetrahedral carbon (sp3) is the fundamental structural motif. The dihedral angle ≈109.47° (§7) is a hard geometric constraint that symmetry-aware GNNs must respect.

SE(3)-equivariant networks (e.g., NequIP, SEGNN) encode both edge vectors and face normals of local tetrahedral environments as features — directly using the cross product structure of §4.

11.6 Convex Hull and Support Vector Machines

The minimum enclosing simplex problem — find the smallest simplex containing a set of points — is equivalent to finding the convex hull and relates to the maximum margin problem in SVMs.

A hard-margin SVM in R3 with 4 support vectors defines a tetrahedral margin geometry. The decision boundary is the hyperplane equidistant from two tetrahedrally arranged point clouds.

11.7 Topology in Deep Learning — Simplicial Complexes

Topological Data Analysis (TDA) and simplicial neural networks (e.g., SNN, SAT) use simplicial complexes — collections of vertices, edges, triangles, tetrahedra — to model higher-order interactions in data.

The tetrahedron Δ3 is the basic 3-cell. Simplicial homology counts “holes” of different dimensions; in a simplicial complex built from data points, a 3-cycle involving tetrahedra detects 3D voids, e.g., protein pockets or topological features of latent spaces.
