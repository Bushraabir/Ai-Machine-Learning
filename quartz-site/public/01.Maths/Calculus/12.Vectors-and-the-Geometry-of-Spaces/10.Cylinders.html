“A cylinder is what you get when you take a curve and drag it through space — the simplest act of extrusion, and yet the seed of every convolutional filter.”

References: Stewart §12.6 | MIT 18.02 Lecture Notes §5

0. A Personal Note Before We Start

When you first see “cylinder” in a multivariable calculus course, the instinct is to think of a tin can. Resist that. The calculus definition of cylinder is vastly more general, and vastly more important.

In MIT 18.02 and Stewart §12.6, a cylinder is not a round tube — it is any surface generated by moving a line (called a ruling) parallel to a fixed direction, sweeping out above a curve in a coordinate plane. This single idea:

  • Produces parabolic cylinders, elliptic cylinders, hyperbolic cylinders, and yes, the circular cylinder
  • Encodes the idea that one variable is missing from the equation — a profound observation
  • Is the 3D prototype of translational symmetry, which underlies convolutional neural networks

Let’s be precise.

1. The Fundamental Definition

Definition (Cylinder). Let C be a plane curve and d a direction vector not parallel to the plane containing C. The cylinder with directrix C and ruling direction d is the surface swept by moving a line through each point of C parallel to d:

S=,P+t,d;∣;P∈C,;t∈R,

Each line P+td:t∈R is called a ruling or generator of the cylinder.

        z
        |   ___---___         ← top "copy" of C
        |  /         \
        | |     C'    |
        |  \___   ___/
        |      ---            ← rulings (lines parallel to z-axis)
        |   ___---___
        |  /         \
        | |     C     |       ← directrix C in the xy-plane
        |  \___   ___/
        └──────────────── x,y

The critical observation for computation: When the ruling direction is parallel to a coordinate axis, the equation of the cylinder is exactly the equation of the directrix curve, with the ruling variable absent.

2. Cylinders with Rulings Parallel to a Coordinate Axis

This is the standard case in Stewart and MIT 18.02. The key rule:

If an equation in x,y,z is missing one variable, its graph is a cylinder whose rulings are parallel to the axis of the missing variable.

This is worth proving carefully.

Theorem. Let f(x,y)=0 be the equation of a curve C in the xy-plane. Then the set S=(x,y,z)∈R3:f(x,y)=0 is a cylinder with directrix C and rulings parallel to the z-axis.

Proof. If (x0​,y0​,0)∈C, then f(x0​,y0​)=0. For any t∈R, the point (x0​,y0​,t) also satisfies f(x0​,y0​)=0 since z does not appear. So the entire vertical line through (x0​,y0​,0) parallel to the z-axis lies in S. Conversely, every point in S lies on such a line. Hence S is swept by vertical lines over C. □

The same argument applies by permutation to equations missing x (rulings ∥ x-axis) or missing y (rulings ∥ y-axis).

3. The Standard Cylinders and Their Equations

3.1 Circular Cylinder

x2+y2=r2​

Parametric form: r(u,v)=(rcosu,;rsinu,;v),u∈[0,2π),;v∈R

3.3 Elliptic Cylinder

a2x2​+b2y2​=1​

Missing variable: z. Rulings: parallel to z-axis.

Directrix: ellipse with semi-axes a (along x) and b (along y) in the xy-plane.

Every horizontal slice is the same ellipse. If a=b=r: circular cylinder.

Example. 4x2​+9y2​=1: ellipse with a=2, b=3 extruded vertically.

3.4 Hyperbolic Cylinder

a2x2​−b2y2​=1​

Missing variable: z. Rulings: parallel to z-axis.

Directrix: hyperbola in the xy-plane. The surface consists of two sheets (one for x>0, one for x<0), each swept vertically.

Cross-sections at z=k: always the same hyperbola a2x2​−b2y2​=1.

4. General Equation Recognition: The One-Variable-Missing Rule

The practical test:

  1. Look at the equation in x,y,z.
  2. Which variable is absent? That axis is the ruling direction.
  3. What curve does the remaining equation describe in 2D? That’s the directrix.
  4. The 3D surface is that curve extruded along the missing axis.

Examples:

y2+z2=4xCircle in yz-planeCircular cylinder, axis = x-axis
x=y2−1zParabola in xy-planeParabolic cylinder
xy=1zHyperbola xy=1 in xy-planeHyperbolic cylinder (rotated 45°)
z=sinxySine wave in xz-planeSinusoidal cylinder
y=exzExponential curve in xy-planeExponential cylinder

That last one — z=sinx — is an infinite corrugated sheet, the cross-section of certain Fourier components.

5. Parametric Representations

For computation and rendering, parametric forms are essential.

General cylinder with directrix C:r(u)=(x(u),y(u),0) and ruling direction d=(0,0,1): R(u,v)=r(u)+v,d=(x(u),;y(u),;v)

Specific cases:

Circular: R(u,v)=(rcosu,;rsinu,;v)

Elliptic: R(u,v)=(acosu,;bsinu,;v)

Parabolic: R(u,v)=(u,;v,;u2)

The normal vector to the surface at a point is: n=∂u∂R​×∂v∂R​

For the circular cylinder: ∂u​R=(−rsinu,rcosu,0), ∂v​R=(0,0,1): n=(−rsinu,rcosu,0)×(0,0,1)=(rcosu,;rsinu,;0)

This points radially outward — the normal to a circular cylinder is horizontal (perpendicular to the axis), as expected.

6. Volume and Surface Area of a Finite Circular Cylinder

The finite right circular cylinder of radius r and height h (the “tin can”):

V=πr2h​

Derivation. By Cavalieri’s principle: every horizontal cross-section at height z∈[0,h] is a disk of area πr2. Integrating: V=∫0h​πr2,dz=πr2h□

Lateral surface area (curved side only): Alateral​=2πrh​

Derivation. Unroll the cylinder: it becomes a rectangle of width 2πr (circumference) and height h.

Total surface area (including top and bottom disks): Atotal​=2πrh+2πr2=2πr(h+r)​

7. Level Curves and Cylinders: The Deep Connection

This is a key conceptual link between cylinders and multivariable calculus.

Definition. For f:R2→R, the level curve at height c is (x,y):f(x,y)=c.

Now lift to 3D: the set (x,y,z):f(x,y)=c is exactly a cylinder over the level curve, with rulings parallel to z.

The graph z=f(x,y) intersects the horizontal plane z=c in the curve f(x,y)=c — precisely the level curve.

So: every level curve of f is the directrix of a vertical cylinder. Visualizing f by its level curves is visualizing a family of cylinders stacked up.

Example. f(x,y)=x2+y2. Level curves: x2+y2=c (circles). The graph is a paraboloid; its level sets are circular cylinders x2+y2=c.

This is why topographic maps (level curves) and 3D surfaces are interchangeable representations — and why gradient descent on a loss function moves perpendicular to the level curves of the loss.

8. Intersection of a Cylinder with a Plane

Problem. Intersect the circular cylinder x2+y2=r2 with the plane z=mx+c.

Substitute z=mx+c into the parametric form (rcosθ,rsinθ,z):

z=mrcosθ+c

So the intersection curve in 3D is: r(θ)=(rcosθ,;rsinθ,;mrcosθ+c)

This is an ellipse in the cutting plane. (This is how Dandelin proved properties of conic sections — cutting a cone or cylinder with planes always gives conics.)

Special case: m=0 (horizontal cut): z=c — a circle.

9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

9.1 The “Missing Variable” Principle = Translational Symmetry = Convolution

The defining property of a cylinder is: the equation is independent of one variable. In other words, the surface looks the same no matter where you are along the ruling direction.

This is translational invariance — the single most important structural prior in convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

A 1D convolutional filter w(u) applied to a signal f(x): (output)(x)=∫w(u),f(x−u),du

produces the same transformation regardless of where in x you are — the filter doesn’t care about absolute position, only relative shape. This is exactly the cylinder principle: the defining equation (the filter) is independent of the “ruling” direction (absolute position x).

In a 2D CNN layer, a filter W(u,v) slides over an image I(x,y) producing feature maps — a 2D version of cylindrical translational symmetry.

The cylinder is the geometric axiom underlying all convolutional architectures.

9.2 Decision Boundaries as Cylinders in High Dimensions

In a binary classifier operating on n-dimensional input, a cylindrical decision boundary in Rn is a surface that depends on only k<n features. For example, a classifier using only x1​ and x2​ (ignoring x3​,…,xn​) has a decision boundary that is a cylinder in Rn — the equation is f(x1​,x2​)=0 regardless of the other coordinates.

This connects to feature selection: identifying which variables the boundary depends on is equivalent to identifying the “ruling directions” of the boundary cylinder.

9.3 Loss Function Geometry: Cylindrical Valleys

If a loss function L(θ1​,θ2​) satisfies L(θ1​,θ2​)=g(θ1​) (independent of θ2​), then its level sets in parameter space are parabolic or circular cylinders — infinitely long troughs. Gradient descent in this case cannot determine the optimal θ2​ — it slides freely along the ruling direction.

This is the geometric picture behind:

  • Underdetermined systems in linear regression (infinitely many solutions)
  • Flat directions in neural network loss landscapes
  • The need for regularization (adding curvature in the cylinder’s ruling direction)
  Loss
    ↑         ___
    |       /     \          ← parabolic cylinder: L(θ₁,θ₂) = θ₁²
    |      |       |            flat in the θ₂ direction
    |       \___/
    └──────────────── θ₁
   /
  θ₂   ← gradient = 0 in this direction (flat valley)

9.4 Gaussian Kernels and Cylindrical Symmetry

The Gaussian kernel in 2D: K(x,y)=e−(x2+y2)/2σ2

has circular symmetry — it depends only on r=x2+y2​, i.e., is constant on circular cylinders x2+y2=c. This is why isotropic Gaussian kernels blur equally in all directions.

An anisotropic Gaussian K(x,y)=e−(x2/σx2​+y2/σy2​)/2 has elliptic cylindrical level sets — blurring more in one direction. This is used in edge-detection filters and attention mechanisms.

9.5 Volumetric Data in 3D CNNs

In 3D convolutional networks (used in medical imaging, protein structure prediction like AlphaFold), filters slide along three axes. When a 3D CNN learns a filter that activates on a tubular structure (e.g., a blood vessel), it is literally learning to detect a cylindrical pattern — a feature that is approximately invariant along the vessel’s axis.

10. Worked Examples

Example 1: Identify and sketch

What surface is y2+z2=9?

Missing variable: x. Directrix: circle y2+z2=9 in the yz-plane (radius 3). Rulings: parallel to x-axis.

Surface: Circular cylinder of radius 3 with axis along the x-axis.

Any point (t,3cosθ,3sinθ) for t∈R,θ∈[0,2π) lies on the surface.

Example 2: Equation from description

Write the equation of the parabolic cylinder whose directrix is the parabola z=y2−2y in the yz-plane, with rulings parallel to x.

Since rulings are parallel to x (so x is absent): z=y2−2y​.

Complete the square: z=(y−1)2−1, i.e., z+1=(y−1)2. Vertex of the parabola at (y,z)=(1,−1) in the yz-plane.

Example 3: Intersection with a plane

Describe the curve of intersection of x2+y2=4 and z=x+3.

Parametrize the cylinder: (2cost,2sint,z). Substitute into the plane: z=2cost+3.

Intersection: (2cost,;2sint,;2cost+3) — an ellipse tilted in 3D.

11. Exercises

  1. Identify each surface: (a) x2+4z2=16, (b) y=lnx, (c) z2−x2=1, (d) x2+y2=2x.

  2. Prove that the intersection of a right circular cylinder x2+y2=r2 with any non-horizontal plane ax+by+cz=d (c=0) is an ellipse (or a circle when a=b=0).

  3. Parametrize the elliptic cylinder 9x2​+4z2​=1 and compute the normal vector at a general point. Verify it is perpendicular to the ruling direction.

  4. Level sets: Sketch the level curves of f(x,y)=x2+4y2 for c=1,4,9 and describe the corresponding cylinders in R3.

  5. ML connection: A 2-layer neural network with 2 inputs and 1 hidden ReLU neuron computes f(x1​,x2​)=ReLU(w1​x1​+w2​x2​+b). The level set f=0 in the (x1​,x2​) plane is a line. Lift to R3 by treating the output as a third coordinate — describe the shape of the graph z=f(x1​,x2​) in terms of cylinders.
