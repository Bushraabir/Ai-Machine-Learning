12.Chain Rule

12.Chain Rule

Apr 23, 202615 min read

“The chain rule is not a formula. It is a theorem about the composition of linear approximations. Everything else — backpropagation, automatic differentiation, implicit differentiation — is a corollary.”

§1. Motivation — Composition and Rate of Change

In single-variable calculus, if y=f(u) and u=g(x), then:

dxdy​=dudy​⋅dxdu​

This is almost too clean. The fractions “cancel.” But in multiple variables, the story is richer: a function can depend on many intermediate variables, each of which depends on many others. The chain rule tells us how to trace the influence of a change at the source through every path it takes to the output.

The multivariable chain rule is not one formula — it is a family of formulas, all instances of one underlying theorem: the derivative of a composition is the composition of the derivatives.

§2. The One-Variable Chain Rule — The Right Way to Think About It

Let f:R→R and g:R→R, both differentiable. Define h=f∘g, i.e., h(x)=f(g(x)).

Theorem (Chain Rule, 1D). h′(x)=f′(g(x))⋅g′(x).

Proof sketch. By differentiability of g at x: g(x+Δx)=g(x)+g′(x)Δx+ε1​Δx,ε1​→0

By differentiability of f at u=g(x): f(u+Δu)=f(u)+f′(u)Δu+ε2​Δu,ε2​→0

Setting Δu=g(x+Δx)−g(x)=g′(x)Δx+ε1​Δx:

h(x+Δx)−h(x)=f′(g(x))[g′(x)Δx+ε1​Δx]+ε2​[g′(x)Δx+ε1​Δx]

Divide by Δx and let Δx→0. The error terms vanish: h′(x)=f′(g(x))g′(x). ■

The key insight. The chain rule says: the linear approximation of a composition is the composition of the linear approximations. Derivatives linearize; linear maps compose by multiplication. That is all.

§3. The Multivariable Chain Rule — General Statement

Let g:Rn→Rm be differentiable at a, and f:Rm→R be differentiable at g(a). Define h=f∘g:Rn→R.

Theorem (Chain Rule, General). h is differentiable at a, and:

dha​=dfg(a)​∘dga​

In matrix/coordinate form, if g=(g1​,…,gm​) and x=(x1​,…,xn​):

∂xj​∂h​=∑i=1m​∂ui​∂f​⋅∂xj​∂gi​​

or equivalently: Jh​=Jf​⋅Jg​ (Jacobian of composition = product of Jacobians).

In differential notation (the most elegant form):

dh=df=∑i=1m​∂ui​∂f​,dui​,dui​=∑j=1n​∂xj​∂gi​​,dxj​

Substitute dui​ into dh: you get the chain rule automatically, by pure algebraic substitution.

§4. Case I — One Independent Variable

z=f(x,y), x=x(t), y=y(t). Then z is a function of t alone:

dtdz​=∂x∂f​dtdx​+∂y∂f​dtdy​

Think of it as: every path from t to z must pass through either x or y. Sum the contributions along each path:

        t
       / \
      x   y
       \ /
        z

Each edge contributes a derivative; each path contributes a product; sum over all paths.

Example. z=x2+sin(xy), x=et, y=t2. Find dtdz​.

∂x∂z​=2x+ycos(xy),∂y∂z​=xcos(xy) dtdx​=et,dtdy​=2t

dtdz​=(2x+ycos(xy))et+xcos(xy)⋅2t

Substituting x=et, y=t2 gives the explicit form if needed.

§5. Case II — Two Independent Variables

z=f(x,y), x=x(s,t), y=y(s,t). Now z is a function of s and t:

∂s∂z​=∂x∂f​∂s∂x​+∂y∂f​∂s∂y​

∂t∂z​=∂x∂f​∂t∂x​+∂y∂f​∂t∂y​

Dependency diagram:

      s   t         s   t
       \ /           \ /
        x             y
         \           /
          \         /
               z

For ∂z/∂s: trace all paths from s to z. Path s→x→z contributes ∂s∂x​⋅∂x∂z​. Path s→y→z contributes ∂s∂y​⋅∂y∂z​. Sum them.

Example. z=exy, x=s2−t2, y=s+2t.

∂s∂z​=yexy(2s)+xexy(1)=(2sy+x)exy ∂t∂z​=yexy(−2t)+xexy(2)=(−2ty+2x)exy

§6. The Tree Diagram Rule — A Systematic Algorithm

For any composition, the chain rule is computed by the following algorithm:

Draw the dependency diagram. Each variable is a node. An arrow from u to v means ”v depends on u.”

For each path from an independent variable to the target, form the product of derivatives along that path.

Sum over all such paths.

This is not a heuristic — it is a direct consequence of the general formula ∂xj​∂z​=∑i​∂ui​∂z​∂xj​∂ui​​.

Example — Three layers. w=f(x,y,z), each of x,y,z depending on s and t:

∂s∂w​=∂x∂w​∂s∂x​+∂y∂w​∂s∂y​+∂z∂w​∂s∂z​

Three paths from s to w; each contributes one term.

§7. Implicit Differentiation — The Chain Rule in Disguise

Suppose F(x,y)=0 defines y implicitly as a function of x. Differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y=y(x):

dxd​F(x,y(x))=0

Chain rule on the left:

Fx​⋅1+Fy​⋅dxdy​=0⟹dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​​

For F(x,y,z)=0 defining z=z(x,y):

∂x∂z​=−Fz​Fx​​,∂y∂z​=−Fz​Fy​​

Proof. Differentiate F(x,y,z(x,y))=0 with respect to x, holding y fixed:

Fx​⋅1+Fy​⋅0+Fz​⋅∂x∂z​=0

Solve for ∂x∂z​. The formula follows immediately. ■

Example. x3+y3+z3−3xyz=1. Find ∂x∂z​.

F=x3+y3+z3−3xyz. Then Fx​=3x2−3yz, Fz​=3z2−3xy.

∂x∂z​=−3z2−3xy3x2−3yz​=z2−xyyz−x2​

§8. The Chain Rule in Differential Notation

In differentials, the chain rule requires no separate statement — it is automatic substitution.

z=f(u,v), u=u(x,y), v=v(x,y):

dz=fu​,du+fv​,dv

Now substitute du=ux​,dx+uy​,dy and dv=vx​,dx+vy​,dy:

dz=fu​(ux​,dx+uy​,dy)+fv​(vx​,dx+vy​,dy) =(fu​ux​+fv​vx​),dx+(fu​uy​+fv​vy​),dy

Read off ∂x∂z​ and ∂y∂z​ as coefficients. The chain rule is invisible — it’s already encoded in the substitution.

§9. The Jacobian Formulation

For f:Rn→Rm and g:Rp→Rn, the Jacobian of f∘g is:

Jf∘g​(x)=Jf​(g(x))⋅Jg​(x)

where Jf​ is m×n and Jg​ is n×p, so the product is m×p. The chain rule is matrix multiplication of Jacobians.

Coordinate form:

(Jf∘g​)ij​=∑k=1n​(Jf​)ik​(Jg​)kj​=∑k​∂uk​∂fi​​∂xj​∂gk​​

This is the master formula from which every special case follows.

§10. Special Cases Worth Knowing

10.1 — Gradient of a Composition

h(x)=f(g(x)), f:Rm→R, g:Rn→Rm:

∇h(x)=Jg​(x)⊤∇f(g(x))

The gradient transforms by the transpose Jacobian. This is the adjoint, and it is exactly why backpropagation uses transposed weight matrices.

10.2 — Directional Derivative via Chain Rule

The directional derivative of f at a in direction u^ is obtained by setting x(t)=a+tu^ and differentiating at t=0:

Du^​f=dtd​​t=0​f(a+tu^)=∇f(a)⋅u^

The chain rule converts a directional derivative into a dot product with the gradient.

10.3 — Tangent Vector to a Curve on a Surface

If r(t) is a curve on a level surface f(x)=c, then f(r(t))=c for all t. Differentiate:

∇f(r(t))⋅r′(t)=0

The gradient is perpendicular to every tangent vector on the surface — a purely chain-rule proof.

10.4 — Change of Variables in PDEs

Suppose u=u(x,t) satisfies a PDE, and we change variables: ξ=x−ct, τ=t. Then:

∂x∂u​=∂ξ∂u​∂x∂ξ​+∂τ∂u​∂x∂τ​=∂ξ∂u​⋅1+∂τ∂u​⋅0=∂ξ∂u​

∂t∂u​=∂ξ∂u​∂t∂ξ​+∂τ∂u​∂t∂τ​=∂ξ∂u​(−c)+∂τ∂u​

So ∂t∂u​+c∂x∂u​=0 becomes ∂τ∂u​=0: the transport equation transforms to a trivial PDE. The chain rule is the engine of every change-of-variables in PDEs.

§11. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Polar Laplacian

Express ∂x2∂2f​+∂y2∂2f​ in polar coordinates x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ.

Step 1. Compute ∂r∂f​ and ∂θ∂f​ by chain rule:

∂r∂f​=fx​cosθ+fy​sinθ ∂θ∂f​=−fx​rsinθ+fy​rcosθ

Step 2. Invert (solve for fx​, fy​):

fx​=cosθ,∂r∂f​−rsinθ​∂θ∂f​ fy​=sinθ,∂r∂f​+rcosθ​∂θ∂f​

Step 3. Differentiate again (apply the operators ∂x∂​ and ∂y∂​ using the chain rule on the result). After careful computation:

∇2f=fxx​+fyy​=∂r2∂2f​+r1​∂r∂f​+r21​∂θ2∂2f​

The Laplacian in polar coordinates, derived entirely by chain rule.

Example 2 — Sensitivity Analysis

Revenue R=p⋅q(p,a) where p is price and a is advertising spend. Find dpdR​ when p and a are both functions of a budget constraint parameter λ.

dλdR​=∂p∂R​dλdp​+∂a∂R​dλda​

∂p∂R​=q+p∂p∂q​,∂a∂R​=p∂a∂q​

The chain rule decomposes the total effect into direct and indirect channels — a standard tool in economics.

Example 3 — Second Derivative via Chain Rule

Let z=f(u), u=x2+y2. Compute ∂x2∂2z​.

First derivative:

∂x∂z​=f′(u)⋅2x

Second derivative (product rule + chain rule):

∂x2∂2z​=∂x∂​[f′(u)⋅2x]=f′′(u)∂x∂u​⋅2x+f′(u)⋅2 =f′′(u)(2x)2+2f′(u)=4x2f′′(u)+2f′(u)

Similarly ∂y2∂2z​=4y2f′′(u)+2f′(u). So:

∇2z=4(x2+y2)f′′(u)+4f′(u)=4u,f′′(u)+4f′(u)

§12. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The chain rule is not merely related to machine learning. It is machine learning. The entire discipline of differentiable programming rests on one fact: you can differentiate a composition by multiplying Jacobians.

12.1 — Backpropagation Is the Chain Rule

A neural network is a composition:

L=ℓ∘fL​∘fL−1​∘⋯∘f1​

where fk​(x)=σk​(Wk​x+bk​) is the k-th layer. Define intermediate activations:

a0​=x,ak​=fk​(ak−1​),L=ℓ(aL​)

By the Jacobian chain rule:

∂ak​∂L​=∂ak+1​∂L​⋅Jfk+1​​(ak​)

Backpropagation is this recursion, run from k=L down to k=0. The “error signal” δk​=∂ak​∂L​ propagates backward through transposed Jacobians.

For a single layer zk​=Wk​ak−1​+bk​, ak​=σ(zk​):

∂Wk​∂L​=δk​⋅ak−1⊤​,δk−1​=Wk⊤​δk​⊙σ′(zk−1​)

The transpose Wk⊤​ is the adjoint of the Jacobian J=Wk​ (since the layer is linear in ak−1​). This is §10.1 above, instantiated for neural networks.

12.2 — Forward Mode vs Reverse Mode

The chain rule can be evaluated in two directions:

Forward mode (tangent computation). Start with da0​=dx and propagate forward:

dak​=Jfk​​(ak−1​)⋅dak−1​

Cost per forward pass: O(n) Jacobian-vector products (one per input). Efficient when n≪m (few inputs).

Reverse mode (cotangent computation, backprop). Start with Lˉ=1 and propagate backward:

aˉk−1​=Jfk​​(ak−1​)⊤⋅aˉk​

Cost: O(m) vector-Jacobian products (one per output). Efficient when m≪n. For neural networks: m=1 (scalar loss), n= billions of parameters. Reverse mode wins decisively.

This is why backpropagation is efficient: it is reverse-mode automatic differentiation, which is the chain rule evaluated right-to-left on the computation graph.

12.3 — Computation Graphs and the Chain Rule

Every computation in a neural network can be represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where:

  • Nodes are intermediate values
  • Edges encode dependencies
  • Each edge carries a local Jacobian

The chain rule says: the gradient at any node is the sum over all paths from that node to the output, of the product of edge Jacobians along each path.

Input x → [Layer 1] → a₁ → [Layer 2] → a₂ → ... → [Loss] → ℒ
              ↑                  ↑
           ∂a₁/∂x           ∂a₂/∂a₁

Backprop traverses this graph in reverse topological order, accumulating products. This is exactly the tree diagram rule from §6, instantiated for computation graphs.

12.4 — Vanishing and Exploding Gradients

For a deep network with L layers and activation σ, the gradient at layer k involves the product:

∂ak​∂L​=∂aL​∂L​⋅∏j=kL−1​Jfj+1​​

Each factor Jfj​​=Wj⊤​diag(σ′(zj​)) has spectral radius (largest singular value) roughly ∣Wj​∣⋅max∣σ′∣.

  • If ∣Jj​∣<1 for most j: the product →0 exponentially fast. Vanishing gradients. Early layers learn nothing.
  • If ∣Jj​∣>1 for most j: the product →∞ exponentially fast. Exploding gradients. Training diverges.

Both pathologies are pure consequences of the chain rule applied to a long product of matrices. Solutions: careful initialization (Xavier/He), batch normalization (controls ∣Jj​∣), residual connections (add identity to the Jacobian), gradient clipping.

ReLU activations have σ′(x)∈0,1, which helps with vanishing gradients compared to sigmoid (σ′≤0.25). This is a chain rule argument.

12.5 — Recurrent Neural Networks and BPTT

In an RNN, the hidden state evolves as ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​;W). The gradient of loss at time T with respect to parameters at time k involves:

∂W∂LT​​​step k​=∂hT​∂LT​​(∏j=kT−1​∂hj​∂hj+1​​)∂W∂hk​​

The product of T−k Jacobians ∂hj​∂hj+1​​ is the chain rule unrolled through time — called Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT). The vanishing/exploding gradient problem is especially severe here because T can be hundreds of steps. LSTMs and GRUs introduce gating mechanisms that approximately maintain ​∏∂hj​∂hj+1​​​≈1.

12.6 — Residual Networks (ResNets) — The Chain Rule Fix

A residual block computes ak+1​=ak​+F(ak​). Its Jacobian:

∂ak​∂ak+1​​=I+JF​(ak​)

The identity I ensures that the product of Jacobians through L residual blocks always contains the identity term:

∏k=1L​(I+JFk​​)

This expands (like a polynomial) into a sum of 2L terms, most of which contain fewer than L Jacobian factors. Some paths are short — gradients can flow through them without vanishing. This is the chain rule explanation of why ResNets can be trained to depth 1000+.

§14. Theorems to Know Cold

Theorem 1 (Chain Rule). If g is differentiable at a and f is differentiable at g(a), then f∘g is differentiable at a and d(f∘g)a​=dfg(a)​∘dga​.

Theorem 2 (Implicit Function Theorem). If F(x,y) is C1 near (a,b) with F(a,b)=0 and Fy​(a,b)=0, then there exists a C1 function y=g(x) near a with F(x,g(x))=0, and ∂xi​∂g​=−Fy​Fxi​​​.

Theorem 3 (Gradient Orthogonality). If r(t) lies on the level set f=c, then ∇f(r(t))⊥r′(t) for all t.

Theorem 4 (Jacobian of Inverse). If f:Rn→Rn is invertible and differentiable, then Jf−1​(f(x))=[Jf​(x)]−1.

§15. Things That Will Trip You Up

∂z/∂x means different things depending on context. If z=f(x,y) and y depends on x, you must decide: are you holding y fixed (partial), or letting it vary (total derivative)? Sloppy notation kills you here.

The chain rule requires differentiability, not just existence of partial derivatives. If g is merely continuous, the composition may not be differentiable.

Implicit differentiation is not magic. It is the chain rule + implicit function theorem. Always verify Fy​=0 before writing dy/dx=−Fx​/Fy​.

Second derivatives require the chain rule twice, and produce extra terms. ∂x2∂2z​=(f′′(u))(ux​)2 alone — you must also include f′(u)uxx​.

In backprop, the gradient of the loss with respect to a parameter Wk​ is not δk​ alone — it requires the outer product δk​ak−1⊤​. Confusing the upstream gradient with the parameter gradient is a common error in manual backprop derivations.
