“The partial derivative is a coward — it only moves along coordinate axes. The directional derivative is the full general.” — Professor’s notebook, MIT 18.02

§0. Motivation: Why Partial Derivatives Are Not Enough

Suppose you are standing on a mountain whose height is given by f(x,y). The partial derivatives ∂f/∂x and ∂f/∂y tell you the rate of change only along the east and north directions, respectively.

But you might want to hike northeast, or southwest, or in any arbitrary direction. The directional derivative is precisely the tool that answers: “How fast is f changing as I walk in direction u?”

This is not a philosophical question — in machine learning, gradient descent asks exactly this, and the directional derivative is the engine underneath.

§1. Setup and Notation

Let:

  • f:Rn→R be a scalar field (a function of n real variables)
  • a∈Rn be a point in the domain
  • u∈Rn be a unit vector (i.e., ∣u∣=1) indicating a direction

We want to measure how f changes at a in the direction u.

Convention (Stewart & MIT 18.02): The direction vector u must be a unit vector. If you are given a direction vector v that is not normalized, compute u=v/∣v∣ first.

§2. The Formal Definition

Definition 2.1 (Directional Derivative)

The directional derivative of f at a in the direction u is defined as:

Du​f(a):=limh→0​hf(a+hu)−f(a)​

provided this limit exists.

Unpacking the definition:

  • a+hu is a point on the line through a in direction u, at signed distance h from a
  • The numerator is the change in f over that displacement
  • Dividing by h and taking h→0 gives the instantaneous rate of change

Geometric picture (2D): Consider the vertical plane through a in direction u. This plane slices the surface z=f(x,y) in a curve. The directional derivative is the slope of this curve at the point above a.

         z = f(x,y)
              |   /← slope here is D_u f(a)
              |  /
              | /
     ─────────●──────────────► direction u
              a

Remark: Partial Derivatives as Special Cases

The partial derivatives are directional derivatives in the coordinate directions: ∂xi​∂f​(a)=Dei​​f(a) where ei​ is the i-th standard basis vector. The directional derivative strictly generalizes this.

§3. The Computational Theorem

The limit definition is conceptually clean but computationally painful. The key theorem connects directional derivatives to the gradient.

Theorem 3.1 (Gradient Formula)

If f is differentiable at a, then for any unit vector u:

Du​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅u​

where ∇f(a)=(∂x1​∂f​,∂x2​∂f​,…,∂xn​∂f​) is the gradient of f at a.

Proof (Full Rigorous Proof)

Setup: Let g:R→R be defined by: g(t)=f(a+tu)

Then by definition: Du​f(a)=limh→0​hf(a+hu)−f(a)​=g′(0)

Applying the Chain Rule: Write x(t)=a+tu, so x(0)=a and x′(t)=u. Then g(t)=f(x(t)).

Since f is differentiable at a, by the multivariable chain rule:

g′(t)=∇f(x(t))⋅x′(t)=∇f(x(t))⋅u

Evaluating at t=0:

g′(0)=∇f(a)⋅u

Therefore Du​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅u. ■

Critical hypothesis: The theorem requires f to be differentiable at a, not merely that partial derivatives exist. Existence of partials alone is insufficient (see §6 for a counterexample).

§4. Geometric Interpretation via the Dot Product

Write the dot product formula using the angle θ between ∇f(a) and u:

Du​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅u=∣∇f(a)∣⋅=1∣u∣​​⋅cosθ=∣∇f(a)∣cosθ

This yields three fundamental facts:

Corollaries

Corollary 4.1 (Direction of Steepest Ascent): The gradient ∇f(a) points in the direction of maximum increase of f, and ∣∇f(a)∣ is the maximum rate of change.

Corollary 4.2 (Level Sets): The gradient ∇f(a) is perpendicular to the level curve (or level surface) f=c passing through a.

§5. Worked Examples

Example 5.1 (Stewart-style)

Problem: Let f(x,y)=x2y+exy. Find Du​f(1,0) in the direction v=⟨3,4⟩.

Step 1: Normalize. ∣v∣=9+16​=5, so u=⟨3/5,4/5⟩.

Step 2: Compute the gradient. ∂x∂f​=2xy+yexy,∂y∂f​=x2+xexy

At (1,0): ∂x∂f​(1,0)=0+0⋅1=0,∂y∂f​(1,0)=1+1⋅1=2

So ∇f(1,0)=⟨0,2⟩.

Step 3: Take the dot product. Du​f(1,0)=⟨0,2⟩⋅⟨3/5,4/5⟩=0⋅53​+2⋅54​=58​

Answer: Du​f(1,0)=8/5.

Example 5.2 (3D — MIT 18.02 style)

Problem: Let f(x,y,z)=x2+2y2−z2. At the point P=(1,1,1):

  1. Find the direction of steepest ascent.
  2. Find the maximum rate of change.
  3. Find a direction in which f does not change.

Gradient: ∇f=⟨2x,4y,−2z⟩⟹∇f(1,1,1)=⟨2,4,−2⟩

  1. Steepest ascent direction: u=∇f/∣∇f∣. ∣∇f∣=4+16+4​=24​=26​ u=26​1​⟨2,4,−2⟩=6​1​⟨1,2,−1⟩

  2. Maximum rate of change: ∣∇f(1,1,1)∣=26​.

  3. Direction of zero change: Any u with ∇f⋅u=0, i.e., any vector perpendicular to ⟨2,4,−2⟩. For example, ⟨2,0,2⟩ (check: 2⋅2+4⋅0+(−2)⋅2=0 ✓). Normalize: u=2​1​⟨1,0,1⟩.

Via definition: Du​f(1,1)=limh→0​h(1+h/2​)2+(1+h/2​)2−2​ =limh→0​h2(1+h/2​)2−2​=limh→0​h2[1+2h/2​+h2/2]−2​ =limh→0​h2h2​+h2​=limh→0​(22​+h)=22​

§6. The Differentiability Hypothesis: A Cautionary Example

The gradient formula Du​f=∇f⋅u requires differentiability, not merely existence of partials. Here is the classic counterexample.

Example 6.1

Define: f(x,y)={x2+y2x2y​​(x,y)=(0,0) 0​(x,y)=(0,0)​

Partial derivatives at origin exist: ∂x∂f​(0,0)=limh→0​hf(h,0)−0​=limh→0​h0​=0 Similarly ∂f/∂y(0,0)=0, so ∇f(0,0)=⟨0,0⟩.

But directional derivative in direction u=⟨cosθ,sinθ⟩: Du​f(0,0)=limh→0​hf(hcosθ,hsinθ)​=limh→0​h(h2cos2θ+h2sin2θ)h2cos2θ⋅hsinθ​=cos2θsinθ

Contradiction: The gradient formula would predict Du​f=⟨0,0⟩⋅u=0 for all u. But we computed Du​f=cos2θsinθ, which is nonzero for θ=π/4.

Conclusion: f is not differentiable at the origin (even though partials exist), so the formula fails. Differentiability is a strictly stronger condition than existence of partial derivatives.

§7. The General Case: f:Rn→Rm

For vector-valued functions F:Rn→Rm, F=(F1​,…,Fm​), the directional derivative is:

Du​F(a)=limh→0​hF(a+hu)−F(a)​=JF​(a)⋅u

where JF​(a) is the Jacobian matrix (m×n): (JF​)ij​=∂xj​∂Fi​​

This is the full generalization: the scalar case has Jacobian = gradient (row vector), and the dot product collapses to a scalar.

§8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This section is not decoration — directional derivatives are the mathematical spine of modern ML.

8.1 Gradient Descent is a Directional Derivative Statement

In supervised learning, we minimize a loss function L(θ) over parameters θ∈Rn.

The gradient descent update rule is: θt+1​=θt​−η∇θ​L(θt​)

Why −∇L?

By Corollary 4.1, the direction of steepest descent is u∗=−∇L/∣∇L∣. The directional derivative in this direction equals −∣∇L∣, the most negative possible value. Hence moving in −∇L decreases L as fast as possible.

In short: gradient descent is the algorithm that always moves in the direction of most negative directional derivative.

8.2 Directional Derivatives in Neural Network Analysis

Width of Loss Landscapes: Understanding a neural network’s behavior involves understanding Du​L for various directions u in parameter space. Sharp minima have large ∣Du​L∣ in many directions; flat minima have small values. This is directly related to generalization.

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM): The SAM optimizer (Foret et al., 2021) explicitly maximizes Du​L over a neighborhood to find flat minima. It solves: max∣ϵ∣≤ρ​L(θ+ϵ)≈L(θ+ρ∣∇L∣∇L​) This is exactly a directional derivative calculation.

8.3 Backpropagation is Directional Derivative Bookkeeping

During backpropagation, we compute ∂L/∂θi​ for each parameter — these are partial derivatives. The full gradient ∇θ​L is assembled from these partials. The gradient is then used as the “directional object” that gradient descent queries through a dot product.

The chain rule proof in §3 is precisely what backpropagation computes layer-by-layer. If the network is F=fL​∘fL−1​∘⋯∘f1​, then backprop computes ∇L by chaining Jacobians — the multivariable version of Theorem 3.1.

8.4 Natural Gradient Descent

Standard gradient descent measures steepness in Euclidean parameter space. But in probability model families (used in RL, variational inference), the natural geometry is not Euclidean — it is the Fisher information metric.

The natural gradient ∇~L=F−1∇L (where F is the Fisher matrix) is the gradient in this curved geometry. This is equivalent to taking directional derivatives with respect to the natural inner product ⟨u,v⟩F​=uTFv instead of the dot product. Amari’s natural gradient descent uses exactly this.

8.5 Directional Derivatives in Adversarial Robustness

In adversarial attacks (FGSM — Fast Gradient Sign Method), the adversarial perturbation is: δ=ϵ⋅sign(∇x​L)

This perturbs the input x in the direction that most increases the loss — again, the direction of maximum directional derivative. The gradient is the primary object, and the attack is literally “move in the direction of steepest ascent of the loss with respect to the input.”

§10. The Gradient as a Linear Functional (Abstract View)

From a functional analysis perspective: if f is differentiable at a, the total derivative (Fréchet derivative) Df(a) is a linear map Rn→R such that:

f(a+h)=f(a)+Df(a)[h]+o(∣h∣)

The directional derivative is simply: Du​f(a)=Df(a)[u]

By the Riesz representation theorem (in Rn with the Euclidean inner product), every bounded linear functional L:Rn→R has a unique vector g such that L(u)=g⋅u. This vector is exactly ∇f(a).

Takeaway: The gradient is the Riesz representer of the total derivative. This is the abstract reason the formula Du​f=∇f⋅u holds. In Riemannian geometry (relevant for natural gradient in ML), the gradient changes because the inner product changes, but the directional derivative Df(a)[u] is metric-independent.

§11. Practice Problems

P1 (Warm-up): Using only the definition (not the gradient formula), compute Du​f(0,0) for f(x,y)=3x2−xy+y2 in direction u=⟨cosθ,sinθ⟩. Then verify with the gradient formula.

P2 (Steepest ascent): For f(x,y,z)=ln(x2+y2+z2), find the unit vector at (1,2,2) in which f increases most rapidly. What is the maximum rate of change?

P3 (Level sets): Show that ∇f(a) is perpendicular to the level surface f=f(a) by using the directional derivative formula and the fact that Du​f=0 for any u tangent to the level surface.

P4 (Non-differentiable): For f(x,y)=∣xy∣/(x2+y2)1/2 at the origin (define f(0,0)=0), compute Du​f(0,0) directly using the definition for u=⟨cosθ,sinθ⟩. Does the gradient formula apply? Why or why not?

P5 (ML-flavored): Suppose a loss function L(θ1​,θ2​)=(θ1​−1)2+4(θ2​−2)2 (a rescaled bowl). At the point θ=(3,3), in which direction does gradient descent step? Compute the directional derivative in that direction and interpret it.
