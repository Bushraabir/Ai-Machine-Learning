“The gradient is not merely a computational device. It is the geometric soul of a differentiable function.”

0. Preamble — Why Should We Care?

Before we define anything, let me state the central thesis of this note:

The gradient ∇f at a point p encodes, in a single vector, the complete first-order local behaviour of f in all directions simultaneously.

This is remarkable. A scalar function f:Rn→R has infinitely many directional derivatives at any point. Yet one vector — the gradient — determines all of them. Understanding why this is true, and what geometric picture it paints, is the entire content of this note.

1. Setup and Definitions

1.1 Partial Derivatives — The Raw Ingredients

Let f:Rn→R be a function of n variables. Write x=(x1​,x2​,…,xn​).

Definition. The partial derivative of f with respect to xi​ at a is: ∂xi​∂f​(a)=limh→0​hf(a+h,ei​)−f(a)​ where ei​ is the i-th standard basis vector.

This is just the ordinary derivative of f when all variables except xi​ are frozen at their values in a.

1.2 The Gradient — Assembling the Partials

Definition. If all partial derivatives of f exist at a, the gradient of f at a is the vector: ∇f(a)=(∂x1​∂f​(a),;∂x2​∂f​(a),;…,;∂xn​∂f​(a))∈Rn

In two variables, this is just: ∇f(x,y)=⟨∂x∂f​,;∂y∂f​⟩

⚠️ A crucial remark. Existence of all partials does not guarantee differentiability (i.e., a good linear approximation). The function f(x,y)=x2+y2xy​ (with f(0,0)=0) has both partials zero at the origin, yet is not even continuous there. Differentiability requires the stronger condition that the linear map h↦∇f(a)⋅h approximates f(a+h)−f(a) with error o(∣h∣). In what follows, we assume f is C1 (continuous partial derivatives), which does guarantee differentiability.

2. The Directional Derivative — Rates of Change in Any Direction

2.1 Definition

Definition. Let u^∈Rn be a unit vector (∣u^∣=1). The directional derivative of f at a in direction u^ is: Du^​f(a)=limh→0​hf(a+hu^)−f(a)​

Note that Dei​​f=∂xi​∂f​, so partial derivatives are special cases.

2.2 The Fundamental Theorem — The Gradient Does It All

Theorem. If f is differentiable at a, then for any unit vector u^: Du^​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅u^

Proof. Define g(t)=f(a+tu^). Then Du^​f(a)=g′(0).

By the chain rule applied to g(t)=f(a1​+tu1​,…,an​+tun​): g′(t)=∑i=1n​∂xi​∂f​(a+tu^)⋅ui​

Setting t=0: g′(0)=∑i=1n​∂xi​∂f​(a)⋅ui​=∇f(a)⋅u^□

This is the engine behind everything. A single dot product with ∇f gives you the rate of change in any direction.

3. The Four Geometric Significances

3.1 Significance I — Direction of Steepest Ascent

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: Du^​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅u^=∣∇f(a)∣cosθ where θ is the angle between ∇f(a) and u^.

This has immediate consequences:

Direction u^cosθDu^​fGeometric meaning
Along ∇f1∥∇f∥Steepest ascent
Against ∇f−1−∥∇f∥Steepest descent
⊥ to ∇f00No change (tangent to level set)

Therefore: ∇f(a) points in the direction of the maximum rate of increase of f at a, and its magnitude ∣∇f(a)∣ is that maximum rate.

Example. Let f(x,y)=x2+4y2 (an elliptic paraboloid). At a=(1,1): ∇f(1,1)=⟨2x,8y⟩​(1,1)​=⟨2,8⟩

  • Direction of steepest ascent: 68​1​⟨2,8⟩
  • Maximum rate of increase: ∣⟨2,8⟩∣=68​=217​
  • If we walk in the direction u^=⟨1,0⟩ (purely along x): Du^​f=⟨2,8⟩⋅⟨1,0⟩=2. This is exactly ∂x∂f​=2. ✓

3.2 Significance II — Perpendicularity to Level Sets (Curves/Surfaces)

This is perhaps the deepest geometric fact about the gradient.

Theorem. Let c=f(a) and let S=x:f(x)=c be the level set through a. If γ(t) is any smooth curve in S with γ(0)=a, then: ∇f(a)⊥γ′(0)

Proof. Since γ(t)∈S for all t, we have f(γ(t))=c for all t. Differentiating with the chain rule: dtd​[f(γ(t))]=∇f(γ(t))⋅γ′(t)=0

At t=0: ∇f(a)⋅γ′(0)=0. Since this holds for any tangent vector to S at a, we conclude ∇f(a) is normal to S at a. □

In R2: The gradient is perpendicular to level curves.

In R3: The gradient is perpendicular to level surfaces; it is the normal vector to the surface f(x,y,z)=c.

Application — Tangent Planes. The equation of the tangent plane to f(x,y,z)=c at a=(a1​,a2​,a3​) is: ∇f(a)⋅(x−a)=0 i.e., fx​(a)(x−a1​)+fy​(a)(y−a2​)+fz​(a)(z−a3​)=0

Example. Tangent plane to the ellipsoid x2+2y2+3z2=6 at (1,1,1):

Let f(x,y,z)=x2+2y2+3z2. Then ∇f=⟨2x,4y,6z⟩.

At (1,1,1): ∇f=⟨2,4,6⟩.

Tangent plane: 2(x−1)+4(y−1)+6(z−1)=0⟹x+2y+3z=6.

3.3 Significance III — The Gradient as the Best Linear Approximation

The full force of differentiability says that near a: f(a+h)≈f(a)+∇f(a)⋅h with error o(∣h∣). This is the linearization of f at a.

Written out in two variables: L(x,y)=f(a,b)+fx​(a,b)(x−a)+fy​(a,b)(y−b)

This is the tangent plane to the graph z=f(x,y) at the point (a,b,f(a,b)).

The gradient is the coefficient vector of the unique affine function that best approximates f locally.

3.4 Significance IV — The Magnitude Measures How “Steep” the Landscape Is

∣∇f(a)∣ is the maximum possible directional derivative at a. Where ∣∇f∣ is large, the function changes rapidly; where it is small (or zero, at a critical point), the function is “flat” locally.

At a critical point ∇f(a)=0: every directional derivative vanishes, meaning a is a candidate for a local extremum or saddle point.

4. A Worked Example — Reading the Gradient Field

Let f(x,y)=sin(x)cos(y).

∇f=⟨cosxcosy,;−sinxsiny⟩

At (0,0): ∇f=⟨1,0⟩ — the function increases most rapidly in the +x direction, and the level curve through the origin has a horizontal tangent (level curve is f=0, i.e., sinxcosy=0, which is x=0 near the origin — a vertical line, and indeed ∇f=⟨1,0⟩⊥⟨0,1⟩). ✓

At (π/2,0): ∇f=⟨0,0⟩ — critical point. Indeed f(π/2,0)=1 is a local maximum. ✓

6. The Gradient in Machine Learning and Deep Learning

This is where the gradient moves from being a beautiful theoretical object to the engine driving virtually all of modern AI.

6.1 Loss Minimization as a Geometric Problem

In ML, we have a loss function L:Rn→R that maps model parameters θ=(θ1​,…,θn​) to a scalar error. The goal is: θ∗=θargmin​;L(θ)

This is precisely the problem of finding a minimum of a scalar function in high-dimensional space. The gradient is the tool.

6.2 Gradient Descent — Following the Steepest Descent Direction

From Significance I: −∇L(θ) is the direction of steepest decrease of L.

Gradient Descent update rule: θt+1​=θt​−η⋅∇θ​L(θt​) where η>0 is the learning rate (step size).

Geometrically: at each step, we stand on the loss landscape and take a step in the direction the mountain descends fastest. This is exactly the gradient perpendicularity principle applied operationally.

Why does this work? For small η, by the linear approximation (Significance III): L(θ−η∇L)≈L(θ)−η∣∇L∣2<L(θ) as long as ∇L=0. The loss is guaranteed to decrease locally.

6.3 Backpropagation — Efficiently Computing ∇θ​L

In a deep neural network, the loss L is a composition of many functions (layers): L=ℓ∘fL​∘fL−1​∘⋯∘f1​

The gradient ∇θ​L is computed via the chain rule applied repeatedly — this is exactly what backpropagation is. It is the multivariable chain rule in disguise.

For layer k with parameters θk​ and output ak​=fk​(ak−1​;θk​): ∂θk​∂L​=∂ak​∂L​⋅∂θk​∂ak​​

The term ∂ak​∂L​ (the upstream gradient) is propagated backward through the network — hence “backpropagation.” The entire algorithm is nothing but systematic gradient computation via the chain rule.

6.4 Geometric Picture of the Loss Landscape

The loss surface L(θ) is a hypersurface in Rn+1. The level sets θ:L(θ)=c are “iso-loss” hypersurfaces in parameter space. By Significance II, ∇θ​L is perpendicular to these surfaces.

  • Critical points (∇L=0): local minima, saddle points, local maxima. In deep networks with millions of parameters, the landscape is extraordinarily complex.
  • Saddle points dominate in high dimensions (exponentially more saddle points than local minima). Understanding the gradient near saddle points drives research in optimization.

Every optimizer is a variation on: compute or approximate ∇L, then use it to update θ.

6.6 Vanishing and Exploding Gradients

In deep networks, ∇L is computed by multiplying many Jacobians (matrix analogues of the gradient) together via the chain rule. If these matrices have eigenvalues <1 or >1, the gradient vanishes or explodes exponentially with depth. This is the vanishing/exploding gradient problem. It is a direct consequence of the geometry of the gradient under repeated composition, and it motivates ResNets, batch normalization, careful weight initialization, etc.

7. The Gradient as a Linear Map — The Mature View

Let me step back and give the more mature perspective that connects this to linear algebra.

If f:Rn→R is differentiable at a, then the derivative Df(a) is a linear map Rn→R, i.e., an element of the dual space (Rn)∗.

The Riesz representation theorem (in the Euclidean inner product) says every such linear functional is represented by a unique vector via the dot product. The gradient ∇f(a) is precisely this representing vector: Df(a)[h]=∇f(a)⋅h

This is why the formula Du^​f=∇f⋅u^ holds for all directions: it is just the linear functional Df(a) evaluated at u^.

The gradient is the Riesz representative of the derivative. It lives in Rn (same space as the domain) because we used the Euclidean inner product to identify Rn with its dual. On a Riemannian manifold with a non-Euclidean metric, the gradient changes accordingly — this is the foundation of the natural gradient in ML.
