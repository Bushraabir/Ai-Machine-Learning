“Finding extrema is the central problem of optimization. Everything else — Lagrange multipliers, second derivative tests, convexity — is commentary.”

0. Preamble — The Problem

Given a function f:Rn→R, we want to find where f is largest or smallest. This question has two flavors:

  1. Unconstrained: optimize f over all of Rn (or an open domain)
  2. Constrained: optimize f subject to g(x)=c (on a curve, surface, etc.)

Both cases reduce to a single geometric idea: at an extremum, the gradient must vanish — or be parallel to the constraint.

1. Absolute vs. Local Extrema

Definition. Let f:D⊆Rn→R.

  • f has an absolute (global) maximum at a∈D if f(a)≥f(x) for all x∈D.
  • f has an absolute (global) minimum at a∈D if f(a)≤f(x) for all x∈D.
  • f has a local maximum at a if f(a)≥f(x) for all x in some open ball around a.
  • f has a local minimum at a if f(a)≤f(x) for all x in some open ball around a.

Existence — Extreme Value Theorem (multivariable). If f is continuous on a closed and bounded set D⊆Rn, then f attains both its absolute maximum and minimum on D.

⚠️ Closed and bounded is essential. On an open domain f(x)=x has no maximum on (0,1). On an unbounded domain f(x,y)=x has no maximum on R2. Both conditions are required.

2. Critical Points — Fermat’s Theorem in Rn

Theorem (Fermat). If f has a local extremum at an interior point a of its domain, and f is differentiable at a, then: ∇f(a)=0

Proof. Fix any direction e^i​. Define g(t)=f(a+te^i​). Then g has a local extremum at t=0, so by the single-variable Fermat theorem: g′(0)=∂xi​∂f​(a)=0 Since i was arbitrary, all partial derivatives vanish, so ∇f(a)=0. □

Definition. A point a where ∇f(a)=0 (or ∇f does not exist) is called a critical point of f.

⚠️ The converse is false. A critical point need not be a local extremum — it could be a saddle point. The gradient vanishing is necessary but not sufficient.

Example of a saddle point. f(x,y)=x2−y2 at the origin.

∇f=⟨2x,−2y⟩=0 at (0,0). But f(t,0)=t2>0 and f(0,t)=−t2<0 for t=0, so (0,0) is neither a local max nor a local min — it is a saddle point (a mountain pass).

3. The Second Derivative Test — Classifying Critical Points

3.1 The Hessian Matrix

At a critical point a, the second-order behavior of f is encoded by the Hessian matrix: Hf​(a)=(fxx​​fxy​ fxy​​fyy​​)​a​

(assuming f∈C2, so fxy​=fyx​ by Clairaut’s theorem).

The Hessian is a real symmetric matrix, so by the spectral theorem all its eigenvalues are real. The signs of these eigenvalues determine the local shape of f.

3.2 The Discriminant (in R2)

Define the discriminant: D(a)=detHf​(a)=fxx​fyy​−(fxy​)2

Theorem (Second Derivative Test). Let a be a critical point of f∈C2.

ConditionClassification
D(a)>0 and fxx​(a)>0Local minimum
D(a)>0 and fxx​(a)<0Local maximum
D(a)<0Saddle point
D(a)=0Inconclusive (higher-order analysis needed)

Why this works. Near a, by Taylor’s theorem with the gradient zero: f(a+h)≈f(a)+21​hTHf​(a),h

The sign of the quadratic form Q(h)=hTHh determines everything:

  • Q>0 for all h=0 (H is positive definite, eigenvalues both positive) ⟹ local min
  • Q<0 for all h=0 (H is negative definite, eigenvalues both negative) ⟹ local max
  • Q takes both signs (H is indefinite, eigenvalues have mixed signs) ⟹ saddle

The discriminant D=λ1​λ2​ (product of eigenvalues = determinant) and fxx​=λ1​+λ2​⋅(rotation terms) encode exactly this.

3.3 Full Worked Example

Let f(x,y)=x3−3x+y2−2y.

Step 1 — Find critical points. ∇f=⟨3x2−3,;2y−2⟩=0 3x2−3=0⟹x=±1,2y−2=0⟹y=1

Critical points: (−1,1) and (1,1).

Step 2 — Compute the Hessian. fxx​=6x,fxy​=0,fyy​=2 Hf​=(6x​0 0​2​)

Step 3 — Apply the test.

At (−1,1): D=(6(−1))(2)−0=−12<0 → saddle point.

At (1,1): D=(6)(2)−0=12>0, fxx​=6>0 → local minimum.

f(1,1)=1−3+1−2=−3.

4. Absolute Extrema on a Closed Bounded Domain

By the Extreme Value Theorem, absolute extrema exist on a closed bounded domain D. The strategy is:

Closed Domain Method

  1. Find all interior critical points (solve ∇f=0)
  2. Examine f on the boundary of D
  3. Compare all values — the largest is the absolute max, the smallest is the absolute min

The boundary examination in step 2 typically reduces to a single-variable optimization problem on each boundary piece.

Example. Find the absolute extrema of f(x,y)=x2−2xy+2y on the square D=[0,3]×[0,2].

Interior critical points: ∇f=⟨2x−2y,;−2x+2⟩=0⟹x=1,;y=1 f(1,1)=1−2+2=1.

Boundary analysis (four edges):

  • y=0,;x∈[0,3]: f=x2. Max at x=3: f=9. Min at x=0: f=0.
  • y=2,;x∈[0,3]: f=x2−4x+4=(x−2)2. Max at x=0 or x=3: f=4. Min at x=2: f=0.
  • x=0,;y∈[0,2]: f=2y. Max at y=2: f=4. Min at y=0: f=0.
  • x=3,;y∈[0,2]: f=9−6y+2y=9−4y. Max at y=0: f=9. Min at y=2: f=1.

Comparing all values: 1,9,0,4,0,4,0,9,1

Absolute maximum: 9 at (3,0). Absolute minimum: 0 at (0,0), (3/2,0)… let me refine — at (0,0) and (2,2).

5. Constrained Optimization — Lagrange Multipliers

5.1 The Geometric Idea

Suppose we want to maximize f(x,y) subject to g(x,y)=c.

Geometrically: we are moving along the constraint curve g=c and asking where f is largest. At the constrained maximum, the level curve of f through that point must be tangent to the constraint curve — otherwise we could slide along the constraint and increase f further.

Two curves are tangent ⟺ they have the same tangent line ⟺ they have parallel normal vectors.

Their normals are ∇f and ∇g. So the condition is: ∇f(a)=λ,∇g(a) for some scalar λ (the Lagrange multiplier).

5.2 The Formal Theorem

Theorem (Lagrange). Suppose f,g∈C1 and f has a local extremum at a subject to the constraint g(a)=c. If ∇g(a)=0, then there exists λ∈R such that: ∇f(a)=λ,∇g(a)

Proof sketch. Since ∇g(a)=0, the constraint surface g=c is a smooth (n−1)-manifold near a. Any tangent vector v to this surface satisfies ∇g(a)⋅v=0.

Since a is a constrained extremum of f, the directional derivative of f along any direction tangent to the constraint must vanish: ∇f(a)⋅v=0 for all such v.

Therefore ∇f(a) is perpendicular to the entire tangent space of the constraint surface — meaning ∇f(a) is parallel to the normal ∇g(a), i.e., ∇f(a)=λ∇g(a). □

5.3 The Lagrange System

To find constrained extrema of f subject to g=c, solve the system: {∇f=λ,∇g g(x)=c​

In two variables (x,y), this is three equations in three unknowns (x,y,λ): fx​=λgx​,fy​=λgy​,g(x,y)=c

5.4 Worked Example

Find the maximum and minimum values of f(x,y)=x2+y2 on the ellipse 4x2​+y2=1.

Wait — this is just distance squared from the origin on an ellipse, so we want the closest and farthest points. Let g(x,y)=4x2​+y2.

Lagrange system: fx​=2x=λ⋅2x​,fy​=2y=λ⋅2y,4x2​+y2=1

Case 1: y=0. From the second equation: 2=2λ⟹λ=1. Substituting: 2x=2x​⟹23x​=0⟹x=0. Constraint: y2=1⟹y=±1. Points: (0,±1), f=1.

Case 2: y=0. Constraint: 4x2​=1⟹x=±2. From first eq: 2x=2λx​⟹λ=4. Points: (±2,0), f=4.

Conclusion: Maximum value 4 at (±2,0); minimum value 1 at (0,±1). ✓ (These are the semi-axes of the ellipse.)

7. The Hessian — General Rn Version

In Rn, the Hessian at a critical point a is the n×n symmetric matrix: [Hf​(a)]ij​=∂xi​∂xj​∂2f​(a)

The classification is by definiteness of Hf​(a):

Hessian HAll eigenvaluesConclusion
Positive definite>0Local minimum
Negative definite<0Local maximum
IndefiniteMixed signsSaddle point
Positive/Negative semidefinite≥0 or ≤0Inconclusive

The determinant test in R2 is just the 2×2 case of checking definiteness via eigenvalue signs (D=λ1​λ2​>0 means same sign; fxx​>0 means both positive).

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

8.1 The Entire Goal of Training is Minimization

Every supervised learning model minimizes a loss function L(θ) over parameters θ∈Rn (where n can be 109 or more). The theory of min/max is the mathematical foundation.

Critical points of L: ∇θ​L=0. These are where gradient descent stalls. Understanding whether a critical point is a minimum, maximum, or saddle is the central problem of deep learning optimization theory.

8.2 The Loss Landscape — What Are We Minimizing Into?

The loss surface L(θ) of a deep neural network is a hypersurface in billions of dimensions. Its landscape looks nothing like the neat single-variable pictures:

  • Local minima are rare. In high dimensions, a local minimum requires all eigenvalues of the Hessian to be positive — exponentially unlikely if eigenvalues are random.
  • Saddle points dominate. Most critical points have Hessians with some positive and some negative eigenvalues. The network gets stuck near saddles, not local minima.
  • Global structure: Surprisingly, for overparameterized networks (more parameters than data), many local minima have similar loss values — the landscape has many “flat valleys” at the bottom.

Empirical insight (Dauphin et al., 2014): The difficulty in training deep networks is escaping saddle points, not getting stuck in bad local minima.

8.3 Second-Order Methods — Using the Hessian

Gradient descent uses only ∇L (first-order). Newton’s method uses HL​ (second-order): θt+1​=θt​−η,[HL​(θt​)]−1∇L(θt​)

At a local minimum, HL​ is positive definite (second derivative test!), so H−1 scales the gradient step to take into account the local curvature — taking large steps in flat directions, small steps in sharp directions. This is exactly the second derivative test applied operationally.

Why not always use Newton’s method? Inverting HL​∈Rn×n costs O(n3) — infeasible for n=109. This motivates quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, L-BFGS) and natural gradient methods that approximate H−1.

8.4 Convexity — When Local = Global

Definition. f is convex if for all x,y and t∈[0,1]: f(tx+(1−t)y)≤tf(x)+(1−t)f(y)

Theorem. If f is convex and ∇f(a)=0, then a is a global minimum.

This is the key property. For convex loss functions (logistic regression, SVMs, linear regression), every critical point is a global minimum — the optimization is “safe.” For non-convex losses (deep networks), there is no such guarantee.

The Hessian connection: f is convex ⟺ Hf​ is positive semidefinite everywhere (⟺ all eigenvalues ≥0). This is the global version of the local minimum condition from the second derivative test.

8.5 Lagrange Multipliers in ML — Constrained Learning

Lagrange multipliers appear explicitly in:

Support Vector Machines (SVM). Maximize the margin subject to classification constraints. The Lagrangian is: L(w,b,α)=21​∣w∣2−∑i​αi​[yi​(w⋅xi​+b)−1] The KKT conditions (generalized Lagrange conditions for inequalities) completely characterize the optimal w.

Regularization as a Lagrange problem. Minimize L(θ) subject to ∣θ∣2≤C is equivalent (by Lagrange) to minimizing L(θ)+λ∣θ∣2 (ridge regression / L2 regularization). The multiplier λ is the regularization coefficient.

Variational autoencoders (VAE). The ELBO objective is a constrained optimization (maximize likelihood subject to KL divergence constraint), handled via Lagrange-style relaxation.

9. The Procedure — A Decision Tree

Given f on domain D, find extrema
│
├─ Is D closed and bounded?
│   ├─ YES → Extreme Value Theorem guarantees extrema exist
│   │         → Find interior critical points (∇f = 0)
│   │         → Analyze boundary (parameterize, or use Lagrange)
│   │         → Compare all values
│   │
│   └─ NO  → Only local extrema may exist
│             → Find critical points (∇f = 0)
│             → Apply Second Derivative Test (Hessian)
│
└─ Is there a constraint g(x) = c?
    └─ YES → Lagrange: solve ∇f = λ∇g, g = c
             → Evaluate f at solutions
             → Compare values (no 2nd derivative test needed
               if only finitely many solutions and domain compact)

10. A Subtle Point — When the Test Fails (D=0)

When D(a)=0, the Hessian is singular (one eigenvalue is zero). The quadratic approximation 21​hTHh is neither positive nor negative definite, and higher-order terms determine the behavior.

Example. f(x,y)=x4+y4. At (0,0): ∇f=0, H=0, D=0. Test is inconclusive. But clearly f≥0 with equality only at origin, so (0,0) is a global minimum.

Example. f(x,y)=x3. At (0,0): ∇f=0, D=0. But f takes both signs near the origin, so (0,0) is a saddle-like point.

When D=0: compute f along various paths through a directly.
