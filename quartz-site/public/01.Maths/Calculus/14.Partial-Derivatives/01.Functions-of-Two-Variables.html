“A function of one variable is a curve. A function of two variables is a landscape — it has hills, valleys, ridges, and saddle points. Learning to read that landscape is the entire project of multivariable calculus.”

0. Motivation

In single-variable calculus, we study f:R→R. But the real world is rarely one-dimensional:

  • Temperature in a room depends on position (x,y) on the floor.
  • The pressure of a gas depends on volume V and temperature T.
  • The loss of a neural network depends on millions of parameters (θ1​,θ2​,…,θp​).

The step from one variable to two is not merely an added variable — it is a qualitative leap in richness. New phenomena appear: partial derivatives, mixed partials, level curves, saddle points, constrained extrema. Understanding the two-variable case deeply is the prerequisite for everything that follows.

1. Definition

Formal Definition

A function of two variables is a rule f that assigns to each ordered pair (x,y) in a set D⊆R2 a unique real number z=f(x,y)∈R:

f:D⊆R2→R,(x,y)↦f(x,y)

  • D is the domain of f — the set of valid inputs.
  • The range is f(x,y):(x,y)∈D⊆R.
  • z=f(x,y) is the dependent variable; x,y are independent variables.

Natural Domain

Unless stated otherwise, the domain of f is the largest subset of R2 for which the expression f(x,y) makes sense (real-valued, defined). This requires excluding:

  • Points where denominators vanish.
  • Points where expressions under even roots are negative.
  • Points where logarithms receive non-positive arguments.

Example: f(x,y)=1−x2−y2y−x2​​

  • Need y−x2≥0: above or on the parabola y=x2.
  • Need 1−x2−y2=0: outside the unit circle.

Domain: (x,y):y≥x2∖(x,y):x2+y2=1.

2. The Graph

Definition

The graph of f:D→R is the set of all points (x,y,z)∈R3 such that z=f(x,y) and (x,y)∈D:

graph(f)=(x,y,f(x,y)):(x,y)∈D⊂R3

Geometrically: a surface in R3 lying above (or below) the xy-plane.

Visualization

Think of the xy-plane as a map. The value f(x,y) is the elevation at location (x,y). The graph is the terrain — the topographic surface.

3. Level Curves

Definition

The level curve (or contour) of f at height c∈R is the set:

Lc​=(x,y)∈D:f(x,y)=c

It is the horizontal cross-section of the graph at height z=c, projected down to the xy-plane.

Crucially: A point (x,y) lies on the level curve Lc​ if and only if the surface passes through height c directly above it.

The Contour Map

A contour map (contour plot) is the collection of level curves Lc​ for various values of c, drawn in the xy-plane. Every topographic map you have ever read is a contour map.

Reading a contour map:

  • Closely spaced contours: Steep terrain (rapid change in f).
  • Widely spaced contours: Gentle terrain (slow change in f).
  • Closed contours: Peaks, valleys, or saddle points.
  • Contours never cross: If Lc​ and Lc′​ crossed at a point (x0​,y0​), then f(x0​,y0​)=c and f(x0​,y0​)=c′ simultaneously — impossible for c=c′.

Examples

f(x,y)=x2+y2: Level curves c=x2+y2 are circles of radius c​ centered at the origin. The surface is a paraboloid. Contours are evenly spaced circles — but the paraboloid gets steeper as r increases, so equal spacing in c gives circles that pack closer together.

f(x,y)=x2−y2: Level curves c=x2−y2 are hyperbolas (for c=0) and the two lines y=±x (for c=0). This is the saddle surface — its contour map looks like a topographic pass between two mountains.

f(x,y)=xy: Level curves xy=c are rectangular hyperbolas with asymptotes along the axes.

4. Limits and Continuity

The Core Difficulty

In R, approaching a point a means coming from the left or right — only two paths. In R2, approaching (a,b) means coming from infinitely many directions and along infinitely many curved paths. For a limit to exist, the function must approach the same value along every possible path.

Definition

lim(x,y)→(a,b)​f(x,y)=L

means: for every ε>0, there exists δ>0 such that:

0<(x−a)2+(y−b)2​<δ⟹∣f(x,y)−L∣<ε

The Two-Path Test (Nonexistence)

To show a limit does not exist: find two paths to (a,b) along which f approaches different values.

Example: Show (x,y)→(0,0)lim​x2+y2x2−y2​ does not exist.

  • Along y=0: f(x,0)=x2/x2=1→1.
  • Along x=0: f(0,y)=−y2/y2=−1→−1.

Since 1=−1, the limit does not exist. □

Example (subtler): Show (x,y)→(0,0)lim​x2+y4xy2​ does not exist.

  • Along y=0: f→0.
  • Along x=y2: f=y4+y4y2⋅y2​=21​→21​.

Limit does not exist. Note: along any straight line y=mx, the limit is 0 — only a parabolic path reveals the failure. This shows why the two-path test must consider curves, not just lines.

Continuity

f is continuous at (a,b) if:

  1. f(a,b) is defined.
  2. (x,y)→(a,b)lim​f(x,y) exists.
  3. (x,y)→(a,b)lim​f(x,y)=f(a,b).

Theorem: Polynomials in x,y are continuous on all of R2. Rational functions are continuous on their domains. Compositions of continuous functions are continuous.

Consequence: For nice functions (polynomials, rationals away from singularities, exponentials, trig), you can evaluate limits by direct substitution. The challenge is only at boundary points of the domain or suspected discontinuities.

5. Partial Derivatives (First Pass)

Full treatment in the next note. Here, the idea.

The partial derivative ∂x∂f​(a,b) is the derivative of f in the x-direction, holding y=b fixed:

∂x∂f​(a,b)=limh→0​hf(a+h,b)−f(a,b)​

Geometrically: it is the slope of the curve obtained by slicing the surface z=f(x,y) with the vertical plane y=b.

Similarly for ∂y∂f​: hold x=a fixed, differentiate in y.

Rule: To compute ∂f/∂x, treat y as a constant and differentiate in x using all single-variable rules.

6. Worked Examples

Example 1: Domain

Find the domain of f(x,y)=ln(x+y−1).

Need x+y−1>0, i.e., x+y>1.

Domain: the open half-plane above the line x+y=1. The boundary line is excluded (log of zero is undefined).

Example 2: Level Curves of a Gaussian

Let f(x,y)=e−(x2+y2).

Level curve at height c (where 0<c≤1): e−(x2+y2)=c⟹x2+y2=−lnc

For 0<c<1: −lnc>0, so these are circles of radius −lnc​. As c→1−: radius →0 (peak at origin). As c→0+: radius →∞ (function decays to zero far from origin).

The contour map is a family of concentric circles — and the circles get farther apart as c decreases (the Gaussian decays rapidly near origin, then flattens out).

Example 3: A Subtle Limit

Show (x,y)→(0,0)lim​x2+y2x2y​=0.

Use the Squeeze Theorem. Observe: ​x2+y2x2y​​≤x2+y2x2∣y∣​≤∣y∣

since x2≤x2+y2. As (x,y)→(0,0), ∣y∣→0. By squeeze, the limit is 0.

Note: To confirm, convert to polar: x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ: r2r2cos2θ⋅rsinθ​=rcos2θsinθ→0 as r→0

since ∣cos2θsinθ∣≤1 for all θ. The polar method works when the bound is independent of θ.

Example 4: Discontinuity

Let f(x,y)={x2+y22xy​​(x,y)=(0,0) 0​(x,y)=(0,0)​

Is f continuous at (0,0)?

Along y=mx: f(x,mx)=x2(1+m2)2mx2​=1+m22m​, which depends on m.

For m=1: limit is 1. For m=0: limit is 0. The limit does not exist, so f is not continuous at (0,0), regardless of how we define f(0,0).

The “Slicing” Interpretation

The surface z=f(x,y) is simultaneously:

  • The set of all level curves stacked at their respective heights.
  • The set of all “traces” — curves obtained by fixing x or y to a constant.
Fix y = b:  curve z = f(x, b) in the plane y = b  →  slope = ∂f/∂x
Fix x = a:  curve z = f(a, y) in the plane x = a  →  slope = ∂f/∂y

8. Functions of Three or More Variables

The definitions extend naturally:

f:D⊆Rn→R,(x1​,…,xn​)↦f(x1​,…,xn​)

We can no longer visualize the graph (it lives in Rn+1). Instead, we study level sets:

Lc​=(x1​,…,xn​):f(x1​,…,xn​)=c

which are (n−1)-dimensional hypersurfaces in Rn.

For n=3: f(x,y,z)=c defines a level surface in R3 — e.g., x2+y2+z2=r2 (sphere), x2/a2+y2/b2+z2/c2=1 (ellipsoid).

All the theory — limits, continuity, partial derivatives, gradients — extends verbatim to n variables. The geometry is harder to visualize but the algebra is identical.

9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

9.1 The Loss Function Is a Function of Millions of Variables

The most important function in all of machine learning is the loss function:

L:Rp→R,θ↦L(θ)

where p is the number of parameters (GPT-4: p∼1012). This is exactly a function of p variables — the direct generalization of f(x,y) to p dimensions.

Everything we study for f(x,y) has a direct ML analog:

f(x,y) conceptML analog
Domain D⊂R2Parameter space Rp
Graph z=f(x,y)Loss landscape (unvisualizable for p≫2)
Level curve f(x,y)=cLoss contour L(θ)=c — iso-loss surface in parameter space
ContinuitySmoothness of L (required for gradient methods to work)
Partial derivative ∂f/∂xGradient component ∂L/∂θi​ — used in backpropagation

9.2 Level Curves as Decision Boundaries

For a binary classifier f:R2→R, the decision boundary is the level curve f(x,y)=0.5 (or f=0 for logistic regression). The classifier outputs:

  • Class 1 if f(x,y)>0.5
  • Class 0 if f(x,y)<0.5

A linear classifier produces straight-line decision boundaries (level curves of a linear f). A neural network can produce arbitrarily complex level curves — this is the Universal Approximation Theorem in geometric form: a neural network can approximate any continuous function f:R2→R, hence any level curve as a decision boundary.

9.3 Continuity and the Vanishing Gradient Problem

The continuity of the loss function near a region of parameter space is directly connected to gradient flow. At a discontinuity or near a point where the gradient is zero but the function is not at a minimum (a flat region or plateau), gradient descent stalls — the optimization analogue of a function with zero partial derivatives everywhere in a region.

The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation ReLU(x)=max(0,x) makes networks that are continuous but not differentiable at x=0. The composed function L(θ) for a ReLU network is a piecewise linear function of θ — continuous everywhere, differentiable almost everywhere.

9.4 Gaussian Functions and Radial Basis Networks

The function f(x,y)=e−(x2+y2)/2σ2 (Example 2 above) is the 2D Gaussian. Its level curves are concentric circles. In ML:

  • Kernel methods (SVMs with RBF kernel): The kernel K(x,x′)=e−∣x−x′∣2/2σ2 measures similarity as a Gaussian of distance.
  • Radial Basis Function (RBF) networks: Hidden units compute ϕ(x)=e−∣x−ci​∣2/2σi2​ — exactly the two-variable Gaussian, with centers ci​.
  • Gaussian Processes: The covariance function is a Gaussian of the input distance — level curves of this covariance are ellipses in input space.

9.5 Latent Space and Feature Maps

In a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) or diffusion model, data is mapped to a 2D (or low-dimensional) latent space. The decoder then defines a function f:R2→Rd (going from 2 latent variables to a high-dimensional output — the reverse direction of what we study here, but the domain is R2).

The latent space interpolation — walking along a straight line or curve in the (z1​,z2​) plane — is exactly choosing a path in the domain D⊆R2 of the decoder function. The continuity of f ensures nearby latent points decode to nearby outputs.

9.6 Two-Variable Functions as Neural Network Layers

A single neuron with two inputs computes:

z=σ(w1​x+w2​y+b)

This is a function f(x,y)=σ(w1​x+w2​y+b). Its level curves are:

w1​x+w2​y+b=σ−1(c)=const

which are straight lines (linear decision boundaries). Stacking neurons allows the level curves of the composed function to become arbitrarily curved — the geometric meaning of expressiveness.

9.7 Domain and Data Manifold

In practice, input data does not fill all of Rn. Natural images, for instance, occupy a low-dimensional manifold M⊂Rn (the manifold hypothesis). The effective domain of the learned function f is this manifold — points off the manifold are out-of-distribution. This is the geometric basis for why neural networks hallucinate and fail on adversarial examples: the function is evaluated outside its meaningful domain D.

10. Summary

A function of two variables f:D⊆R2→R is a surface in R3, studied through:

Domain: Largest set where f(x,y) makes sense — exclude division by zero, square roots of negatives, logs of non-positives.

Graph: The surface z=f(x,y) in R3 — the geometric object we analyze.

Level curves: f(x,y)=c — horizontal slices of the graph, contour lines on a map. They never cross; dense packing means steep terrain.

Limits: Must agree along every path to (a,b), not just along lines. Two-path test proves nonexistence; polar coordinates and squeeze theorem prove existence.

Continuity: Defined exactly as in one variable, but the limit condition is harder to verify due to the infinity of approach paths.

The entire theory extends to n variables. In machine learning, the loss landscape L(θ) is a function of millions of variables — but every concept introduced here (domain, level sets, continuity, partial derivatives) applies directly, at scale.
