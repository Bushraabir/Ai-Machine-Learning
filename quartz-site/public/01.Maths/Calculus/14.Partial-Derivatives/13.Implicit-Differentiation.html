13.Implicit Differentiation

Apr 23, 202616 min read

“Explicit functions are a convenience. Most relationships in nature are implicit — a constraint that variables must satisfy. Implicit differentiation is how we extract rates of change from constraints, without ever solving for one variable in terms of the others.”

§1. Motivation — When You Cannot Solve Explicitly

Consider the curve x3+y3=6xy (the folium of Descartes). Can you solve for y in terms of x? Technically yes — via the cubic formula — but the result is a nightmare. Yet we can ask: what is the slope of this curve at the point (3,3)?

Or consider exy+sin(x+y)=2. Solving for y is impossible in closed form. But the curve exists, it has slopes at each point, and we can compute them.

Implicit differentiation extracts dxdy​ (or ∂x∂z​) directly from the defining equation F(x,y)=0, without isolating the dependent variable. The theoretical guarantee that such a function even exists is the Implicit Function Theorem.

§2. The Core Idea — Differentiate the Constraint

Suppose y is implicitly defined as a function of x by F(x,y)=0. This means there exists (locally) a function y=g(x) such that:

F(x,,g(x))=0for all x near a

Differentiate both sides with respect to x. Left side: chain rule. Right side: zero.

dxd​F(x,g(x))=Fx​(x,y)⋅1+Fy​(x,y)⋅dxdy​=0

Solve:

dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​​

That is the whole algorithm. The rest of this note is making it rigorous, generalizing it, and understanding when it works.

§3. The Implicit Function Theorem — The Guarantee

Before computing derivatives, we must ask: does F(x,y)=0 actually define y as a function of x near a given point?

Theorem (Implicit Function Theorem, 2D). Let F:R2→R be C1 in a neighborhood of (a,b), with F(a,b)=0. If:

Fy​(a,b)=0

then there exists an open interval I containing a and a unique C1 function y=g(x) on I such that:

  • g(a)=b
  • F(x,g(x))=0 for all x∈I
  • g′(x)=−Fy​(x,g(x))Fx​(x,g(x))​

What the theorem says geometrically. Near (a,b), the level curve F=0 looks like the graph of a function. The condition Fy​=0 ensures the curve is not “vertical” there — it has a well-defined slope.

What happens when Fy​=0? The theorem fails. The curve may have a vertical tangent, a self-intersection, a cusp, or a fold. These are exactly the singular points where implicit differentiation breaks down.

Example. F(x,y)=x2+y2−1 (unit circle). Fy​=2y. The theorem fails at y=0, i.e., at (1,0) and (−1,0) — exactly where the circle has vertical tangents.

§4. Algorithm — One Equation, Two Variables

Given F(x,y)=0, to find dxdy​:

Method 1 — Direct chain rule: Differentiate F(x,y)=0 with respect to x, treating y as a function of x. Apply the chain rule to every term involving y. Collect dxdy​ terms on one side and solve.

Method 2 — Differential: Take dF=0, i.e., Fx​,dx+Fy​,dy=0. Divide by dx: dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​.

Both methods are equivalent. Method 2 is faster for complex expressions.

Example 1 — Folium of Descartes

x3+y3=6xy. Find dxdy​.

Set F=x3+y3−6xy. Then:

Fx​=3x2−6y,Fy​=3y2−6x

dxdy​=−3y2−6x3x2−6y​=y2−2x2y−x2​

At (3,3): dxdy​=9−66−9​=3−3​=−1. The tangent line at (3,3) is y−3=−(x−3), i.e., x+y=6.

Example 2 — Transcendental Equation

exy+sin(x+y)=2. Find dxdy​ at (0,0).

F=exy+sin(x+y)−2.

Fx​=yexy+cos(x+y),Fy​=xexy+cos(x+y)

At (0,0): Fx​=0+cos(0)=1, Fy​=0+cos(0)=1.

dxdy​​(0,0)​=−11​=−1

Example 3 — Second Derivative

x2+y2=r2 (circle). Find dx2d2y​.

First derivative: 2x+2ydxdy​=0⟹dxdy​=−yx​.

Second derivative. Differentiate dxdy​=−yx​ with respect to x:

dx2d2y​=−y2y⋅1−x⋅dxdy​​=−y2y−x(−yx​)​=−y2y+yx2​​=−y3y2+x2​=−y3r2​

Note: substitute dxdy​ before simplifying — always.

§5. Three Variables — Implicit Surface

Now F(x,y,z)=0 defines a surface. Suppose z=z(x,y) implicitly.

Differentiate F(x,y,z(x,y))=0 with respect to x (holding y constant):

Fx​+Fz​∂x∂z​=0⟹∂x∂z​=−Fz​Fx​​

Differentiate with respect to y:

Fy​+Fz​∂y∂z​=0⟹∂y∂z​=−Fz​Fy​​

Condition for validity: Fz​=0 at the point (Implicit Function Theorem in 3D).

The gradient of z=g(x,y) is:

∇g=(−Fz​Fx​​,,−Fz​Fy​​)

and the tangent plane to the surface F=0 at (a,b,c) is:

Fx​(a,b,c)(x−a)+Fy​(a,b,c)(y−b)+Fz​(a,b,c)(z−c)=0

Example — Ellipsoid

4x2​+9y2​+z2=1. Find ∂x∂z​ and ∂y∂z​.

F=4x2​+9y2​+z2−1.

Fx​=2x​,Fy​=92y​,Fz​=2z

∂x∂z​=−2zx/2​=−4zx​,∂y∂z​=−2z2y/9​=−9zy​

At (2,0,0): Fz​=0 — the theorem fails. Indeed, (2,0,0) is a point with a vertical tangent plane (z is not differentiable there).

§6. The General Case — n Variables, m Constraints

Let F:Rn+m→Rm be C1 near (a,b)∈Rn×Rm with F(a,b)=0.

Write the variables as (x,y)∈Rn×Rm. The Jacobian of F with respect to y:

Jy​F=(∂yj​∂Fi​​)i,j=1m​

Theorem (Implicit Function Theorem, General). If det(Jy​F(a,b))=0, then there exists a unique C1 function y=g(x) near a with g(a)=b and F(x,g(x))=0. Moreover:

Jx​g=−(Jy​F)−1⋅Jx​F

This is the matrix version of dxdy​=−Fx​/Fy​.

Proof sketch. Differentiate F(x,g(x))=0 with respect to x via the chain rule:

Jx​F+Jy​F⋅Jx​g=0

Since Jy​F is invertible (by hypothesis), solve: Jx​g=−(Jy​F)−1Jx​F. ■

The existence of g (not just its derivative) requires a deeper argument — the contraction mapping theorem or inverse function theorem.

§7. Implicit Differentiation via Differentials — The Slickest Method

For any equation F(x,y)=c, take the total differential:

dF=Fx​,dx+Fy​,dy=0

Then dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​ immediately, by treating dF=0 as a linear constraint on dx and dy.

For F(x,y,z)=c:

Fx​,dx+Fy​,dy+Fz​,dz=0

  • Hold y fixed (dy=0): ∂x∂z​=−Fz​Fx​​
  • Hold x fixed (dx=0): ∂y∂z​=−Fz​Fy​​

No chain rule bookkeeping. Just algebra on differentials.

The Cyclic Identity (a Beautiful Consequence)

From F(x,y,z)=0, we have three implicit partial derivatives:

∂x∂z​​y​=−Fz​Fx​​,∂y∂x​​z​=−Fx​Fy​​,∂z∂y​​x​=−Fy​Fz​​

Multiply all three:

∂x∂z​​y​⋅∂y∂x​​z​⋅∂z∂y​​x​=(−Fz​Fx​​)(−Fx​Fy​​)(−Fy​Fz​​)=−1

∂x∂z​​y​⋅∂y∂x​​z​⋅∂z∂y​​x​=−1​

This is the cyclic identity (or triple product rule, or Euler’s chain rule). It is not the chain rule — it produces −1, not +1. It appears everywhere in thermodynamics: (∂P/∂V)T​(∂V/∂T)P​(∂T/∂P)V​=−1 is the same identity for an ideal gas.

Warning. This is not ∂x∂z​⋅∂y∂x​⋅∂z∂y​ in the chain rule sense. The subscripts matter — each partial holds a different variable fixed.

§8. Second-Order Implicit Differentiation

Given F(x,y)=0, compute dx2d2y​.

Step 1. Compute y′=−Fy​Fx​​.

Step 2. Differentiate y′=−Fy​(x,y)Fx​(x,y)​ with respect to x, using the quotient rule and chain rule (treating y=y(x)):

y′′=−Fy2​(Fxx​+Fxy​y′)Fy​−Fx​(Fyx​+Fyy​y′)​

Substitute y′=−Fx​/Fy​ and simplify:

y′′=−Fy3​Fxx​Fy2​−2Fxy​Fx​Fy​+Fyy​Fx2​​

This is the standard formula. In practice, substitute y′ explicitly rather than memorizing this — the derivation is more reliable.

Example — Implicitly Defined Curve

x2+xy+y2=3. Find y′′ at (1,1).

Differential: 2x,dx+y,dx+x,dy+2y,dy=0 ⇒(2x+y),dx+(x+2y),dy=0 y′=−x+2y2x+y​

At (1,1): y′=−33​=−1.

Second derivative. Differentiate y′(x+2y)=−(2x+y):

(x+2y)y′′+(1+2y′)y′=−(2+y′)

At (1,1), y′=−1: (3)y′′+(1−2)(−1)=−(2−1) 3y′′+1=−1⟹y′′=−32​

§9. Logarithmic Differentiation — Implicit Differentiation’s Cousin

For y=f(x)g(x) or products/quotients of many factors, take the log of both sides first, then differentiate implicitly.

y=f(x)g(x). Take lny=g(x)lnf(x).

Differentiate both sides with respect to x:

yy′​=g′(x)lnf(x)+g(x)f(x)f′(x)​

y′=y[g′lnf+fgf′​]=f(x)g(x)[g′(x)lnf(x)+f(x)g(x)f′(x)​]

Example. y=xsinx.

lny=sinx⋅lnx yy′​=cosxlnx+xsinx​ y′=xsinx(cosxlnx+xsinx​)

§10. Worked Examples — Gallery

Example A — Tangent Line to an Implicit Curve

Find the tangent line to y2=x3(2−x) (lemniscate-type) at (1,1).

F=y2−x3(2−x)=y2−2x3+x4.

Fx​=−6x2+4x3,Fy​=2y

At (1,1): Fx​=−6+4=−2, Fy​=2.

dxdy​=−2−2​=1

Tangent line: y−1=1(x−1), i.e., y=x.

Example B — Horizontal and Vertical Tangents

x2−xy+y2=3.

Fx​=2x−y,Fy​=−x+2y,y′=−−x+2y2x−y​=x−2y2x−y​

Horizontal tangents (y′=0): 2x−y=0⇒y=2x. Substitute into the curve: x2−x(2x)+(2x)2=3⇒3x2=3⇒x=±1 Points: (1,2) and (−1,−2).

Vertical tangents (y′=∞, i.e., Fy​=0): x−2y=0⇒x=2y. Substitute: (2y)2−(2y)y+y2=3⇒3y2=3⇒y=±1 Points: (2,1) and (−2,−1).

Example C — Partial Derivatives from an Implicit Surface

The ideal gas law PV=nRT defines any variable as a function of the others. Take F(P,V,T)=PV−nRT=0.

∂T∂V​​P​=−FV​FT​​=−P−nR​=PnR​

∂V∂P​​T​=−FP​FV​​=−VP​

∂P∂T​​V​=−FT​FP​​=−−nRV​=nRV​

Check the cyclic identity:

∂T∂V​​P​⋅∂P∂T​​V​⋅∂V∂P​​T​=PnR​⋅nRV​⋅(−VP​)=−1✓

§11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

11.1 — Implicit Layers in Deep Learning

The standard neural network layer is explicit: given input x, compute output y=f(x;θ) in one forward pass.

An implicit layer is defined by a constraint:

F(y,x;θ)=0

The output y is the solution to this equation. Examples:

  • Fixed-point layers: y=f(y,x) (Deep Equilibrium Models)
  • Optimization layers: y=argminz​L(z,x;θ), which satisfies ∇z​L(y,x;θ)=0

To train these by gradient descent, we need ∂θ∂y​ and ∂x∂y​ — but y has no explicit formula. Enter implicit differentiation.

The Implicit Function Theorem applied. Differentiate F(y(θ),x,θ)=0 with respect to θ:

square matrix∂y∂F​​​∂θ∂y​+∂θ∂F​=0

∂θ∂y​=−(∂y∂F​)−1∂θ∂F​

This is the general IFT formula. We never need to unroll the iterative solver that finds y — we only need the Jacobian ∂y∂F​ at the solution. This is called implicit differentiation through fixed points.

11.2 — Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQ)

A DEQ (Bai et al., 2019) replaces all layers with:

z⋆=fθ​(z⋆,x)

i.e., z⋆ is a fixed point. Forward pass: iterate f until convergence (Newton’s method, Anderson acceleration). Backward pass: never unroll the iterations. Instead, at convergence:

F(z⋆,x;θ)=z⋆−fθ​(z⋆,x)=0

Implicit differentiation gives:

∂θ∂z⋆​=−(I−∂z∂fθ​​​z⋆​)−1∂θ∂fθ​​​z⋆​

The matrix (I−Jf​) must be invertible — this is the IFT condition. At a stable fixed point (spectral radius of Jf​<1), the Neumann series guarantees invertibility:

(I−Jf​)−1=∑k=0∞​Jfk​

Convergence of the fixed-point iteration and invertibility of (I−Jf​) are two sides of the same contraction condition.

Memory advantage. An explicit L-layer network stores O(L) activations for backprop. A DEQ stores only the fixed point z⋆ — O(1) memory, regardless of how many iterations the forward pass took.

11.3 — Differentiable Optimization Layers (OptNet, cvxpylayers)

An optimization layer solves:

y⋆(x)=argminy​f(y;x)s.t. g(y;x)≤0

At the optimum, the KKT conditions (necessary optimality conditions) give a system:

F(y⋆,λ⋆,x)=0

where λ are the dual variables. Implicit differentiation on F=0:

∂x∂(y⋆,λ⋆)​=−(∂(y,λ)∂F​)−1∂x∂F​

This is the foundation of differentiable convex optimization (Amos & Kolter, 2017). The optimization layer can be embedded anywhere in a neural network, and gradients flow through it via the IFT, not through the solver iterations.

11.4 — Neural ODEs

A Neural ODE (Chen et al., 2018) defines the hidden state dynamics by:

dtdh​=fθ​(h(t),t)

with h(t0​)=x. The output h(T) is the solution of this ODE at time T. The adjoint method for computing ∂θ∂L​ is implicit differentiation applied to the ODE:

Define the adjoint a(t)=∂h(t)∂L​. It satisfies the adjoint ODE:

dtda​=−a(t)⊤∂h∂fθ​​

The gradient with respect to parameters:

∂θ∂L​=−∫t0​T​a(t)⊤∂θ∂fθ​​,dt

No backpropagation through the ODE solver. Memory is O(1) in depth. The adjoint ODE is derived by implicit differentiation of the optimality conditions — the IFT, applied continuously.

11.5 — The IFT and Meta-Learning

MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning, Finn et al., 2017) seeks parameters θ such that one gradient step on a new task gives good performance. The inner-loop update:

ϕ(θ)=θ−α∇θ​Ltask​(θ)

The meta-objective requires ∂θ∂ϕ​. For one gradient step this is explicit. For many steps, computing ∂θ∂ϕ​ via unrolling is expensive. iMAML (Rajeswaran et al., 2019) instead defines ϕ as the solution to:

ϕ⋆(θ)=argminϕ​[Ltask​(ϕ)+2λ​∣ϕ−θ∣2]

which satisfies ∇ϕ​Ltask​(ϕ⋆)+λ(ϕ⋆−θ)=0. This is F(ϕ⋆,θ)=0. Implicit differentiation:

∂θ∂ϕ⋆​=(∇ϕ2​Ltask​+λI)−1⋅λI

The Hessian inverse appears — but it can be approximated by conjugate gradient, making iMAML practical. The IFT removes the need to store the full inner-loop computation graph.

§13. Theorems to Know Cold

Theorem 1 (Implicit Function Theorem, 2D). If F∈C1 near (a,b), F(a,b)=0, Fy​(a,b)=0: then y=g(x) exists locally, is C1, and g′=−Fx​/Fy​.

Theorem 2 (IFT, nD). If F∈C1 near (a,b), F(a,b)=0, det(Jy​F)=0: then y=g(x) exists locally, is C1, and Jg=−(Jy​F)−1Jx​F.

Theorem 3 (Cyclic Identity). If F(x,y,z)=0 defines each variable implicitly in terms of the other two: ∂y∂x​​z​⋅∂z∂y​​x​⋅∂x∂z​​y​=−1

Theorem 4 (Inverse Function Theorem — a Corollary of IFT). If f:Rn→Rn is C1 near a and det(Jf​(a))=0, then f is locally invertible near a, and Jf−1​(f(a))=(Jf​(a))−1.

Proof: Apply IFT to F(y,x)=f(y)−x=0.

§14. Things That Will Trip You Up

Fy​=0 kills implicit differentiation at that point. The tangent may be vertical, or the curve may have a singularity. Always check.

The notation ∂x∂z​ is ambiguous without context. Is y being held fixed? Is z defined implicitly by one equation or two? Always state what is being held constant.

Second derivatives require substituting y′ before simplifying. Never leave y′ in the expression for y′′ — substitute the formula you found and simplify.

The cyclic identity gives −1, not +1. This surprises everyone the first time. It is not the chain rule.

Implicit differentiation gives local information only. The IFT guarantees a function y=g(x) only near the point where Fy​=0. The function may not extend globally, and there may be multiple branches.

In the ML context: implicit differentiation through a solver assumes the solver has converged. The formula ∂θ∂y​=−(Jy​F)−1Jθ​F is only valid at the exact solution F(y⋆,θ)=0. Approximate solutions give biased gradients.
