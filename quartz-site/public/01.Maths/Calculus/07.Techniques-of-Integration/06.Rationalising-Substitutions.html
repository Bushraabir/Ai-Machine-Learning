06.Rationalising Substitutions

06.Rationalising Substitutions

Apr 18, 202610 min read

“The art of integration is the art of recognising which disguise the integrand is wearing.” — a sentiment shared by every analyst who has stared at ∫1+x​dx​ for too long.

0. What Problem Are We Actually Solving?

An integrand is irrational when it contains an algebraic expression that cannot be written as a ratio of polynomials — typically because of a radical such as n,⋅,​ or a fractional power. Standard techniques (partial fractions, IBP) require rational functions. The strategy is therefore:

Introduce a new variable t that kills the radical, transforming the integral into a rational one, which we can then attack by partial fractions.

This is the entirety of the method. Everything else is bookkeeping.

1. Taxonomy of Rationalising Substitutions

1.1 Simple Root: nax+b​

Setting: The integrand involves nax+b​ for integers n≥2.

Substitution: Set t=nax+b​⟹x=atn−b​,dx=an,tn−1​,dt.

Why it works: After substitution every occurrence of nax+b​ becomes t, and dx introduces a polynomial in t. The resulting integrand is rational in t.

Example 1.1

Compute I=∫1+x​dx​.

Set t=x​, so x=t2, dx=2t,dt: I=∫1+t2t,dt​=2∫1+tt​,dt.

Write 1+tt​=1−1+t1​: I=2∫!(1−1+t1​)dt=2[t−ln∣1+t∣]+C.

Back-substitute t=x​: I=2x​−2ln(1+x​)+C.​

Example 1.2

Compute I=∫1+3x2​3x​​,dx.

Set t=3x​, so x=t3, dx=3t2,dt. Also 3x2​=t2: I=∫1+t2t​⋅3t2,dt=3∫1+t2t3​,dt.

Polynomial long division: 1+t2t3​=t−1+t2t​, so I=3∫!(t−1+t2t​)dt=3[2t2​−21​ln(1+t2)]+C.

Back-substitute: I=23​x2/3−23​ln!(1+x2/3)+C.​

1.2 Two Different Roots: m⋅​ and n⋅​

When two distinct root orders appear, choose tk where k=lcm(m,n). This single substitution simultaneously rationalises both radicals.

Substitution: tk=ax+b, where k=lcm(m,n).

Example 1.3

Compute I=∫x​+3x​dx​.

Here m=2, n=3, so k=lcm(2,3)=6. Set t6=x, dx=6t5,dt: x​=t3,3x​=t2. I=∫t3+t26t5,dt​=6∫t2(t+1)t5​,dt=6∫t+1t3​,dt.

Long division: t+1t3​=t2−t+1−t+11​, so I=6[3t3​−2t2​+t−ln∣t+1∣]+C.

Back-substitute t=x1/6: I=2x​−3x1/3+6x1/6−6ln!(1+x1/6)+C.​

1.3 Quadratic Under the Root: ax2+bx+c​

The substitution depends on the sign of a and the discriminant Δ=b2−4ac. Complete the square first: ax2+bx+c=a!(x+2ab​)!2+4a4ac−b2​.

After translation u=x+b/(2a), the radicand reduces to one of three forms. For each, a trigonometric or hyperbolic substitution rationalises the root (in the generalised sense of removing the square root):

Reduced formSubstitutionIdentity used
u2+p2u=ptanθ1+tan2θ=sec2θ
p2−u2u=psinθ1−sin2θ=cos2θ
u2−p2u=psecθsec2θ−1=tan2θ

Note. Trig substitutions are sometimes called rationalising because they convert quadratic​ into a trig polynomial — no longer an algebraic radical. (See Stewart §7.3 and MIT 18.02 notes.)

1.4 The Weierstrass Substitution (Universal for Trig Rationals)

Setting: Integrands of the form R(sinx,cosx) — rational in sinx and cosx.

Substitution: t=tan!2x​,x∈(−π,π).

Then: sinx=1+t22t​,cosx=1+t21−t2​,dx=1+t22,dt​.

Every occurrence of sinx and cosx becomes rational in t, and dx is rational in t.

Derivation (for the notebook)

From the half-angle identities: sinx=2sin2x​cos2x​ and cosx=cos22x​−sin22x​. Divide numerator and denominator by cos22x​: sinx=sec22x​2tan2x​​=1+t22t​,cosx=sec22x​1−tan22x​​=1+t21−t2​.

For dx: since t=tan(x/2), we have dt=21​sec2(x/2),dx, so dx=sec2(x/2)2,dt​=1+t22,dt​.

Example 1.4

Compute I=∫1+sinxdx​.

I=∫1+1+t22t​1​⋅1+t22,dt​=∫(1+t)22,dt​=−1+t2​+C=−1+tan(x/2)2​+C.

Example 1.5

Compute I=∫2+cosθdθ​.

I=∫2+1+t21−t2​1​⋅1+t22,dt​=∫2(1+t2)+(1−t2)2,dt​=∫3+t22,dt​.

=3​2​arctan!3​t​+C=3​2​arctan!(3​tan(θ/2)​)+C.

2. The Procedure — A Rigorous Framework

Given ∫f(x),dx involving a radical ng(x)​:

  1. Identify the simplest radical expression r(x) present.
  2. Set t=r(x), invert to get x=ϕ(t), compute dx=ϕ′(t),dt.
  3. Substitute everywhere: ∫f(ϕ(t)),ϕ′(t),dt. Verify the integrand is rational in t.
  4. Decompose via partial fractions if needed.
  5. Integrate term by term.
  6. Back-substitute t=r(x).

Theorem (Change of Variables, Rigorous Form). Let ϕ:[c,d]→[a,b] be continuously differentiable and bijective with ϕ(c)=a, ϕ(d)=b. If f is continuous on [a,b], then ∫ab​f(x),dx=∫cd​f(ϕ(t)),ϕ′(t),dt. (Stewart, §5.5; the indefinite version holds wherever f∘ϕ and ϕ′ are continuous.)

3. Visualisation

3.1 Geometric Picture for t=x​

The substitution t=x​ “unfolds” the curve y=f(x) over [0,R2] into the curve y=f(t2)⋅2t over [0,R]. The area is the same; the shape changes. The key insight is that the Jacobian dx=2t,dt compensates exactly for the change in domain.

x-domain:   0 ────────────────────── R²
                    (compressed near 0)
                        ↕ t = √x
t-domain:   0 ───────────── R
                    (stretched near 0, 2t factor)

The factor 2t vanishing at t=0 reflects the fact that the square root “compresses” x near 0 — the substitution stretches it back.

3.2 Weierstrass as Stereographic Projection

The substitution t=tan(x/2) has a beautiful geometric interpretation: it is the stereographic projection of the unit circle onto the real line, with the projection point at (−1,0).

         Unit Circle
              · (0,1)
             /|
            / |  sin x
           /  |
(-1,0)----/---+------·-------> t-axis
    proj  |   |cos x  
          |   +------ (cos x, sin x)
          · (0,-1)

A point (cosx,sinx) on the circle is connected to (−1,0), and the line meets the t-axis at t=tan(x/2). This is why t parametrises every rational combination of sinx and cosx — the rational map from a circle to a line via stereographic projection.

4. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

This is not merely a historical curiosity. Rationalising substitutions appear in modern machine learning in several guises.

4.1 Change of Variables in Normalising Flows

A normalising flow is a generative model that transforms a simple base distribution pZ​(z) (e.g., N(0,I)) into a complex target pX​(x) via an invertible, differentiable map f:z↦x=f(z).

The exact density is given by the change-of-variables formula: pX​(x)=pZ​!(f−1(x))⋅​detJf−1​(x)​, where Jf−1​ is the Jacobian of f−1. This is the multidimensional analogue of dx=ϕ′(t),dt.

A rational quadratic spline flow (used in state-of-the-art flows) literally constructs f piecewise as a ratio of quadratic polynomials — a direct descendant of rational function theory. The bijectivity and differentiability requirements are exactly the conditions in the Change of Variables Theorem.

The Jacobian computation (the “cost” of a flow layer) is precisely the ϕ′(t) term in our substitution formula.

4.2 Kernel Methods and Rational Approximations

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Gaussian Processes use kernel functions k(x,x′). A common kernel is the RBF (Radial Basis Function): k(x,x′)=exp!(−2σ2∣x−x′∣2​).

When approximating kernels via random Fourier features (Rahimi & Recht, 2007), one needs to compute integrals of the form ∫k^(ω),eiω⊤(x−x′),dω where k^ is the Fourier transform. For the Matérn kernel, k^ involves expressions like (1+∣ω∣2/ν)−(ν+d/2) — a rational function of ∣ω∣2, and integration proceeds by partial fractions in spherical coordinates.

4.3 Activation Functions and Integration

In Bayesian deep learning, one must often compute the Gaussian expectation of an activation function σ: Ez∼N(μ,v)​![σ(z)]=∫−∞∞​σ(z)⋅2πv​1​e−(z−μ)2/(2v),dz.

For the sigmoid σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1, this integral has no closed form. But using a probit approximation σ(z)≈Φ(λz) (where Φ is the Gaussian CDF), one can compute it analytically — again by completing the square in the exponent (a polynomial/algebraic manipulation in the integrand), the same algebraic manoeuvre underlying quadratic rationalisation.

4.4 Score-Based Diffusion Models

Score-based diffusion models (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, DDPMs) require integration over noise schedules. The log-likelihood involves integrals of the form: L=−∫0T​β(t),E![∣sθ​(xt​,t)∣2]dt, where β(t) is a noise schedule (e.g., β(t)=βmin​+t(βmax​−βmin​)). Changing variables s=αˉ(t)=exp!(−∫0t​β(τ),dτ) is a rationalising substitution in spirit — it transforms a complicated integral over t into a simpler one over the signal-to-noise ratio s.

5. Common Errors and Pitfalls

  1. Forgetting to transform dx. After setting t=ng(x)​, one must compute dx in terms of dt and substitute it. Leaving dx alone is the most common mistake.

  2. Domain issues. The substitution t=x​ requires x≥0. For definite integrals, transform the limits: if x:0→4, then t:0→2.

  3. Weierstrass at x=π. The substitution t=tan(x/2) is undefined at x=±π. For integrals over [0,2π], split at π or use the alternative substitution t=cot(x/2).

  4. Wrong lcm. When two radicals are present, using lcm(m,n)−1 instead of lcm(m,n) will leave residual radicals.

  5. Neglecting absolute values. When t2n​=∣tn∣, and tn can be negative, write ∣tn∣ and case-split or restrict the domain appropriately.
