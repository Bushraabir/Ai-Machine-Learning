04.Trigonometric Substitution

Apr 18, 202614 min read

A professor’s personal notebook · Stewart §7.3 · MIT 18.02

Personal note: Trigonometric substitution is geometry in disguise. Every one of the three cases is a right triangle — you are literally naming the sides of a triangle and using the Pythagorean theorem to collapse a square root into a single trig function. Once you draw the triangle, the algebra becomes inevitable. The student who skips the triangle drawing is the student who makes sign errors and forgets to back-substitute. Draw the triangle. Always.

§1 · The Core Idea — Why Trig Substitution Works

We want to integrate expressions involving a2−x2​, a2+x2​, or x2−a2​.

These square roots resist every technique seen so far — they are not derivatives of simple functions, and u-substitution leaves the radical intact.

The key insight: the Pythagorean theorem eliminates square roots of quadratics. Specifically:

1−sin2θ=cos2θ⟹a2−a2sin2θ​=acosθ

1+tan2θ=sec2θ⟹a2+a2tan2θ​=asecθ

sec2θ−1=tan2θ⟹a2sec2θ−a2​=atanθ

We introduce x=a⋅(trig function of θ) to force the radicand into a perfect square.

This is the substitution u=g(θ) running in reverse: we substitute x as a function of θ, integrate in θ, then back-substitute to express the answer in x. The triangle is the back-substitution machine.

§2 · The Three Cases — Substitution Table

Radical formSubstitutionRangeIdentity usedSimplified radical
a2−x2​x=asinθ−2π​≤θ≤2π​1−sin2θ=cos2θacosθ
a2+x2​x=atanθ−2π​<θ<2π​1+tan2θ=sec2θasecθ
x2−a2​x=asecθ0≤θ<2π​ or π≤θ<23π​sec2θ−1=tan2θatanθ

Why these specific ranges?

For x=asinθ: we need cosθ≥0 so that a2−x2​=acosθ (not −acosθ). This holds on [−π/2,π/2].

For x=atanθ: we need secθ>0, which holds on (−π/2,π/2).

For x=asecθ: tanθ≥0 on [0,π/2); if x<−a, use [π,3π/2).

The range restriction is not bureaucracy — it ensures the substitution is a bijection (invertible) on that domain, which is required for u-substitution to be valid. Ignoring the range leads to subtle sign errors in back-substitution.

§4 · Case 1 — a2−x2​: Substitution x=asinθ

Worked Example 1: ∫4−x2​dx​ (recovering arcsin)

Let x=2sinθ, dx=2cosθ,dθ, 4−x2​=2cosθ.

∫2cosθ2cosθ,dθ​=∫dθ=θ+C

Back-substitute from triangle: sinθ=x/2, so θ=arcsin(x/2).

=arcsin2x​+C

This recovers the arcsin formula. Trig sub is the proof that formula is correct.

Worked Example 2: ∫9−x2​,dx

Let x=3sinθ, dx=3cosθ,dθ, 9−x2​=3cosθ.

=∫3cosθ⋅3cosθ,dθ=9∫cos2θ,dθ=9∫21+cos2θ​,dθ

=29​(θ+2sin2θ​)+C=29​θ+49sin2θ​+C

Now sin2θ=2sinθcosθ. From triangle: sinθ=x/3, cosθ=9−x2​/3.

sin2θ=2⋅3x​⋅39−x2​​=92x9−x2​​

=29​arcsin3x​+49​⋅92x9−x2​​+C=29​arcsin3x​+2x9−x2​​+C​

Geometric interpretation: ∫−33​9−x2​,dx=9π/2 is the area of a semicircle of radius 3. ✓ (since πr2/2=9π/2.)

Worked Example 3: ∫9−x2​x2​,dx

Let x=3sinθ, dx=3cosθ,dθ, 9−x2​=3cosθ, x2=9sin2θ.

=∫3cosθ9sin2θ​⋅3cosθ,dθ=9∫sin2θ,dθ=29​∫(1−cos2θ),dθ

=29​(θ−2sin2θ​)+C=29​θ−49sin2θ​+C

From triangle: θ=arcsin(x/3), sin2θ=2x9−x2​/9.

=29​arcsin3x​−2x9−x2​​+C​

Worked Example 4 (definite): ∫03/2​9−x2​x2​,dx

Method 1 — back-substitute, then apply limits. Use the antiderivative from Example 3: [29​arcsin3x​−2x9−x2​​]03/2​ =29​arcsin21​−2(3/2)9−9/4​​−0=29​⋅6π​−2(3/2)(33​/2)​=43π​−893​​

Method 2 — change limits to θ. When x=0: sinθ=0, so θ=0. When x=3/2: sinθ=1/2, so θ=π/6.

9∫0π/6​sin2θ,dθ=29​[θ−2sin2θ​]0π/6​=29​(6π​−2sin(π/3)​)=29​(6π​−43​​)=43π​−893​​

Both methods agree. ✓

§5 · Case 2 — a2+x2​: Substitution x=atanθ

Worked Example 5: ∫x2+4​dx​

Let x=2tanθ, dx=2sec2θ,dθ, x2+4​=2secθ.

=∫2secθ2sec2θ,dθ​=∫secθ,dθ=ln∣secθ+tanθ∣+C

From triangle: tanθ=x/2, secθ=x2+4​/2.

=ln​2x2+4​​+2x​​+C=ln​x+x2+4​​+C

(absorbed the ln(1/2) into C.)

=ln(x+x2+4​)+C​

This equals sinh−1(x/2)+C — connecting to the inverse hyperbolic forms.

Worked Example 6: ∫(x2+4)3/2dx​

Let x=2tanθ, dx=2sec2θ,dθ, (x2+4)3/2=8sec3θ.

=∫8sec3θ2sec2θ,dθ​=41​∫secθdθ​=41​∫cosθ,dθ=4sinθ​+C

From triangle: sinθ=x/x2+4​.

=4x2+4​x​+C​

Worked Example 7: ∫x3x2+9​,dx

Note: Before jumping to trig sub, check if u-sub works. Let u=x2+9, du=2x,dx, x2=u−9:

=∫(u−9)u​⋅2du​=21​∫(u3/2−9u1/2),du=21​(52u5/2​−318u3/2​)+C

Worked Example 8: ∫x2+4​,dx

Let x=2tanθ, dx=2sec2θ,dθ, x2+4​=2secθ.

§6 · Case 3 — x2−a2​: Substitution x=asecθ

This case requires the most care because ∣x∣≥a, and the domain splits into x≥a and x≤−a.

Worked Example 9: ∫xx2−9​​,dx

Let x=3secθ, dx=3secθtanθ,dθ, x2−9​=3tanθ.

=∫3secθ3tanθ​⋅3secθtanθ,dθ=3∫tan2θ,dθ=3∫(sec2θ−1),dθ

=3(tanθ−θ)+C

=x2−9​−3,arcsec3x​+C​

Worked Example 10: ∫x2x2−25​dx​

Let x=5secθ, dx=5secθtanθ,dθ, x2−25​=5tanθ, x2=25sec2θ.

=∫25sec2θ⋅5tanθ5secθtanθ,dθ​=251​∫secθdθ​=251​∫cosθ,dθ=25sinθ​+C

§7 · Completing the Square — Extending Trig Sub to General Quadratics

When the expression under the radical is ax2+bx+c (with a=1 or b=0), complete the square first to reduce to one of the three standard forms.

Algorithm: ax2+bx+c=a[(x+2ab​)2+ac​−4a2b2​]

Let u=x+b/(2a), which shifts the variable. Then the form under the radical becomes A(u2±k2) or A(k2−u2), and the appropriate substitution applies.

Worked Example 11: ∫x2−6x+13​dx​

Complete the square: x2−6x+13=(x−3)2+4.

Let u=x−3, du=dx:

=∫u2+4​du​

This is Example 5 with a=2! From that result:

=ln​u+u2+4​​+C=ln​(x−3)+x2−6x+13​​+C​

Worked Example 12: ∫−x2+4x+5​2x+1​,dx

Complete the square: −x2+4x+5=−(x2−4x)+5=−[(x−2)2−4]+5=9−(x−2)2.

Let u=x−2, x=u+2, dx=du, 2x+1=2(u+2)+1=2u+5:

=∫9−u2​2u+5​,du=∫9−u2​2u​,du+5∫9−u2​du​

First integral: let w=9−u2, dw=−2u,du: ∫9−u2​2u​,du=−∫w−1/2,dw=−2w​=−29−u2​

Second integral: standard form #22: 5∫9−u2​du​=5arcsin3u​

=−29−(x−2)2​+5arcsin3x−2​+C=−2−x2+4x+5​+5arcsin3x−2​+C​

Worked Example 13 (the hardest type): ∫(5+4x−x2)3/2dx​

Complete the square: 5+4x−x2=−(x2−4x−5)=−[(x−2)2−9]=9−(x−2)2.

So (5+4x−x2)3/2=(9−(x−2)2)3/2.

Let u=x−2:

=∫(9−u2)3/2du​

Now u=3sinθ, du=3cosθ,dθ, (9−u2)3/2=27cos3θ:

=∫27cos3θ3cosθ,dθ​=91​∫sec2θ,dθ=9tanθ​+C

From triangle: sinθ=u/3, so cosθ=9−u2​/3, tanθ=u/9−u2​.

=99−u2​u​+C=95+4x−x2​x−2​+C​

§9 · Strategy — When to Use Trig Sub vs. Other Methods

Expression involves √(a²−x²), √(a²+x²), or √(x²−a²)?
│
├─ Is there an odd power of x outside the radical?
│   Example: x³√(x²+4), x√(9−x²)
│   → Try u = (expression under radical) FIRST
│      If u-sub closes → done. If not → trig sub.
│
├─ Is it purely the radical alone, or radical with even powers?
│   Example: ∫√(a²−x²)dx, ∫x²/√(a²−x²)dx
│   → Trig sub directly
│
├─ Is the quadratic not in standard form?
│   Example: √(x²−6x+13), √(5+4x−x²)
│   → Complete the square first → standard form → trig sub
│
└─ Is the integrand rational in √(a²±x²) after substitution?
    Example: ∫dx/(x²√(x²−25))
    → Trig sub → result is trig integral (usually just ∫cosθ or ∫sinθ)

The five-step procedure:

  1. Identify the radical form and the value of a.
  2. Make the substitution from the table in §2.
  3. Draw the triangle and label all three sides.
  4. Convert entirely to θ: radical, dx, any remaining x terms.
  5. Integrate in θ, then back-substitute using the triangle.

§10 · Connection to Inverse Hyperbolic Functions

The results of trig sub are often rewritable using inverse hyperbolic functions (which arise naturally from the x=atanθ and x=asecθ substitutions):

∫x2+a2​dx​=ln(x+x2+a2​)+C=sinh−1ax​+C

∫x2−a2​dx​=ln​x+x2−a2​​+C=cosh−1ax​+C,x>a

∫a2−x2dx​=a1​tanh−1ax​+C=2a1​ln​a−xa+x​​+C,∣x∣<a

The equivalence ln(x+x2+a2​)=sinh−1(x/a) follows from the definition sinh−1(t)=ln(t+t2+1​). Both forms appear in tables; know that they are equal.

§12 · AI / Machine Learning Connections

12.1 The Gaussian Integral — Foundation of All Probabilistic ML

The most important integral in machine learning is:

∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π​

Its multivariable proof (MIT 18.02) uses polar coordinate substitution — which is trig sub in disguise. The key step:

(∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx)2=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2,r,dr,dθ

The substitution x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ is trig substitution in 2D. Every normal distribution, every Gaussian process, every VAE, every diffusion model is normalised by this integral.

The generalisation to d dimensions:

∫Rd​e−∣x∣2/(2σ2),dx=(2πσ2)d/2

is proved by d sequential trig substitutions (or one spherical coordinate substitution — the d-dimensional analogue).

12.2 Normalisation Constants in Kernel Methods

Gaussian RBF kernel: K(x,x′)=e−∣x−x′∣2/(2σ2)

The fact that this kernel is positive-definite (required for a valid SVM kernel) is proved via Bochner’s theorem: K is the Fourier transform of p(ω)=(2πσ2)−d/2e−σ2∣ω∣2/2, which requires the integral

K^(ω)=∫Rd​K(x)e−iωTx,dx

This is a d-dimensional trig integral, evaluated by completing the square in the exponent and using the Gaussian integral result — whose proof is the polar trig substitution above.

12.3 Computing the Partition Function in Energy-Based Models

In statistical mechanics and energy-based models (EBMs), the normalisation constant (partition function) is:

Z=∫Rd​e−E(x)/T,dx

For a quadratic energy E(x)=21​xTAx (multivariate Gaussian):

Z=∫e−21​xTAx,dx=detA​(2π)d/2​

This is proved by:

  1. Diagonalise A=QΛQT (change of variables u=QTx, Jacobian = 1)
  2. Each dimension separates: ∏i=1d​∫e−λi​ui2​/2,dui​=∏i​2π/λi​​
  3. Each factor uses the Gaussian integral, proved via trig substitution in polar form

The result Z=(2π)d/2/detA​ appears in every Gaussian process, every Bayesian linear regression, and every Laplace approximation in deep learning.

12.4 Arc Length and Geodesics in Latent Space

In variational autoencoders and Riemannian manifold learning, one computes arc lengths along curves in a learned latent space. The arc length of a parametric curve γ(t)=(x(t),y(t)) is:

L=∫ab​x′(t)2+y′(t)2​,dt

For the unit circle x=cost, y=sint: L=∫02π​1,dt=2π — trivial. But for an ellipse x=acost, y=bsint:

L=∫02π​a2sin2t+b2cos2t​,dt

This is an elliptic integral — not expressible in closed form — but it is reduced to standard form via trig substitution. Geodesic distance in learned latent manifolds (used in disentangled representation learning) requires exactly this arc-length computation.

12.5 Attention Score Normalisation — The dk​​ Factor

The scaled dot-product attention in Transformers:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QKT​)V

The dk​​ in the denominator is explained as follows. If qi​,kj​∼N(0,1) independently, then qi​⋅kj​=∑l=1dk​​qil​kjl​ has variance dk​.

The standard deviation of q⋅k is dk​​, computed via:

Var[∑l=1dk​​ql​kl​]=∑l=1dk​​Var[ql​kl​]=dk​

where Var[ql​kl​]=E[ql2​kl2​]=E[ql2​]E[kl2​]=1 (computed using the Gaussian integral ∫x2e−x2/2/2π​,dx=1, which again uses the trig-based Gaussian integral at its core).

12.6 Normalising Flows — The Jacobian Determinant

In normalising flows (RealNVP, Glow, Neural Spline Flows), we learn an invertible map f:Rd→Rd and the log-likelihood is:

logpX​(x)=logpZ​(f(x))+log​det∂x∂f​​

For radial flows f(z)=z+βh(α,r)(z−z0​) where r=∣z−z0​∣, computing det∂f/∂z requires evaluating:

∫S​h(α,r),dS

over a sphere — a surface integral evaluated via spherical trig substitution. The residual stream geometry of transformer circuits is analysed using exactly these integrals.

§13 · Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Wrong case. Seeing x2+9​ and substituting x=3sinθ (Case 1) instead of x=3tanθ (Case 2). The sine substitution gives 9−9sin2θ+9​=18−9sin2θ​, which is not simpler. Always match the form to the table.

Mistake 2 — Forgetting dx. Writing x=asinθ but forgetting to replace dx=acosθ,dθ. This is the most common error — the dθ substitution must be applied to dx as well.

Mistake 3 — Sign errors from the range. a2−a2sin2θ​=a∣cosθ∣. On [−π/2,π/2], cosθ≥0, so ∣cosθ∣=cosθ. If you accidentally use θ outside this range, you get a sign flip.

Mistake 4 — Not completing the square first. Attempting trig sub on x2−4x+8​ directly. The correct first step is completing the square: x2−4x+8=(x−2)2+4, then substituting u=x−2.

Mistake 5 — Incorrect back-substitution. Writing sinθ=x/a then guessing cosθ=1−x2/a2​ from memory, with an error in the sign or the structure. Draw the triangle every time — it prevents all back-substitution errors.

Mistake 6 — Using trig sub when u-sub suffices. ∫xa2−x2​,dx: let u=a2−x2, du=−2x,dx. This gives −21​∫u1/2,du=−3u3/2​+C=−3(a2−x2)3/2​+C. No trig needed.
