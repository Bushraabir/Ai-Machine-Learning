“The Fundamental Theorem is not a computational trick. It is a declaration that two operations — differentiation and integration — which were invented to solve completely different problems, are in fact inverse processes. This is one of the most surprising facts in all of mathematics.”

0. The Two Problems, and Why Their Connection is Miraculous

Calculus was born from two entirely separate questions:

The Tangent Problem (Fermat, Descartes, ~1630s):

Given a curve y=f(x), what is the slope of the tangent line at a point? This led to the derivative f′(x).

The Area Problem (Archimedes, Cavalieri, ~250 BC – 1630s):

Given a curve y=f(x), what is the area under it between two points? This led to the definite integral ∫ab​f(x),dx.

These are geometrically opposite questions — one is local (what is f doing right here?), the other is global (what has f accumulated over an interval?). There is no obvious reason they should be related.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) says they are not merely related — they are inverse operations, as opposite as multiplication and division.

This is the theorem. Everything else is commentary.

1. Statement: Two Parts, One Idea

FTC Part 1 — Differentiation Undoes Integration

Theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b], then the area function

g(x)=∫ax​f(t),dt

is differentiable on (a,b) and:

dxd​∫ax​f(t),dt=f(x)​

This says: if you integrate f and then differentiate, you get f back.

FTC Part 2 — Integration Undoes Differentiation

Theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b] and F is any antiderivative of f (i.e., F′=f), then:

∫ab​f(x),dx=F(b)−F(a)​

This says: if you differentiate F to get f, then integrating f recovers the net change in F.

The Compact Picture

DifferentiationFTC 2⇌FTC 1​​Integration

\frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_a^x f(t), dt\right] = f(x) \tag{FTC 1}

\int_a^b \left[\frac{d}{dx}F(x)\right] dx = F(b) - F(a) \tag{FTC 2}

2. Proof of FTC Part 1

Setup. Let f be continuous on [a,b], and define g(x)=∫ax​f(t),dt.

Step 1: Compute g(x+h)−g(x).

By additivity of the integral:

g(x+h)−g(x)=∫ax+h​f(t),dt−∫ax​f(t),dt=∫xx+h​f(t),dt

Step 2: Apply the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals.

Since f is continuous on [x,x+h] (for small h>0), ∃,ch​∈(x,x+h) such that:

∫xx+h​f(t),dt=f(ch​)⋅h

Step 3: Take the limit.

g′(x)=limh→0​hg(x+h)−g(x)​=limh→0​hf(ch​)⋅h​=limh→0​f(ch​)

Since ch​∈(x,x+h), as h→0 we have ch​→x. By continuity of f:

limh→0​f(ch​)=f(x)

Therefore g′(x)=f(x). ■

What Continuity Actually Does

The proof depends on continuity in a precise way. If f has a jump discontinuity at x0​:

f(x0−​)=f(x0+​)

then as h→0+, f(ch​)→f(x0+​) and as h→0−, f(ch​)→f(x0−​). The left and right limits of the difference quotient disagree. So g is not differentiable at x0​ — even though g is still continuous there (integration smooths out jump discontinuities by one degree of regularity).

3. Proof of FTC Part 2

Setup. f continuous on [a,b], F any antiderivative (F′=f).

Step 1: Invoke FTC Part 1.

We know g(x)=∫ax​f(t),dt satisfies g′(x)=f(x).

So both F and g are antiderivatives of f.

Step 2: Antiderivatives differ by a constant.

If F′(x)=g′(x)=f(x) on (a,b), then (F−g)′(x)=0 on (a,b).

By the Mean Value Theorem: if a function has zero derivative everywhere on an interval, it is constant. So:

F(x)−g(x)=Cfor some constant C∈R

Step 3: Determine C and evaluate.

At x=a: g(a)=∫aa​f(t),dt=0. So:

F(a)−0=C⟹C=F(a)

Therefore g(x)=F(x)−F(a) for all x∈[a,b].

At x=b:

∫ab​f(t),dt=g(b)=F(b)−F(a)

■

Notation

We write F(b)−F(a) as:

[F(x)]ab​orF(x)​ab​

The MVT is Load-Bearing

The step “antiderivatives differ by a constant” is not obvious — it requires the MVT for derivatives. Specifically: if h′=0 on (a,b), pick any x∈(a,b); by MVT applied to [a,x], h(x)−h(a)=h′(c)(x−a)=0. So h(x)=h(a) for all x. Without MVT, we cannot conclude F−g=const.

4. The Net Change Theorem

FTC Part 2 has an important physical restatement:

∫ab​F′(x),dx=F(b)−F(a)

The integral of a rate of change equals the net change.

F(t)F′(t)∫ab​F′,dt=F(b)−F(a)

VelocityAccelerationNet change in velocity
Population P(t)Growth rate P′(t)Net population change

6. Why Any Antiderivative Works

FTC Part 2 says ∫ab​f=F(b)−F(a) for any antiderivative F. Why doesn’t the choice matter?

Suppose F and G are both antiderivatives of f: F′=G′=f.

Then (F−G)′=0 so F(x)=G(x)+C for some constant.

G(b)−G(a)=[F(b)−C]−[F(a)−C]=F(b)−F(a)

The constant cancels. This is why we can write ∫x2,dx=3x3​+C and use any particular C when evaluating a definite integral.

7. Visualization: The Geometric Heart of FTC

FTC Part 1 — Geometric Proof Sketch

Fix x. The difference g(x+h)−g(x)=∫xx+h​f(t),dt is the area of a thin vertical strip:

f(t)
  |
  |    *
  |   * *
f(x)|--*---*--
  |  *     *
  |         *
  +----|----|----> t
       x   x+h
       |←h→|
       thin strip ≈ f(x)·h

As h→0, the strip becomes a rectangle of height f(x) and width h. So:

g′(x)=limh→0​harea of strip​=limh→0​hf(x)⋅h+O(h2)​=f(x)

FTC Part 2 — Telescoping Visualization

Partition [a,b] into n subintervals. By MVT for derivatives, on each [xi−1​,xi​]:

F(xi​)−F(xi−1​)=F′(ci​),Δx=f(ci​),Δx

Sum over all subintervals — the differences telescope:

F(b)−F(a)=∑i=1n​[F(xi​)−F(xi−1​)]=∑i=1n​f(ci​),Δx

The right side is a Riemann sum for ∫ab​f. As n→∞:

F(b)−F(a)=∫ab​f(x),dx■

This is arguably the most transparent proof of FTC Part 2 — it shows the integral as a telescoping sum of infinitesimal changes in F.

F(b) - F(a) = [F(x₁)-F(x₀)] + [F(x₂)-F(x₁)] + ··· + [F(xₙ)-F(xₙ₋₁)]
                    ≈f(c₁)Δx       ≈f(c₂)Δx              ≈f(cₙ)Δx
            = Σ f(cᵢ)Δx
            → ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx

8. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

The FTC is not background mathematics for ML — it is operationally present in the core algorithms.

8.1 Backpropagation is FTC Part 2

Consider a scalar loss L as a function of parameters θ(t) evolving along a path. By the chain rule (which is the infinitesimal form of FTC Part 2):

L(θ(T))−L(θ(0))=∫0T​dtdL​,dt=∫0T​∇θ​L⋅θ˙,dt

In discrete gradient descent with steps θk+1​=θk​−η∇L(θk​):

L(θK​)−L(θ0​)≈∑k=0K−1​∇θ​L(θk​)⋅(θk+1​−θk​)=−η∑k=0K​∣∇L∣2

This is a Riemann sum for ∫0T​∇L⋅θ˙,dt — FTC Part 2 in discrete form. Backpropagation computes this derivative so we can integrate it via gradient descent. The entire training loop is:

evaluate Lforward pass​​→compute F′=∇Lbackprop​​→integrate: Δθ=−ηF′gradient step​​

FTC Part 2: integrate the derivative to get net change.

8.2 The Adjoint Method in Neural ODEs is FTC Part 1

In a Neural ODE, the forward pass computes:

z(T)=z(0)+∫0T​fθ​(z(t),t),dt

To train, we need dθdL​. The adjoint state a(t)=∂z(t)∂L​ satisfies:

dtda​=−a(t)⊤∂z∂fθ​​

This ODE is derived by differentiating the area function L(z(T))=L!(z(0)+∫0T​fθ​,dt) with respect to z(t) — applying FTC Part 1 inside the gradient computation. The entire backward pass of a Neural ODE is FTC Part 1 applied to a functional.

8.3 Score Functions and the Log-Derivative Trick

In policy gradient RL and variational inference, we need:

∇θ​Ex∼pθ​​[f(x)]=∇θ​∫f(x),pθ​(x),dx

Passing ∇θ​ under the integral (valid by dominated convergence):

=∫f(x),∇θ​pθ​(x),dx=∫f(x),pθ​(x),pθ​(x)∇θ​pθ​(x)​,dx=Ex∼pθ​​[f(x),∇θ​logpθ​(x)]

This is differentiating under the integral sign — FTC Part 1 applied to a parameter-dependent integral. The result is the REINFORCE / score function estimator, the foundation of policy gradient methods in RL.

8.4 Normalizing Flows: FTC as Change of Variables

A normalizing flow transforms a simple density pz​(z) to a complex density px​(x) via an invertible map x=g(z). The change-of-variables formula:

px​(x)=pz​(g−1(x))​det∂x∂g−1​​

In 1D, this is just:

px​(x)=pz​(z)​dxdz​​=∣g′(g−1(x))∣pz​(g−1(x))​

which is the 1D FTC: ∫ab​pz​(g−1(x))∣dg−1/dx∣,dx=∫g−1(a)g−1(b)​pz​(z),dz. Normalizing flows are architecturally built on FTC as a change-of-variables identity.

8.5 Continuous-Time Bellman Equation

In continuous-time RL with value function V(s,t) and reward r(s,t):

V(s,t)=∫tT​r(s(τ),τ),e−γ(τ−t),dτ+e−γ(T−t)V(s(T),T)

V is an area function of t (with lower limit t). Differentiating via FTC:

∂t∂V​+H(s,∇s​V)=γV

This is the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation — the central equation of optimal control. It is obtained by applying FTC Part 1 to the value function integral. Every RL algorithm (Q-learning, actor-critic, PPO) is, at the continuous-time limit, solving this PDE — which is a consequence of the FTC.

9. Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

Error 1: Forgetting the Chain Rule in FTC Part 1

dxd​∫1x2​et3,dt=ex6

Correct: =e(x2)3⋅2x=ex6⋅2x.

The upper limit is u(x)=x2, so multiply by u′(x)=2x.

Error 2: Mixing the Integration Variable with the Limit

∫1x​x2,dx←ILLEGAL

The integration variable and the limit cannot share a name. Write ∫1x​t2,dt=3x3​−31​.

Error 3: Using FTC Part 2 Across a Discontinuity

∫−11​x21​,dx=[−x1​]−11​=−1−1=−2

f(x)=1/x2 has a non-integrable singularity at x=0. The function is not continuous on [−1,1], so FTC Part 2 does not apply. (The correct answer is +∞ — the integral diverges.)

Always verify continuity on [a,b] before applying FTC Part 2.

Error 4: Sign Errors When Differentiating a Lower Limit

dxd​∫xb​f(t),dt=dxd​(−∫bx​f(t),dt)=−f(x)

Not +f(x). Flip the integral, pick up a minus sign.

10. The Bigger Picture: FTC in the Landscape of Mathematics

The FTC is not an isolated result. It is the n=1 case of a family of theorems connecting derivatives to integrals across dimensions:

TheoremDomainStatement
FTC[a,b]⊂R1∫ab​f′,dx=f(b)−f(a)
Green’s TheoremRegion D⊂R2∬D​(∂x∂Q​−∂y∂P​)dA=∮∂D​P,dx+Q,dy
Stokes’ TheoremSurface S⊂R3∬S​(∇×F)⋅dS=∮∂S​F⋅dr
Divergence TheoremVolume V⊂R3∭V​(∇⋅F),dV=∬​∂V​F⋅dS
Generalized Stokesn-manifold M∫M​dω=∫∂M​ω

All of these say the same thing: the integral of a derivative over a region equals the integral of the original over the boundary. The FTC is the 1D baby case. The generalized Stokes’ theorem (differential geometry) is the full adult.

This is why the FTC matters beyond calculus — it is a special case of one of the most general theorems in mathematics.
