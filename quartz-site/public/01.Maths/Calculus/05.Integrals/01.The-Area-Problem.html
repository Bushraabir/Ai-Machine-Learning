01.The Area Problem

“The definite integral is defined as a limit of Riemann sums, and the process of evaluating a definite integral is called integration.” — Stewart, Calculus (§5.2)

1. The Question

Given a continuous function f(x)≥0 on [a,b], what is the area of the region bounded by y=f(x), the x-axis, and the vertical lines x=a, x=b?

For rectangles, triangles, and circles we have exact formulas. But for an arbitrary curve — y=x2, y=sinx, y=ex — no elementary geometry applies. We need a new definition of area itself.

This is not merely a computational problem. It is a foundational one: we must construct the concept of “area under a curve” from scratch, using limits.

2. The Core Idea — Approximation by Rectangles

The strategy, due essentially to Archimedes (and made rigorous by Cauchy and Riemann in the 19th century), is:

  1. Subdivide the interval [a,b] into n subintervals.
  2. Approximate the region by n rectangles whose heights are determined by f.
  3. Take the limit as n→∞ and the rectangles become infinitely thin.

If this limit exists and is independent of how we choose the rectangle heights, we call it the definite integral of f over [a,b].

3. Riemann Sums — The Formal Construction

3.1 Partition

A partition of [a,b] is a finite set of points P=x0​,x1​,x2​,…,xn​ with a=x0​<x1​<x2​<⋯<xn​=b.

The i-th subinterval is [xi−1​,xi​] with width Δxi​=xi​−xi−1​.

A uniform partition sets all widths equal: Δxi​=Δx=nb−a​, giving xi​=a+i,Δx,i=0,1,…,n.

3.2 Sample Points and the Riemann Sum

On each subinterval [xi−1​,xi​], choose a sample point xi∗​∈[xi−1​,xi​]. The rectangle over the i-th subinterval has:

  • Base =Δxi​
  • Height =f(xi∗​)
  • Area =f(xi∗​),Δxi​

The Riemann sum is the total area of all n rectangles:

Sn​=∑i=1n​f(xi∗​),Δxi​

For an increasing f: Left sum underestimates, Right sum overestimates. For a decreasing f: the roles reverse. The true area is always squeezed between the lower and upper sums.

4. The Definite Integral — Taking the Limit

4.1 Definition

Definition (Riemann Integral). Let f be defined on [a,b]. The definite integral of f from a to b is \int_a^b f(x),dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^_),\Delta x provided this limit exists and is the same for every choice of sample points x_i^_. When this limit exists, f is called Riemann integrable on [a,b].

The notation ∫ab​f(x),dx is a deliberate echo of the sum: ∫ is a continuous Σ, and dx is the infinitesimal limit of Δx.

4.2 Integrability Theorem

Theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b], then f is Riemann integrable on [a,b].

More generally, f is Riemann integrable if it is bounded and has only finitely many discontinuities on [a,b]. Continuity is sufficient but not necessary.

Why does continuity guarantee this? Because on each subinterval, continuity (via the Extreme Value Theorem) guarantees that supf and inff are achieved, and uniform continuity (on a closed interval) forces supf−inff→0 as Δx→0, so upper and lower sums converge to the same limit.

5. Worked Example — Area Under y=x2 on [0,1]

Compute ∫01​x2,dx from the definition.

Use a uniform partition with n subintervals, right endpoints: xi∗​=ni​.

Rn​=∑i=1n​(ni​)2⋅n1​=n31​∑i=1n​i2

The convergence is O(1/n) — halving the error requires doubling n. The midpoint rule, by contrast, achieves O(1/n2) convergence, which is why it is preferred in numerical computation.

8. The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

Theorem. If f is continuous on [a,b], then there exists a point c∈(a,b) such that ∫ab​f(x),dx=f(c)(b−a)

This says: there is some height f(c) such that the rectangle with base (b−a) and height f(c) has exactly the same area as the region under the curve. The value f(c)=b−a1​∫ab​f(x),dx is called the average value of f on [a,b].

Proof. By the Extreme Value Theorem, m≤f(x)≤M on [a,b]. By the bound property above, m(b−a)≤∫ab​f,dx≤M(b−a) m≤b−a1​∫ab​f,dx≤M By the Intermediate Value Theorem (since f is continuous), there exists c∈[a,b] achieving this intermediate value. ■

9. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The area problem and the antiderivative problem appear to be completely unrelated. The Fundamental Theorem is the surprising discovery that they are the same problem.

Part 1 — The Area Function Has a Derivative

Define the area function (or accumulation function): A(x)=∫ax​f(t),dt

This is the area under f from a up to the variable upper limit x.

FTC Part 1. If f is continuous on [a,b], then A(x) is differentiable on (a,b) and A′(x)=dxd​∫ax​f(t),dt=f(x)

Proof. Compute: A′(x)=limh→0​hA(x+h)−A(x)​=limh→0​h1​∫xx+h​f(t),dt

By the MVT for integrals, h1​∫xx+h​f(t),dt=f(ch​) for some ch​∈[x,x+h]. As h→0, ch​→x, and by continuity of f, f(ch​)→f(x). ■

Consequence: A(x) is an antiderivative of f. This is the bridge.

Part 2 — Evaluation via Antiderivatives

FTC Part 2. If F is any antiderivative of f on [a,b], then ∫ab​f(x),dx=F(b)−F(a)=:[F(x)]ab​

Proof. Both A(x) and F(x) are antiderivatives of f, so F(x)=A(x)+C for some constant. Then F(b)−F(a)=A(b)−A(a)=∫ab​f(t),dt−0. ■

The entire Riemann sum machinery — partitions, sample points, limits of sums — is bypassed by this theorem. Instead of computing limn→∞​∑f(xi∗​)Δx, we find an antiderivative and evaluate at the endpoints.

Example. ∫01​x2,dx=[3x3​]01​=31​−0=31​. Done in one line, versus the entire §5 calculation above.

10. Area Between Curves

When f(x)≥g(x) on [a,b], the area of the region between the curves is:

A=∫ab​[f(x)−g(x)],dx

This follows from linearity and the geometric interpretation: the area between the curves equals the area under f minus the area under g.

Example. Area between f(x)=x and g(x)=x2 on [0,1]:

Since x≥x2 on [0,1]: A=∫01​(x−x2),dx=[2x2​−3x3​]01​=21​−31​=61​

When the curves cross, split the integral at the intersection points and take absolute values on each piece.

11. Signed Area

The definite integral computes signed area: regions where f(x)<0 contribute negatively.

∫02π​sinx,dx=[−cosx]02π​=−1−(−1)=0

This is geometrically correct: the positive area on [0,π] and the negative area on [π,2π] cancel exactly. To find total (unsigned) area, integrate ∣f(x)∣:

∫02π​∣sinx∣,dx=∫0π​sinx,dx+∫π2π​(−sinx),dx=2+2=4

12. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The area problem is not merely historical. It is structurally embedded in modern machine learning at multiple levels.

12.1 Loss Functions as Integrals — Expected Risk

The fundamental object in statistical learning theory is the expected risk:

R[f]=E(x,y)∼D​[ℓ(f(x),y)]=∫X×Y​ℓ(f(x),y),p(x,y),d(x,y)

This is a definite integral over the data distribution. Training a model is an attempt to minimise this integral. Since the true distribution p(x,y) is unknown, we approximate the integral by a Riemann sum — the empirical risk:

R^[f]=n1​∑i=1n​ℓ(f(xi​),yi​)

Training on mini-batches is precisely this: approximating the area (expected risk) by rectangles (the batch average). Larger batches → more rectangles → better approximation of the integral. The theory of uniform convergence (Vapnik-Chervonenkis theory) is precisely the study of how fast this Riemann sum approximation converges to the true integral.

12.2 Numerical Integration in Model Training

Many quantities in deep learning require numerical integration because no closed form exists:

  • Marginal likelihood in Bayesian deep learning: p(x)=∫p(x∣z),p(z),dz
  • ELBO in variational autoencoders: L=Eq(z∣x)​[logp(x∣z)]−KL(q∣p)
  • Score functions in diffusion models

These are all area-under-curve problems. They are handled numerically by Monte Carlo integration — a stochastic Riemann sum: ∫f(x),dx≈N1​∑i=1N​f(xi​),xi​∼p(x)

This is importance sampling at its core: choosing where to place the sample points to get the best approximation, exactly as the midpoint rule outperforms the left rule for smooth functions.

12.3 The ROC Curve and AUC

The AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) is a standard classifier evaluation metric. The ROC curve plots True Positive Rate vs. False Positive Rate as the decision threshold varies. The AUC is literally:

AUC=∫01​TPR(FPR),d(FPR)

This is computed numerically via the trapezoidal rule — a Riemann sum using trapezoids instead of rectangles. An AUC of 1 means the curve encloses the full unit square; AUC = 0.5 is the diagonal (random classifier). The entire metric is a definite integral.

12.4 Diffusion Models — Forward and Reverse SDEs

Score-based generative models (e.g. DDPM, DDIM) define a stochastic differential equation:

dx=f(x,t),dt+g(t),dWt​

The probability flow ODE associated with the reverse process requires solving:

x(t)=x(0)+∫0t​f(x(s),s),ds+∫0t​g(s),dWs​

These are stochastic integrals (Itô integrals), but their numerical simulation is done via discretised Riemann-Stieltjes sums — the Euler-Maruyama method. The quality of the generated samples depends directly on how accurately these integrals are computed.

12.5 Attention Mechanism and Continuous Limits

The Transformer attention mechanism can be written as:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(d​QK⊤​)V

In continuous kernel attention (as in neural processes and some vision transformers), this becomes a weighted integral:

f(x)=∫k(x,x′),v(x′),dx′

where k(x,x′) is a kernel (the attention weight). This is a convolution integral, and the area problem is the ground concept underlying it.

12.6 Activation Functions and Area

Recall from the antiderivatives note: the Softplus function is the antiderivative of the sigmoid:

Softplus(x)=ln(1+ex)=∫−∞x​σ(t),dt

The area under the sigmoid from −∞ to x is exactly Softplus. This means that when we integrate the output of a sigmoid activation over its input domain, we recover Softplus — a smoother approximation to ReLU. This design principle (choosing activations whose integrals have desirable properties) is an application of the area problem.

13. Summary

The area problem gave birth to the definite integral, which in turn revealed — through the Fundamental Theorem — the deep unity between differentiation and integration.

The logical chain:

geometric intuitionArea by rectangles​​⟶Riemann sumn→∞lim​∑f(xi∗​)Δx​​⟶definite integral∫ab​f(x),dx​​FTC​antiderivativeF(b)−F(a)​​

The definite integral is simultaneously:

  • A geometric object (area under a curve)
  • An analytic limit (of Riemann sums)
  • A computational tool (via the FTC and antiderivatives)
  • A probabilistic quantity (expected value, marginal likelihood, AUC)
  • A physical measurement (total displacement, total energy, total probability)

The key insight that took mathematicians from Archimedes to Riemann to formalise: area is a limit, and that limit is computed exactly by reverting differentiation.
