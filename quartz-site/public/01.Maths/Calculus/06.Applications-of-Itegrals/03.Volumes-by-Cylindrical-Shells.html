03.Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

03.Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

Apr 18, 202610 min read

“The shell method is what you reach for when the disk/washer method forces you into a contortion you don’t want. It is, in many ways, the more elegant cousin.”

Tags

#calculus #integration #volume #multivariable #ML-connection #stewart #18.02

0. Motivation — Why Another Method?

Recall from Volumes by Disks and Washers that we sliced a solid of revolution perpendicular to the axis, getting circular cross-sections. This works beautifully until it doesn’t.

The failure mode. Consider rotating the region bounded by y=x−x2 and y=0 about the y-axis. To use washers, you’d need to express x as a function of y — which requires solving a quadratic. That’s manageable here, but imagine y=sin(x2). Inverting that is impossible in closed form.

The fix. Instead of slicing perpendicular to the axis, slice parallel to it. Each slice becomes a thin cylindrical shell. This is the shell method.

1. Geometric Setup — Building the Shell

Take a thin vertical strip of the region at position x, width Δx, height f(x).

When you revolve this strip about the y-axis, it sweeps out a cylindrical shell — think of a paper towel tube with very thin walls.

Volume of one shell. The shell has:

  • Inner radius: r1​=x
  • Outer radius: r2​=x+Δx
  • Height: h=f(x)

ΔV=πr22​⋅h−πr12​⋅h=πh[(x+Δx)2−x2]

Expand: ΔV=πf(x)[2x,Δx+(Δx)2]

As Δx→0, the (Δx)2 term is negligible: ΔV≈2πx⋅f(x),Δx

The factor 2πx is the circumference of the shell at radius x. We are literally unrolling the shell into a flat slab of dimensions: circumference × height × thickness.

ΔV≈circumference2πx​​⋅heightf(x)​​⋅thicknessΔx​​​

2. The Main Theorem

Theorem (Shell Method — Rotation about the y-axis). Let f be continuous and f(x)≥0 on [a,b] with 0≤a<b. The volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region under y=f(x) on [a,b] about the y-axis is: V=∫ab​2πx,f(x),dx

Proof sketch. Partition [a,b] into n subintervals. The Riemann sum ∑i=1n​2πxi∗​f(xi∗​),Δxi​ approximates the total volume (as shown in §1). By the definition of the definite integral, taking n→∞ (so ∣Δ∣→0) gives the integral. □

3. The Mnemonic — CHIT

When using the shell method, write down:

dV=2π⋅C(shell radius)​​⋅H(shell height)​​⋅IT (thickness)dx​​

Circumference × Height × thickness = CHIT (or just think: unroll the shell into a flat box).

4. Worked Examples

Example 1 — The Classic

Problem. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by y=x−x2 and y=0 about the y-axis.

Solution.

First, find the x-intercepts: x−x2=0⇒x(1−x)=0, so x∈[0,1].

Shell radius at position x: just x (distance to y-axis). Shell height: f(x)=x−x2.

V=∫01​2πx(x−x2),dx=2π∫01​(x2−x3),dx

=2π[3x3​−4x4​]01​=2π(31​−41​)=2π⋅121​=6π​

Cross-check intuition. Try setting this up as a washer integral (rotating about y-axis). You’d need x=21±1−4y​​. The shell method was clearly preferable.

Problem. Rotate the region between y=x​ and y=x (for x∈[0,1]) about the line x=2.

Example 3 — Rotation about the x-axis (Shells Are Horizontal)

Problem. Rotate the region bounded by x=y2 and x=1 about the x-axis.

When rotating about the x-axis, the natural strips are horizontal (in y). The formula becomes:

V=∫cd​2πy⋅g(y),dy

where g(y) is the horizontal extent of the region at height y.

Setup. The region has y∈[0,1] (by symmetry, consider the upper half and double, or integrate from 0 to 1 with the full horizontal extent).

At height y: the region extends from x=y2 to x=1, so g(y)=1−y2.

V=∫01​2πy(1−y2),dy=2π∫01​(y−y3),dy

=2π[2y2​−4y4​]01​=2π⋅41​=2π​

5. General Formula — Any Axis

The key is always: identify the shell radius and shell height as functions of the integration variable.

The general template:

V=2π∫startend​(shell radius)⋅(shell height),d(variable)

6. Shell vs. Washer — Decision Heuristic

                     Is the integrand expressible naturally 
                     in the variable parallel to the axis?
                              /            \
                            YES             NO
                             |               |
                      Use WASHER         Use SHELL
                      (perpendicular     (parallel
                        slices)           slices)

More concretely:

  • Use washers when rotating about y-axis and x=g(y) is easy; or rotating about x-axis and y=f(x) is easy.
  • Use shells when the function is hard to invert, or when the shell radius and height are naturally expressed in the integration variable you want.

Personal note: I almost always try the shell method first when rotating about a vertical axis with a function given as y=f(x). The algebra is usually cleaner.

7. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

At first glance, this seems like pure geometric calculus. But the shell method — and integration in general — underlies several fundamental concepts in modern ML.

7.1 Probability Density Functions and Marginalization

In Bayesian ML, we constantly marginalize joint distributions:

p(x)=∫p(x,z),dz

The shell method is conceptually the same decomposition: we decompose 3D volume into infinitesimal shells (2D surfaces × thickness), just as we decompose a joint distribution into conditional slices. The change-of-variables in integration is everywhere in probabilistic modeling.

7.2 Gaussian Integrals and the Normal Distribution

The normalization constant of the Gaussian distribution requires computing:

∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π​

The standard proof uses rotation to polar coordinates — which is precisely the spirit of the shell/disk decomposition:

(∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx)2=∬e−(x2+y2),dx,dy=∫0∞​e−r2⋅2πr,dr

That last integrand 2πr⋅e−r2 is exactly the shell method formula in disguise (2πr = circumference, e−r2 = height of the “function”, dr = thickness). This is why the Gaussian is so analytically tractable — it separates in polar coordinates.

7.3 Volume of the n-Ball — The Curse of Dimensionality

In high-dimensional ML (think: weight spaces of neural networks), a key geometric fact is:

Most of the volume of a high-dimensional ball lies near its surface (shell).

This is made rigorous by computing the volume Vn​(R) of an n-dimensional ball of radius R:

Vn​(R)=Γ(n/2+1)πn/2​Rn

The shell at radius r has “volume” (surface measure × thickness):

dVn​=n⋅Γ(n/2+1)πn/2​rn−1,dr

For large n, the factor rn−1 concentrates near r=R. The fraction of the ball’s volume within ϵ of the surface is:

1−(1−Rϵ​)n→1as n→∞

This is the curse of dimensionality — random points in high dimensions are nearly always near the boundary shell. It explains why:

  • Distance-based algorithms (KNN) degrade in high dimensions
  • Sampling from high-dimensional distributions concentrates on shells
  • Neural networks live in very high-dimensional parameter spaces where geometric intuition from 3D fails

7.4 Radial Basis Functions and Kernel Methods

Many kernel functions in SVMs and Gaussian Processes depend only on ∣x−x′∣ — they are radially symmetric. Computing feature map integrals with such kernels uses exactly shell-method decompositions:

∫Rd​K(∣x∣),dx=∫0∞​K(r)⋅Sd−1​(r),dr

where Sd−1​(r) is the surface area of the (d−1)-sphere of radius r. This is the d-dimensional generalization of our 2πr circumference factor.

7.5 Normalizing Flows

Normalizing flows are deep learning models that learn invertible transformations f:Rd→Rd to transform a simple distribution into a complex one. The change-of-density formula uses the Jacobian determinant:

pX​(x)=pZ​(f−1(x))⋅​det∂x∂f−1​​

For radially symmetric flows, this Jacobian computation reduces to a 1D integral — again, the shell decomposition. The shell method is the 1D instance of the general “co-area formula” from geometric measure theory.

8. A Rigorous Note on Validity

The formula V=∫ab​2πxf(x),dx requires:

  1. f continuous on [a,b] — ensures Riemann integrability.
  2. f(x)≥0 on [a,b] — shells have non-negative height; otherwise use ∣f(x)∣ or split the integral.
  3. 0≤a — the axis of rotation (y-axis, x=0) must not cut through the region. If a<0, the shell radius isn’t simply x; you need to be more careful about cancellation.

When f can be negative or the region straddles the axis, write:

V=∫ab​2πx[ftop​(x)−fbottom​(x)],dx

and ensure ftop​≥fbottom​ throughout.

10. Quick Reference — Setup Checklist

When you encounter a volume of revolution problem:

  • Identify the region (sketch it!)
  • Identify the axis of rotation
  • Choose method: can I express everything naturally in one variable?
  • If shells: write dV=2π⋅r⋅h⋅d(var)
  • Determine r = distance from the strip to the axis
  • Determine h = length of the strip (top − bottom or right − left)
  • Find limits of integration
  • Integrate and simplify
