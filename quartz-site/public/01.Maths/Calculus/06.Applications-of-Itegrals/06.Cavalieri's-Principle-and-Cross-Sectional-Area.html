06.Cavalieri's Principle and Cross Sectional Area

06.Cavalieri's Principle and Cross-Sectional Area

Apr 18, 202612 min read

“Cavalieri never computed a single integral. Yet he understood the essence of integration two decades before Newton and Leibniz were born. The principle he articulated is not a trick — it is a theorem about the nature of volume itself.”

Tags

#calculus #integration #volume #geometry #cavalieri #cross-section #ML-connection #stewart #18.02

0. Historical Note

Bonaventura Cavalieri (1598–1647), a student of Galileo, developed his Method of Indivisibles around 1635 — a precursor to integral calculus. His core insight: you can compare volumes (or areas) without knowing their exact shapes, provided you know their cross-sections agree everywhere. Riemann would later make this rigorous, but the geometric intuition is entirely Cavalieri’s.

1. The Principle — Two Versions

1.1 The 2D Version (Areas)

Cavalieri’s Principle (2D). Suppose two plane regions lie between the same two parallel lines y=c and y=d. If every horizontal line y=k (for c≤k≤d) intersects both regions in segments of equal length, then the two regions have equal area.

ASCII illustration:

y = d ─────────────────────────────────────
        │←── L(k) ──→│    │←── L(k) ──→│
y = k  ─┼────────────┼────┼────────────┼──
        │            │    │            │
y = c ─────────────────────────────────────

        Region 1           Region 2
        (different shape, same cross-section lengths)
        ⟹  Area(Region 1) = Area(Region 2)

1.2 The 3D Version (Volumes)

Cavalieri’s Principle (3D). Suppose two solids lie between the same two parallel planes. If every plane parallel to these two planes intersects both solids in cross-sections of equal area, then the two solids have equal volume.

This is the version we care about most in integral calculus.

2. The Rigorous Statement and Proof

Theorem. Let S1​ and S2​ be two solids contained between the planes x=a and x=b. Let A1​(x) and A2​(x) denote the areas of cross-sections of S1​ and S2​ cut by the plane at position x, for each x∈[a,b]. If A1​(x)=A2​(x) for all x∈[a,b], then: V(S1​)=V(S2​)

Proof. By the cross-sectional area method (see §3), the volume of any solid with integrable cross-sectional area function is: V=∫ab​A(x),dx

Since A1​(x)=A2​(x) for all x∈[a,b], we have: V(S1​)=∫ab​A1​(x),dx=∫ab​A2​(x),dx=V(S2​)□

Remark. The real content of this theorem is in the volume formula V=∫ab​A(x),dx, which we establish independently in §3. Cavalieri’s Principle is then an immediate corollary — and a powerful one.

3. Volumes by Cross-Sectional Area — The General Method

This is the foundational method from which both the disk/washer method and Cavalieri’s Principle flow as special cases.

3.1 Setup

Let S be a solid that lies between the planes x=a and x=b. For each x∈[a,b], let A(x) be the area of the cross-section of S in the plane perpendicular to the x-axis at position x.

                          A(x) = cross-sectional area
                          at position x
            
        a ─────────────────────────────── b
        |         |         |             |
        |  A(a)   |  A(x)   |    A(b)     |
        |  ╔═══╗  |  ╔══╗   |    ╔════╗   |
        |  ║   ║  |  ║  ║   |    ║    ║   |
        |  ╚═══╝  |  ╚══╝   |    ╚════╝   |
        |         |         |             |
        
        Slice the solid into thin slabs of thickness Δx.
        Each slab has volume ≈ A(x) · Δx.

3.2 Derivation

Partition [a,b] into n subintervals of width Δxi​. In the i-th slab, pick a sample point xi∗​.

Each slab is approximately a cylinder (in the generalized sense: a shape with uniform cross-section): ΔVi​≈A(xi∗​),Δxi​

Total volume: V≈∑i=1n​A(xi∗​),Δxi​

This is a Riemann sum. Taking ∣Δ∣→0:

V=∫ab​A(x),dx​

Validity condition: A(x) must be continuous (or at least integrable) on [a,b].

4. The Disk and Washer Methods as Special Cases

The cross-section method is the general framework. The disk and washer methods arise when the solid is a solid of revolution.

4.1 Disk Method

Rotate y=f(x)≥0 on [a,b] about the x-axis. The cross-section at x is a disk of radius f(x):

A(x)=π[f(x)]2

V=∫ab​π[f(x)]2,dx

4.2 Washer Method

Rotate the region between y=f(x) (outer) and y=g(x) (inner) about the x-axis. The cross-section at x is a washer (annulus) with outer radius f(x) and inner radius g(x):

A(x)=π[f(x)]2−π[g(x)]2

V=∫ab​π([f(x)]2−[g(x)]2),dx

4.3 Non-Circular Cross-Sections

The method handles any cross-sectional shape — this is where Cavalieri’s Principle becomes powerful in practice.

5. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Pyramid with Square Base (Non-Circular)

Problem. Find the volume of a pyramid with height h and square base of side a.

Setup. Place the apex at the origin, base at x=h. By similar triangles, the cross-section at position x is a square with side s(x)=ha​x.

A(x)=(ha​x)2=h2a2​x2

V=∫0h​h2a2​x2,dx=h2a2​⋅3h3​=31​a2h

This is the standard formula V=31​⋅base⋅height, derived rigorously. Note: no solid of revolution was needed.

Example 2 — Cavalieri Applied: Sphere = Stack of Disks

Problem. Find the volume of a sphere of radius R.

Setup. Center the sphere at the origin. The cross-section at position x∈[−R,R] is a disk of radius r(x)=R2−x2​:

A(x)=π(R2−x2)

V=∫−RR​π(R2−x2),dx=π[R2x−3x3​]−RR​

=π[(R3−3R3​)−(−R3+3R3​)]=π⋅34R3​=34​πR3✓

Example 3 — Cavalieri’s Principle in Action: Oblique Cylinder

Problem. Show that an oblique cylinder has the same volume as a right cylinder with the same base area A and height h.

Right Cylinder:          Oblique Cylinder:
  ┌─────┐                 ╱─────╱
  │     │                ╱     ╱
  │  A  │  height h     ╱  A  ╱  height h (perpendicular)
  │     │              ╱     ╱
  └─────┘             ╱─────╱

Proof via Cavalieri. Every horizontal plane at height y intersects both solids in a cross-section congruent to the base (by the definition of a cylinder — the cross-sections are translates of the base). Hence A1​(y)=A2​(y)=A for all y∈[0,h].

By Cavalieri’s Principle: Voblique​=Vright​=∫0h​A,dy=Ah□

This is why “base times height” works for any cylinder — the obliqueness is irrelevant.

Example 4 — Solid with Triangular Cross-Sections

Problem. The base of a solid is the region in the xy-plane bounded by y=1−x2 and y=0. Every cross-section perpendicular to the x-axis is an equilateral triangle with one side in the base. Find the volume.

Setup. The base extends over x∈[−1,1]. At position x, the base of the equilateral triangle has length ℓ(x)=1−x2.

Area of equilateral triangle with side ℓ: A=43​​ℓ2

So: A(x)=43​​(1−x2)2

V=∫−11​43​​(1−x2)2,dx=43​​∫−11​(1−2x2+x4),dx

=43​​[x−32x3​+5x5​]−11​=43​​⋅1516​=1543​​

Key insight: the solid is not a surface of revolution at all. Cross-sections handle it cleanly.

Example 5 — Comparing Two Solids via Cavalieri

Problem. A sphere of radius R and a cylinder of radius R and height 2R with a double cone removed — show they have equal volume.

This is Archimedes’ Hat-Box Theorem (his proudest result, reportedly requested for his tombstone).

Solid A: Sphere of radius R, centered at origin. Cross-section at height y: Asphere​(y)=π(R2−y2)

Solid B: Cylinder of radius R, height 2R, centered at origin, minus two cones (apex at origin, base at y=±R). At height y, the cone has radius ∣y∣, so the cross-section is a washer: AB​(y)=πR2−πy2=π(R2−y2)

Since Asphere​(y)=AB​(y) for all y∈[−R,R]:

Vsphere​=Vcylinder​−Vdouble cone​=πR2⋅2R−2⋅31​πR2⋅R=2πR3−32​πR3=34​πR3✓

Archimedes proved this entirely without calculus, using balance arguments. Cavalieri’s Principle makes the proof a single paragraph.

7. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

7.1 Computing Expectations — The Probabilistic Cross-Section

In probability and ML, computing the expectation of a function g(X,Y) under a joint distribution is:

E[g(X,Y)]=∫∫g(x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy

By Fubini’s theorem (the rigorous generalization of Cavalieri), we can integrate one variable at a time:

=∫[∫g(x,y),p(y∣x),dy]p(x),dx=∫h(x),p(x),dx

The inner bracket h(x)=E[g(X,Y)∣X=x] is exactly a cross-section of the integrand at fixed x. We are summing cross-sections — Cavalieri’s method, probabilistically.

7.2 Sufficient Statistics and Cross-Sections of the Likelihood

In statistics, a sufficient statistic T(X) captures all the information in data X about a parameter θ. The Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem says:

p(x∣θ)=h(x)⋅g(T(x),θ)

The “cross-section” of the likelihood at fixed T(x)=t has the same shape regardless of x. This is a Cavalieri-like invariance: the data can vary (different x) while the informational cross-section (likelihood shape) stays the same.

7.3 Integral Approximations — Monte Carlo as Riemann Sums

Monte Carlo integration estimates ∫Ω​f(x),dx by sampling:

I^=N∣Ω∣​∑i=1N​f(xi​),xi​∼Uniform(Ω)

The cross-section formula V=∫A(x),dx is the theoretical ground truth that Monte Carlo approximates. In deep learning, computing the partition function Z=∫e−E(x),dx of an energy-based model is intractable — we approximate it via Monte Carlo (MCMC). Cavalieri’s framework — slice, measure, integrate — is the conceptual scaffold.

7.4 Attention Mechanisms — Weighted Cross-Sections

The self-attention operation in transformers computes:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QK⊤​)V

Each output token is a weighted sum of value vectors — a weighted integral over the sequence “dimension.” If you think of the value matrix as a function over sequence positions, attention is computing a weighted cross-section (projection) of the information content at each query position. The softmax weights play the role of a probability measure over cross-sections.

7.5 Neural Tangent Kernel and Volume in Function Space

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework analyzes infinite-width neural networks through the kernel:

K(x,x′)=Eθ​[∂θ∂f(x;θ)​⋅∂θ∂f(x′;θ)​]

This kernel defines an inner product on function space. The “volume” of the region of functions a neural network can represent is analyzed via the eigenspectrum of this kernel — which is computed by integrating cross-sections of the kernel over the data distribution. Cavalieri’s geometric decomposition underlies the functional analysis.

7.6 The Curse of Dimensionality — Cross-Sections of the Hypercube

In a d-dimensional unit hypercube [0,1]d, consider cross-sections perpendicular to the main diagonal. By Cavalieri, the volume is:

V=∫01​A(t),dt=1

But the cross-sectional area A(t) is a polynomial of degree d−1 whose shape concentrates near t=1/2 as d→∞ (by the CLT — the cross-sections are projections of uniform distributions). This concentration means that all the “probability mass” in high-dimensional spaces is in a thin shell around the equator — the same geometric phenomenon seen in Volumes by Cylindrical Shells §7.3.

8. Rigorous Subtleties

8.1 When Does the Formula Fail?

The formula V=∫ab​A(x),dx requires A(x) to be Riemann integrable on [a,b]. By a theorem of Riemann, a bounded function is integrable if and only if its set of discontinuities has measure zero.

For most geometric solids encountered in calculus, A(x) is continuous — so no issue. But for a solid with a fractal cross-section that jumps discontinuously, one must appeal to the Lebesgue integral instead.

8.2 The Measure-Theoretic Version of Cavalieri

In measure theory, Cavalieri’s Principle is a consequence of Fubini’s Theorem:

Let S⊆R3 be a measurable set. Then: Vol(S)=∫−∞∞​Area(S∩x=t),dt

This holds for any Lebesgue-measurable set S and is the rigorous foundation of Cavalieri’s geometric intuition. The condition is that S be measurable — which all “reasonable” geometric solids are.

8.3 Cavalieri Does NOT Imply Congruence

Two solids can satisfy Cavalieri’s conditions (equal cross-sections at every level) and yet be non-congruent. The sphere and the Archimedean solid of Example 5 have the same volume but are obviously not congruent. Volume equality is weaker than congruence.

10. Setup Checklist

When using cross-sections to find volume:

  • Sketch the solid — identify its extent in x (or y, or z)
  • Determine the shape of the cross-section at a generic position x
  • Write A(x) as a function of x using geometry of that shape
  • Set up V=∫ab​A(x),dx with correct limits
  • Integrate
  • Sanity-check: does the answer have units of length3? Does it scale correctly with the parameters?
