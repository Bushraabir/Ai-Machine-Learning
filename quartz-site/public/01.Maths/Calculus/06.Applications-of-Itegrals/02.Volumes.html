“Volume is what happens when you take the area idea seriously in three dimensions. The transition from area to volume is conceptually one step — integrate cross-sectional area — but that one step opens a vast landscape of three-dimensional geometry.”

§ 0 — The Unifying Principle

Every volume formula in this note is a single idea dressed in different clothing:

V=∫ab​A(x),dx​

where A(x) is the cross-sectional area of the solid at position x. Slice the solid into infinitely thin slabs of thickness dx, compute the area of each cross-section, and integrate. This is Cavalieri’s Principle elevated to a theorem.

The only question in any volume problem is: what is A(x)?

§ 1 — Volumes by Cross-Sections (General)

Theorem (Cavalieri’s Principle)

If two solids have equal cross-sectional area A(x) at every x∈[a,b], then they have equal volume, regardless of their shape.

The Volume Formula

Let S be a solid bounded between x=a and x=b. If the cross-section of S perpendicular to the x-axis at position x has area A(x), and A is integrable on [a,b], then:

V=∫ab​A(x),dx

Derivation. Partition [a,b] with points x0​<x1​<⋯<xn​. The slab between xi−1​ and xi​ has approximate volume A(xi∗​),Δxi​. Summing:

V≈∑i=1n​A(xi∗​),Δxi​

Taking ∣P∣→0 gives the integral. □

Solid S sliced perpendicular to the x-axis:

         A(x) ← cross-section at x
          ╭───╮
    ╭─────╯███╰──────╮
    │████████████████│
    │█████A(x)███████│  ← solid
    │████████████████│
    ╰────────────────╯
    a        x       b
    
    V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx
    
    Infinitely many slabs of thickness dx,
    each contributing A(x)·dx to total volume.

§ 2 — The Disk Method

Setup

Rotate the region under y=f(x)≥0 on [a,b] about the x-axis. Each cross-section perpendicular to the x-axis is a disk with radius f(x).

A(x)=π,[f(x)]2

Formula

V=π∫ab​[f(x)]2,dx​

V=π∫cd​[g(y)]2,dy

§ 3 — The Washer Method

Setup

Rotate the region between f(x)≥g(x)≥0 on [a,b] about the x-axis. Each cross-section is an annulus (disk with a hole) — a washer.

  • Outer radius: R(x)=f(x)
  • Inner radius: r(x)=g(x)
  • Cross-sectional area: A(x)=πR2−πr2=π([f(x)]2−[g(x)]2)

Formula

V=π∫ab​([f(x)]2−[g(x)]2),dx​

Washer cross-section at position x:

     ╭───────────────╮  ← outer radius R = f(x)
    ╱  ╭───────────╮  ╲
   │  ╱    hole     ╲  │ ← inner radius r = g(x)
   │ │    (empty)    │ │
   │  ╲             ╱  │
    ╲  ╰───────────╯  ╱
     ╰───────────────╯

  Area = π(R² - r²) = π([f(x)]² - [g(x)]²)

Common error. The washer formula is π(R2−r2), not π(R−r)2. These are different. Always square each radius separately, then subtract.

§ 4 — The Shell Method

Motivation

The disk/washer method slices perpendicular to the axis of rotation, giving circular cross-sections. The shell method slices parallel to the axis of rotation, giving cylindrical shells. For certain regions, shells give a single clean integral where washers require splitting.

Setup

Rotate the region under y=f(x)≥0 on [a,b] (with a≥0) about the y-axis. A thin vertical strip at position x with width dx sweeps out a cylindrical shell:

  • Radius: x
  • Height: f(x)
  • Thickness: dx
  • Volume of shell: 2πx⋅f(x),dx (circumference × height × thickness)

Formula

V=2π∫ab​x,f(x),dx​

Shell method — rotating about y-axis:

     y
     │    f(x)
     │  ╭──────╮
     │  │██████│ ← thin strip at x
     │  │██████│   width dx, height f(x)
     │  │██████│
     └──┼──────┼──→ x
        x  x+dx

  This strip sweeps a cylindrical shell:
  
       ╭──────────────────────╮
      ╱│                      │╲
     │ │  ← shell radius x    │ │
      ╲│  height f(x)         │╱
       ╰──────────────────────╯
       
  Volume = 2πx · f(x) · dx

General Shell Formula

For rotation about the y-axis with region between f and g (f≥g):

V=2π∫ab​x,[f(x)−g(x)],dx

For rotation about the x-axis, integrate with respect to y:

V=2π∫cd​y,[f(y)−g(y)],dy

Disk vs. Shell: Which to Use?

SituationPreferred Method
Rotating about x-axis, region given as y=f(x)Disk/Washer in x
Rotating about y-axis, region given as y=f(x)Shell in x (avoids inverting f)
Rotating about y-axis, region given as x=g(y)Disk/Washer in y
Rotating about x-axis, region given as x=g(y)Shell in y

The shell method is typically superior when inverting the function to change variables would be messy.

§ 5 — Rotation About Lines Other Than the Axes

When rotating about the line y=k (instead of y=0), the radius is the distance from the curve to the axis.

  • Region above y=k: radius =f(x)−k
  • Region below y=k: radius =k−f(x)

Example. Rotating y=x2 about y=−1: V=π∫ab​[x2−(−1)]2,dx=π∫ab​[x2+1]2,dx

Similarly for rotation about x=k: replace x with ∣x−k∣ as the radius in the disk formula, or adjust the shell radius.

The key. Radius always means the perpendicular distance from the curve to the axis of rotation. Draw a picture. Identify the distance formula. Then plug into the universal formula V=∫A(x),dx.

§ 6 — Volumes by Known Cross-Sections (Non-Circular)

Not all solids come from rotation. A solid may have cross-sections that are squares, triangles, semicircles, or any shape.

Method

If the cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are known shapes, express A(x) in terms of a characteristic length determined by the base curve, then integrate.

Common cross-section areas:

ShapeIf side/diameter =sArea A(x)

Isosceles right triangle (leg =s)s=f(x)−g(x)21​[f(x)−g(x)]2

Example. The base of a solid is the region between y=x​ and y=0 on [0,4]. Cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares with side =x​:

V=∫04​(x​)2,dx=∫04​x,dx=[2x2​]04​=8

§ 7 — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Disk Method

Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating y=x​ on [0,4] about the x-axis.

V=π∫04​(x​)2,dx=π∫04​x,dx=π[2x2​]04​=8π

Example 2 — Washer Method

Find the volume obtained by rotating the region between y=x and y=x2 on [0,1] about the x-axis.

Intersections: x=x2⇒x=0,1. On [0,1]: x≥x2, so R=x, r=x2.

V=π∫01​(x2−x4),dx=π[3x3​−5x5​]01​=π(31​−51​)=152π​

Example 3 — Shell Method

Find the volume obtained by rotating y=x−x2 (above y=0) on [0,1] about the y-axis.

Inverting y=x−x2 for x in terms of y is messy. Use shells.

V=2π∫01​x(x−x2),dx=2π∫01​(x2−x3),dx=2π[3x3​−4x4​]01​=2π⋅121​=6π​

Example 4 — Rotation About y=1

Rotate the region between y=x2 and y=0 on [0,1] about y=1.

The axis y=1 is above the region. Distances:

  • Outer radius (from axis down to y=0): R=1−0=1
  • Inner radius (from axis down to y=x2): r=1−x2

V=π∫01​(R2−r2),dx=π∫01​(1−(1−x2)2),dx

=π∫01​(1−1+2x2−x4),dx=π∫01​(2x2−x4),dx

=π[32x3​−5x5​]01​=π(32​−51​)=157π​

Example 5 — Classic: Volume of a Sphere

The sphere of radius r is the rotation of y=r2−x2​ about the x-axis on [−r,r]. Cross-sections are disks of radius r2−x2​:

V=π∫−rr​(r2−x2),dx=π[r2x−3x3​]−rr​=π[(r3−3r3​)−(−r3+3r3​)]=34​πr3

The familiar formula, derived from first principles. □

Example 6 — Shell, Region Between Two Curves

Rotate the region between y=x+2 and y=x2 about the y-axis.

Intersections: x+2=x2⇒x=−1,2.

For x≥0: shells work cleanly. But the region extends to x=−1<0.

For shells about the y-axis with region crossing x=0, the shell radius is ∣x∣. Split at x=0:

V=2π∫−10​∣x∣(x+2−x2),dx+2π∫02​x(x+2−x2),dx

In the first piece, ∣x∣=−x for x<0:

V=2π∫−10​(−x)(x+2−x2),dx+2π∫02​x(x+2−x2),dx

Computing each (left as exercise): V=67π​+38π​=67π+16π​=623π​.

Or: use the washer method in y, where the region is described cleanly as x=g(y) with a single outer-inner pair. This is often cleaner for such problems.

§ 9 — Connection to MIT 18.02

In 18.02, the volume formula V=∫ab​A(x),dx is recovered as a iterated integral:

V=∭S​dV=∫ab​(∬D(x)​dA)dx=∫ab​A(x),dx

where D(x) is the cross-sectional region at x. This is Fubini’s theorem — the triple integral over a 3D solid factors into an outer integral over x and an inner double integral over the cross-section.

Solids of revolution in cylindrical coordinates. The solid obtained by rotating y=f(x) about the x-axis is described in cylindrical coordinates (x,r,θ) as (x,r,θ):a≤x≤b, 0≤r≤f(x), 0≤θ≤2π. The volume is:

V=∫ab​∫0f(x)​∫02π​r,dθ,dr,dx=∫ab​∫0f(x)​2πr,dr,dx=∫ab​π[f(x)]2,dx

The disk formula is exactly the triple integral in cylindrical coordinates with the θ and r integrations carried out first.

The shell formula from cylindrical coordinates. Reordering integration — integrating over x first, then r:

V=∫0R​∫02π​∫x:r≤f(x)​r,dx,dθ,dr=2π∫0R​r⋅(length at radius r),dr

This is the shell formula. The two methods are the same triple integral evaluated in different orders — Fubini’s theorem.

§ 10 — Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

10.1 — Volumetric Data and 3D Convolutions

Medical imaging (CT, MRI), LiDAR point clouds, and 3D object recognition all involve volumetric data — functions defined on R3. Computing the total mass, density integral, or activation volume of a 3D feature map is:

V=∫∫∫S​f(x,y,z),dV

which reduces to the Cavalieri principle when f=1 (indicator function). Deep networks processing 3D data (3D CNNs, PointNet, NeRF) operate on discretizations of this integral. Understanding the continuous limit — the true volume integral — is essential for interpreting what these networks compute.

10.2 — Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)

NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020) represents a 3D scene as a neural network that maps position (x,y,z) and viewing direction to color and density. The rendered pixel color along a ray is:

C(r)=∫tn​tf​​T(t),σ(r(t)),c(r(t),d),dt

where T(t)=exp!(−∫tn​t​σ(r(s)),ds) is transmittance, and σ is volume density. This is a volume integral along a ray — the continuous generalization of the cross-section formula. The density σ plays the role of A(x); integrating it along the ray gives the accumulated opacity.

The discrete approximation used in practice is:

C(r)≈∑i=1N​Ti​(1−e−σi​δi​),ci​

This is a Riemann sum approximation of the volume integral — exactly the slab approximation from § 1.

NeRF volume rendering:

  camera → ray through scene → pixel color
  
  ╭──────────────────────────────────────╮
  │ 3D scene (density field σ(x,y,z))   │
  │                                      │
  │  •  •  ●  ●  ●  ●  •  •  •  •      │  ← sample points
  │                ↑ high density        │
  ╰──────────────────────────────────────╯
  
  C = ∫ T(t)·σ(t)·c(t) dt  ← volume integral
    ≈ Σ Tᵢ·(1−e^{−σᵢδᵢ})·cᵢ  ← Riemann sum

10.3 — Volumetric Loss Functions in 3D Generation

3D generative models (occupancy networks, signed distance functions, 3D diffusion models) define a loss over a volume:

L=∫Ω​∣fθ​(x)−f∗​(x)∣2,dV

This is a continuous loss integral — the L2 distance between two volumetric functions. In practice, it is approximated by Monte Carlo sampling (a Riemann sum), but the object being minimized is precisely the volume integral.

10.4 — Solid Angle and Attention

In graphics and physically-based rendering (which underlies diffusion models trained on rendered data), the solid angle subtended by a surface patch is:

Ω=∬S​r2cosθ​,dA

This is a surface integral (18.02), but its numerical approximation mirrors the cross-section method: sample directions, weight by the integrand, sum. Attention mechanisms in transformers compute a weighted sum over queries — a discrete analog of exactly this kind of integral over a “solid angle” of context.

10.5 — Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks and Univariate Integration

KAN (Liu et al., 2024) represents functions as compositions of learnable univariate functions placed on edges. Each edge function is implicitly a definite integral (or its inverse). The architecture is motivated by the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which expresses multivariate functions in terms of univariate ones — a decomposition that, in the continuous limit, involves volume integrals factoring into products of one-dimensional integrals (when the function is separable). Understanding when ∭f(x)g(y)h(z),dx,dy,dz=(∫f)(∫g)(∫h) — which is the separability of volumes — directly informs when KAN representations are exact.

10.6 — Expectation as Volume Under a Surface

In probability, the expected value of f(X,Y) under a joint distribution p(x,y) is:

E[f(X,Y)]=∬f(x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy

This is the volume under the surface z=f(x,y),p(x,y) in R3. Every 2D expectation in machine learning — joint entropy, mutual information, 2D kernel density estimation, bivariate loss functions — is a volume computation. Monte Carlo integration approximates this volume with a Riemann sum over samples.

§ 11 — Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Squaring after subtracting instead of subtracting after squaring. Washer: π(R2−r2)=π(R−r)2. Always square each radius separately, then subtract.

Mistake 2: Using the wrong radius when rotating about a non-standard axis. If rotating about y=2 and the curve is y=x2, the radius is 2−x2 (not x2). Always draw the axis and measure the perpendicular distance.

Mistake 3: Forgetting limits when using the shell method with region crossing x=0. The shell radius is ∣x∣, so negative x must be handled separately. When in doubt, switch to washers.

Mistake 4: Applying the disk formula to a region that isn’t touching the axis. If the region between y=1 and y=3 is rotated about the x-axis, there is a hole — use the washer method, not the disk method.

Mistake 5: Not identifying intersection points correctly. The limits of integration must be the x-values where the bounding curves meet (or the stated boundary). Sketch the region and its rotation before writing any integral.

Mistake 6: Confusing which method integrates in which variable. Disk/Washer rotated about x-axis → integrate in x. Shell about y-axis with y=f(x) → integrate in x. Getting this crossed produces an integral that is either wrong or much harder than necessary.
