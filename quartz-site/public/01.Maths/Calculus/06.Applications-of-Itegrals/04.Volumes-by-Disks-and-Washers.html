04.Volumes by Disks and Washers

04.Volumes by Disks and Washers

Apr 18, 202611 min read

“Solids of revolution are the first place calculus reveals something the eye cannot directly see: a 2D region, spun through space, carving out a 3D object with exact, computable volume. The disk method is the most direct incarnation of this idea.”

Tags

#calculus #integration #volume #solids-of-revolution #ML-connection #stewart #18.02

0. Conceptual Foundation — Cavalieri’s Principle

Before any formula, here is the geometric principle underlying everything.

Cavalieri’s Principle. If two solids have the same cross-sectional area A(x) at every x∈[a,b], then they have the same volume.

This means: volume is completely determined by the cross-sectional area function. If we can write down A(x), we can compute the volume by integrating it.

V=∫ab​A(x),dx​

This is the master formula. The disk and washer methods are simply the special cases where the cross-sections happen to be circles or annuli (rings).

1. The Disk Method

1.1 Setup

Take a region under y=f(x) on [a,b] and revolve it about the x-axis.

What does a cross-section look like? Slice the solid with a plane perpendicular to the x-axis at position x. The cross-section is a disk (filled circle) with:

  • Radius R=f(x) (the y-value at that x)
  • Area A(x)=π[f(x)]2

         |      ╭─╯          ╰─╮   ← y = f(x)
         |    ╭─╯              ╰─╮
         |   ─╯    region        ╰─
─────────┼──────────────────────────  x
         |   a                   b

                  │     ●─────┤ ← radius = f(x)
                  │           │

                  Disk of radius f(x)

1.2 The Formula

Applying Cavalieri’s Principle with A(x)=π[f(x)]2:

V=π∫ab​[f(x)]2,dx​

Rotation about the y-axis. If instead we revolve x=g(y) on [c,d] about the y-axis:

V=π∫cd​[g(y)]2,dy

The logic is identical; only the orientation changes.

1.3 Worked Example — The Cone

Problem. Prove that the volume of a cone of radius r and height h is 31​πr2h.

Setup. Place the apex at the origin, axis along the x-axis. The slant edge is the line y=hr​x, for x∈[0,h].

Disk radius at position x: R(x)=hr​x.

V=π∫0h​(hr​x)2dx=h2πr2​∫0h​x2,dx=h2πr2​⋅3h3​=31​πr2h□

This is deeply satisfying: the factor of 31​ emerges from ∫x2,dx=3x3​, not from some geometric trick.

1.4 Worked Example — The Sphere

Problem. Prove that the volume of a sphere of radius r is 34​πr3.

Setup. The sphere is the solid obtained by revolving the semicircle y=r2−x2​ on [−r,r] about the x-axis.

Disk radius at position x: R(x)=r2−x2​.

V=π∫−rr​(r2−x2),dx=π[r2x−3x3​]−rr​

=π[(r3−3r3​)−(−r3+3r3​)]=π⋅34r3​=34​πr3□

2. The Washer Method

2.1 Setup

Now suppose the region is between two curves: y=f(x) on top and y=g(x) on the bottom, with f(x)≥g(x)≥0 on [a,b].

When revolved about the x-axis, the cross-section at x is no longer a full disk — it is an annulus (washer): a disk with a hole punched in it.

        Outer radius: R = f(x)
        Inner radius: r = g(x)

                 ╭──────────────╮
               ╭─╯  ╭──────╮    ╰─╮
              ─╯    │  hole │     ╰─
                    ╰──────╯
               ╰─╮              ╭─╯
                 ╰──────────────╯

        Area = π R² - π r² = π(R² - r²)

Washer area: A(x)=π[f(x)]2−π[g(x)]2=π([f(x)]2−[g(x)]2)

2.2 The Formula

V=π∫ab​([f(x)]2−[g(x)]2)dx​

Common mistake. [f(x)]2−[g(x)]2=[f(x)−g(x)]2. The subtraction is outside the squaring. You square each radius separately, then subtract.

Rotation about the y-axis: V=π∫cd​([fright​(y)]2−[fleft​(y)]2)dy

where fright​ and fleft​ are the right and left boundaries of the region as functions of y.

2.3 Worked Example — Classic Washer

Problem. Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by y=x2 and y=x (for x∈[0,1]) about the x-axis.

Find intersections: x2=x⇒x=0 or x=1.

On [0,1]: x≥x2, so outer radius R=x, inner radius r=x2.

V=π∫01​(x2−x4)dx=π[3x3​−5x5​]01​=π(31​−51​)=152π​

2.4 Worked Example — Rotation about a Non-Standard Axis

Problem. Rotate the region bounded by y=x2 and y=4 about the line y=4.

Key shift. The axis is y=4, not y=0. The radius of each disk is the distance from the curve to the axis.

The region is bounded above by y=4 and below by y=x2, for x∈[−2,2].

Since we rotate about y=4 (which is the top boundary), and y=x2 is below it:

R(x)=4−x2(distance from y=x2 to y=4)

No hole (the top boundary is the axis itself), so this is a pure disk method:

V=π∫−22​(4−x2)2,dx=π∫−22​(16−8x2+x4),dx

By symmetry, =2π∫02​(16−8x2+x4),dx:

=2π[16x−38x3​+5x5​]02​=2π(32−364​+532​)=2π⋅15256​=15512π​

2.5 Worked Example — Revolving About a Vertical Axis (Washers in y)

Problem. Rotate the region bounded by x=y2 and x=4 about the y-axis.

The curves meet at y=±2.

At height y: the region runs from x=y2 (left boundary) to x=4 (right boundary).

Revolving about y-axis: the outer radius is R=4, inner radius is r=y2.

V=π∫−22​(16−y4)dy=2π∫02​(16−y4),dy=2π[16y−5y5​]02​

=2π(32−532​)=2π⋅5128​=5256π​

3. General Framework — Any Axis of Rotation

Let d(x) denote the distance from a point at coordinate x to the axis of rotation.

ConfigurationOuter Radius RInner Radius r
About x-axis, f≥g≥0f(x)g(x)
About y=k (above region), f≤kk−g(x)k−f(x)
About y=k (below region), f≥kf(x)−kg(x)−k
About y-axis, fR​≥fL​≥0 (in y)fR​(y)fL​(y)
About x=k (right of region)k−fL​(y)k−fR​(y)
About x=k (left of region)fR​(y)−kfL​(y)−k

The rule: R is always the radius to the far boundary, r to the near boundary (or zero for the disk case).

5. Relation to Shell Method — The Complementary Pair

The disk/washer method and the shell method are dual decompositions of the same solid. Given the same solid:

Disk/WasherShell
SlicesPerpendicular to axisParallel to axis
ElementThin circular slabThin cylindrical shell
Best whenFunction easy to express in variable along axisFunction hard to invert
Example: rotate y=f(x) about x-axisNatural (integrate in x)Need f invertible as x=f−1(y)
Example: rotate y=f(x) about y-axisNeed inverse x=f−1(y)Natural (integrate in x)

Personal heuristic. For rotation about the x-axis with y=f(x) given: use disks/washers (integrate in x, everything is natural). For rotation about the y-axis with y=f(x) given: strongly prefer shells (avoid inverting f).

6. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

6.1 Cavalieri’s Principle → Lebesgue Integration

The master formula V=∫A(x),dx is an instance of Fubini’s theorem: computing a higher-dimensional integral by iterated 1D integrals over cross-sections. In ML, we constantly use this:

E(x,y)∼p​[f(x,y)]=∫(∫f(x,y),p(y∣x),dy)p(x),dx

The inner integral is a “cross-section” (conditional expectation at fixed x), and the outer integral averages over all x. Fubini is the theorem that justifies swapping integration order — essential for almost every derivation in probabilistic ML.

6.2 The Gaussian — Disk Slices of the Bell Curve

The normalization of the multivariate Gaussian N(0,I) in Rd requires:

∫Rd​e−∣x∣2/2,dx=(2π)d/2

The proof proceeds by disk slicing: level sets of e−∣x∣2/2 are spheres. At radius r, the level set has surface area Sd−1​rd−1 (a generalized “disk”), so:

∫Rd​e−∣x∣2/2,dx=∫0∞​e−r2/2⋅Sd−1​rd−1,dr

This is the disk method in d dimensions — integrating spherical cross-sections. The entire theory of multivariate Gaussians rests on this.

6.3 Fisher Information and the Shape of the Loss Landscape

In optimization and information geometry (used in natural gradient descent, second-order optimization for deep networks), the Fisher information matrix is:

Fij​(θ)=E[∂θi​∂logp(x∣θ)​⋅∂θj​∂logp(x∣θ)​]

For a 1D exponential family, F(θ) has a clean geometric interpretation as the curvature of the cross-section of the statistical manifold. The disk/washer decomposition provides the geometric intuition: the Fisher metric measures how the “cross-sectional shape” of the probability simplex changes as you move along θ.

6.4 Activation Functions and “Solid” Coverage

In neural networks, the effective volume of the activation region of a neuron (the set of inputs that activate it) can be analyzed using disk/washer cross-sections when the decision boundaries are spherical or ellipsoidal (e.g., RBF networks, Gaussian kernel SVMs). The expressivity of such neurons is directly related to the volume of these regions:

Vactive​=∫region​A(x),dx

Understanding this volume is crucial for capacity analysis.

6.5 KL Divergence — Integration Over Cross-Sections of Probability Space

The Kullback-Leibler divergence:

DKL​(p∣q)=∫p(x)logq(x)p(x)​,dx

is itself an integral of cross-sections. In variational inference (the backbone of VAEs), we minimize:

L=Eq(z∣x)​[logp(x∣z)]−DKL​(q(z∣x)∣p(z))

The second term has an exact closed form for Gaussians — derived by disk-slicing (the cross-sectional areas of Gaussian ellipsoids integrate cleanly). This is why VAEs are analytically tractable.

6.6 Volume of Confidence Ellipsoids

In Bayesian linear regression and Gaussian processes, the posterior uncertainty is captured by an ellipsoidal confidence region:

E=θ:(θ−θ^)⊤Σ−1(θ−θ^)≤χd2​(α)

The volume of this ellipsoid is computed by the washer/disk method after an orthogonal change of variables (diagonalizing Σ):

V(E)=Γ(d/2+1)πd/2​∏i=1d​λi​​⋅[χd2​(α)]d/2

where λi​ are eigenvalues of Σ. The Γ function in the denominator comes directly from integrating the disk cross-sections via the gamma integral ∫0∞​rd−1e−r,dr=Γ(d).

7. Rigorous Conditions for Validity

The formula V=π∫ab​[f(x)]2,dx requires:

  1. f continuous on [a,b] — guarantees [f(x)]2 is Riemann integrable.
  2. f(x)≥0 on [a,b] — the radius must be non-negative. If f takes negative values, the radius is ∣f(x)∣ (the disk is the same regardless of sign), so replace f(x) with ∣f(x)∣.
  3. For washers: f(x)≥g(x)≥0 on [a,b] — outer radius is genuinely larger, and both radii are non-negative. If g<0, the formula breaks down; rewrite as distance to the axis.

What if f changes sign? Split the integral at zeros and use ∣f(x)∣ throughout. The disk’s radius is always a positive length — the sign of f is geometrically irrelevant.

8. Setup Checklist

  • Sketch the region carefully — label curves, identify top/bottom or left/right
  • Identify the axis of rotation
  • Determine integration variable (x or y) based on which way you slice
  • Identify cross-section type: disk (one boundary) or washer (two boundaries)
  • Write R(x) and r(x) as distances to the axis (not just function values!)
  • Set limits from the intersection points or given bounds
  • Write V=π∫[R2−r2],dx (or dy) and integrate
  • Sanity check: units (length3), sign (must be positive), rough magnitude

9. Quick Reference

Disk: V=π∫ab​[R(x)]2,dxWasher: V=π∫ab​([R(x)]2−[r(x)]2)dx

In y: V=π∫cd​([R(y)]2−[r(y)]2)dy

Memory hook: “Area of a washer = πR2−πr2. Volume = integrate the washer area.”
