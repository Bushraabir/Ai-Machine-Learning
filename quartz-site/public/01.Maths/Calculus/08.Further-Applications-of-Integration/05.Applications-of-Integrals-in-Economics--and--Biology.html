Prelude — The Same Engine, Different World

The physics note established that integration is the mathematics of accumulation. In economics and biology, the same machinery reappears — but what accumulates is different:

FieldWhat accumulatesMeasure
PhysicsForce, pressuredx, dA
EconomicsValue, surplus, discounted cash flowdq, dt
BiologyPopulation, drug dose, probability of survivaldt, dN

The Riemann sum template is unchanged: Q=∫ab​f(x),dx=limn→∞​∑i=1n​f(xi∗​),Δx

What changes is the interpretation of f and x.

ECONOMICS

1. Consumer Surplus & Producer Surplus

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §6.1)

Setup: In a market, demand is described by the inverse demand function p=D(q) (willingness to pay) and supply by p=S(q). At equilibrium (Q^_, P^_):

D(Q^_) = S(Q^_) = P^*

Consumer Surplus (CS): Consumers who would have paid more than P∗ gain a “surplus” — the difference between what they were willing to pay and what they actually paid.

\boxed{CS = \int_0^{Q^_} D(q), dq - P^_ \cdot Q^*}

Derivation: Partition [0, Q^_] into n small intervals of width Δq. In strip i, a consumer buying Δq units valued them at \approx D(q_i^_). Surplus on strip i: (D(q_i^_) - P^_)\Delta q. Sum and take limit: CS = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n (D(q_i^_) - P^_)\Delta q = \int_0^{Q^_}(D(q) - P^_), dq \quad \blacksquare

Producer Surplus (PS): Analogously, producers who would have sold for less than P^_ gain surplus: \boxed{PS = P^_ \cdot Q^* - \int_0^{Q^*} S(q), dq}

Total Welfare: W=CS+PS=∫0Q∗​(D(q)−S(q)),dq

Example 1.1 — Linear Demand & Supply

Setup: D(q)=120−2q, S(q)=20+3q. Find equilibrium and both surpluses.

Equilibrium: 120−2q=20+3q⟹100=5q⟹Q∗=20,P∗=80

Consumer Surplus: CS=∫020​(120−2q),dq−80⋅20 =[120q−q2]020​−1600=(2400−400)−1600=400​

Producer Surplus: PS=80⋅20−∫020​(20+3q),dq =1600−[20q+23q2​]020​=1600−(400+600)=600​

Total welfare: W=400+600=1000.

Geometric check: CS is the area of the triangle above P∗=80 and below the demand curve from q=0 to q=20. Base =20, height =D(0)−P∗=120−80=40. Area =21​(20)(40)=400. ✓

Example 1.2 — Nonlinear Demand: Market for a Tech Product

Setup: D(q)=q+1​200​, S(q)=5q+15. Find Q^_, P^_, and CS.

Equilibrium: q+1​200​=5q+15. Try q=3: 2200​=100,5(3)+15=30.No.

Try q=15: 4200​=50, 5(15)+15=90. No. Try q=7: 8​200​=70.7, 5(7)+15=50. Getting closer. Try q=9: 10​200​=63.2, 60. Try q=24: 5200​=40, 135. Hmm.

Numerically, equilibrium at approximately Q∗≈9.64, P∗≈62.

CS (exact form): CS = \int_0^{Q^_} \frac{200}{\sqrt{q+1}}, dq - P^_ Q^* = \left[400\sqrt{q+1}\right]_0^{Q^_} - P^_ Q^_ = 400(\sqrt{Q^_+1} - 1) - P^* Q^*

Takeaway: With nonlinear demand, closed-form equilibrium may require numerical methods, but the surplus integral is still analytically tractable once Q∗ is known.

2. Present Value & Continuous Discounting

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §7.8 context)

Money has time value: 1$ received today is worth more than 1receivedinthefutureduetoearningpotential.Undercontinuouscompoundingatrater$:

\text{Future value of } $P \text{ at time } t: \quad FV = Pe^{rt} \text{Present value of } $F \text{ received at time } t: \quad PV = Fe^{-rt}

For a continuous income stream at rate f(t) (dollars per unit time):

PV=∫0T​f(t),e−rt,dt​

Derivation: In time interval [t,t+dt], income received ≈f(t),dt. Its present value is f(t),dt⋅e−rt. Accumulate from 0 to T. ■

Perpetuity (T→∞, constant f): PV=∫0∞​fe−rt,dt=f[−re−rt​]0∞​=rf​

Example 2.1 — Business Acquisition Valuation

Setup: A business generates revenue at rate f(t)=50,000+3,000t dollars/year (linearly growing). Discount rate r=0.06. What is the present value over a 10-year horizon?

PV=∫010​(50000+3000t),e−0.06t,dt

Split into two integrals:

Part 1: ∫010​50000,e−0.06t,dt=50000⋅[−0.06e−0.06t​]010​ =0.0650000​(1−e−0.6)=833333(1−0.5488)=833333(0.4512)≈375,963

Part 2: ∫010​3000t,e−0.06t,dt. By parts: u=t, dv=e−0.06tdt: =3000[−0.06t,e−0.06t​+0.061​∫010​e−0.06t,dt] =3000[−0.0610e−0.6​+0.061​⋅0.061−e−0.6​] =3000[−91.47+125.33]=3000×33.86≈101,580

\boxed{PV \approx $477{,}543}

Interpretation: If you could buy this business for less than $477{,}543 today, it’s a positive-NPV investment at a 6% discount rate.

Example 2.2 — Continuous Perpetuity

Setup: An endowment fund pays out $$80{,}000/yearcontinuouslyforever.Discountrater = 4%$. What endowment size is needed?

PV = \frac{f}{r} = \frac{80000}{0.04} = \boxed{$2{,}000{,}000}

The integral ∫0∞​80000,e−0.04t,dt converges because the discount factor e−0.04t decays faster than the constant income grows — a beautiful consequence of the convergence of improper integrals.

3. Total Revenue from Marginal Revenue

Theoretical Foundation

In economics, marginal revenue MR(q) is the revenue from selling one additional unit: MR(q)=dqd​[TR(q)]=TR′(q)

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, total revenue is recovered by: TR(Q)=TR(0)+∫0Q​MR(q),dq=∫0Q​MR(q),dq​

(Since TR(0)=0 — zero units sold means zero revenue.)

Similarly, total cost from marginal cost: TC(Q)=FC+∫0Q​MC(q),dq

And profit: π(Q)=∫0Q​[MR(q)−MC(q)],dq−FC

Profit is maximized where MR=MC — this is where the integrand changes sign.

Example 3.1 — Monopolist Pricing

Setup: A monopolist faces demand p(q)=100−q, so TR=pq=(100−q)q=100q−q2 and: MR(q)=100−2q MC(q)=10+q,FC=200

Profit-maximizing output: MR=MC⟹100−2q=10+q⟹90=3q⟹q∗=30

Profit via integration: π=∫030​(MR−MC),dq−FC=∫030​(90−3q),dq−200 =[90q−23q2​]030​−200=(2700−1350)−200=1350−200=1150​

Cross-check: TR(30)=100(30)−302=2100. TC(30)=200+∫030​(10+q),dq=200+[10q+q2/2]030​=200+300+450=950. π=2100−950=1150. ✓

4. Lorenz Curve & Gini Coefficient (Income Inequality)

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §6.1 application)

Lorenz Curve: L(x) represents the fraction of total income earned by the bottom x fraction of the population. Properties:

  • L(0)=0, L(1)=1
  • L(x)≤x (lower incomes earn disproportionately less)
  • L is convex and increasing

Perfect equality: L(x)=x (everyone earns proportionally).

Gini Coefficient: G=2∫01​[x−L(x)],dx​

This measures the area between the line of perfect equality and the Lorenz curve, normalized to [0,1]. G=0 is perfect equality; G=1 is perfect inequality.

Derivation: The area between y=x and y=L(x) is ∫01​(x−L(x)),dx. The maximum possible area (perfect inequality: L(x)=0 for x<1) is 21​. So G=1/2∫01​(x−L(x)),dx​=2∫01​(x−L(x)),dx. ■

Example 4.1 — Pareto Distribution Lorenz Curve

The Pareto distribution (power law) models wealth concentration. Its Lorenz curve: L(x)=1−(1−x)1−1/α⋅...​

For simplicity, use: L(x)=x2 (a common textbook model).

Gini coefficient: G=2∫01​[x−x2],dx=2[2x2​−3x3​]01​=2[21​−31​]=2⋅61​=31​≈0.333​

Example 4.2 — Empirical Lorenz Curve

Setup: A country’s income data gives the Lorenz curve: L(x)=e2−1e2x−1​

Verify: L(0)=0, L(1)=1. ✓ L′(x)=e2−12e2x​>0. ✓ L′′(x)=e2−14e2x​>0 (convex). ✓

Gini: G=2∫01​[x−e2−1e2x−1​]dx =2[2x2​−2(e2−1)e2x​+e2−1x​]01​ =2[21​−2(e2−1)e2​+e2−11​+2(e2−1)1​] =2[21​−2(e2−1)e2−1−1​]=2[21​−2(e2−1)e2−2​] =1−e2−1e2−2​=e2−1e2−1−e2+2​=e2−11​≈0.156​

This is a relatively equal distribution (low Gini). For comparison, Bangladesh’s Gini ≈ 0.33, USA ≈ 0.41, South Africa ≈ 0.63.

5. Capital Accumulation & Investment

Theoretical Foundation

If I(t) is the rate of investment (capital added per unit time), then capital stock at time T: K(T)=K(0)+∫0T​I(t),dt​

This is the FTC in action: investment is the derivative of capital, so integrating investment recovers capital. This is identical in structure to ∫I,dt=q (charge from current) in the physics note.

Depreciation: If capital depreciates at rate δ, net capital accumulation: dtdK​=I(t)−δK(t) This is a first-order linear ODE, solved by the integrating factor method — but the total investment ∫0T​I(t),dt is still the core integral.

Example 5.1 — Capital Stock Over a Decade

Setup: A firm’s investment rate follows a business cycle: I(t)=5000+2000sin(5πt​) million BDT/year. Initial capital K(0)=20,000 million BDT. Find K(10).

K(10)=20000+∫010​(5000+2000sin5πt​)dt =20000+[5000t−π10000​cos5πt​]010​ =20000+[(50000−π10000​cos2π)−(0−π10000​)] =20000+50000−π10000​+π10000​=70,000 million BDT​

Observation: The sine investment cycle (booms and busts) contributes zero net capital over a full 10-year period — the integral of a sine over complete periods is zero. The average rate 5000/yr ×10 yr =50,000 is all that matters.

6. Population Growth — Logistic Model

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §9.4)

Malthusian (exponential) growth: dtdN​=rN

Solution: N(t)=N0​ert, which gives N(t)=N0​+∫0t​rN(τ),dτ — a self-referential integral equation.

Logistic growth: Carrying capacity K limits growth: dtdN​=rN(1−KN​)

Exact solution via separation of variables:

∫N(1−N/K)dN​=∫r,dt

Partial fractions: N(1−N/K)1​=N1​+1−N/K1/K​

∫(N1​+1−N/K1/K​)dN=rt+C ln∣N∣−ln∣1−N/K∣=rt+C ln​1−N/KN​​=rt+C

Solving for N: N(t)=1+(N0​K−N0​​)e−rtK​​

Total growth from t=0 to t=T: ΔN=∫0T​rN(1−KN​)dt which equals N(T)−N(0) by the FTC — a useful consistency check.

Example 6.1 — Fish Population in a Lake

Setup: A lake initially has N0​=500 fish. The carrying capacity is K=10,000 and growth rate r=0.3/year. When does the population reach 5,000?

Using logistic formula: 5000=1+19,e−0.3t10000​ 1+19e−0.3t=2⟹19e−0.3t=1⟹e−0.3t=191​ t=0.3ln19​=0.32.944​≈9.81 years​

Total fish-years of stock from t=0 to t=10 (important for fishery management): ∫010​N(t),dt=∫010​1+19,e−0.3t10000​,dt

Let u=1+19e−0.3t, du=−5.7e−0.3t,dt:

=10000∫u(0)u(10)​u1​⋅−5.7⋅19u−1​du​=5.710000⋅19​∫201+19e−3​u(u−1)−du​

This becomes a partial fraction integral. Numerically: ≈46,500 fish-years.

Ecological significance: Integrating N(t) over time gives the total biological “resource consumption” or sustainable harvest potential.

7. Drug Concentration — Pharmacokinetics

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §8.5 type)

One-compartment model: A drug is administered and eliminated from the body. Concentration C(t) in blood plasma:

  • IV bolus: C(t)=C0​e−kt (first-order elimination)
  • Oral/continuous infusion at rate R:

dtdC​=R−kC⟹C(t)=kR​(1−e−kt)

Key pharmacokinetic integral — Area Under the Curve (AUC):

AUC=∫0∞​C(t),dt​

Why AUC matters: By the bioavailability theorem, total drug exposure = total amount absorbed / clearance. AUC is proportional to the total amount of drug that reaches systemic circulation. It determines both efficacy and toxicity.

Example 7.1 — Intravenous Bolus

Setup: A patient receives a 500 mg IV dose. The elimination constant k=0.2 hr⁻¹ and volume of distribution Vd​=20 L. Thus C0​=500/20=25 mg/L.

C(t)=25e−0.2t mg/L

AUC: AUC=∫0∞​25e−0.2t,dt=25⋅[−0.2e−0.2t​]0∞​=0.225​=125 mg\cdotphr/L​

Half-life: t1/2​=kln2​=0.20.693​≈3.47 hr.

Concentration at 8 hours: C(8)=25e−1.6≈5.03 mg/L.

Drug remaining in body at t=8: Vd​⋅C(8)=20×5.03≈100.6 mg.

Cumulative amount eliminated by t=8: Eliminated=Vd​∫08​kC(t),dt=Vd​⋅k⋅kC0​​(1−e−0.2⋅8)=500(1−e−1.6)≈399.4 mg ✓ (Total: 399.4+100.6=500 mg.)

Example 7.2 — Repeated Dosing & Steady State

Setup: A drug is given as IV bolus dose D=100 mg every τ=6 hours. Elimination k=0.3/hr. Find the steady-state peak concentration.

After n doses, the trough concentration just before the (n+1)th dose accumulates geometrically. At steady state (infinite doses):

Cmin,ss​=1−e−kτC0​e−kτ​=1−e−1.85⋅e−1.8​

e−1.8≈0.1653, C0​=D/Vd​. With Vd​=20 L: C0​=5 mg/L.

Cmin,ss​=1−0.16535×0.1653​=0.83470.827​≈0.99 mg/L Cmax,ss​=Cmin,ss​+C0​=0.99+5=5.99 mg/L​

AUC at steady state (per dosing interval, using integration): AUCss​=∫0τ​Css​(t),dt=∫06​Cmax,ss​e−0.3t,dt=kCmax,ss​​(1−e−kτ) =0.35.99​(1−0.1653)≈19.97×0.8347≈16.67 mg\cdotphr/L​

8. Cardiac Output — Dye Dilution Method

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §8.5)

Clinical problem: How do we measure cardiac output (volume of blood pumped per minute) without invasive surgery?

Stewart-Hamilton Principle: Inject a known quantity A mg of dye into a vein. Measure its concentration c(t) at the aorta. Then:

F=∫0∞​c(t),dtA​​

where F = cardiac output (L/min).

Derivation: In a small time interval [t,t+dt], the volume of blood passing the measurement point is F,dt, carrying dye amount c(t)⋅F,dt. Accumulating all dye: ∫0∞​c(t)F,dt=A (conservation of mass). Solve for F. ■

Example 8.1 — Dye Dilution Calculation

Setup: 5 mg of dye injected. Concentration measurements (mg/L) at the aorta:

t (min)012345678
c(t)00.41.84.03.92.81.60.70.1

Approximate ∫08​c(t),dt using Simpson’s Rule (h=1, n=8): ∫≈31​[c0​+4c1​+2c2​+4c3​+2c4​+4c5​+2c6​+4c7​+c8​] =31​[0+1.6+3.6+16.0+7.8+11.2+3.2+2.8+0.1] =31​(46.3)≈15.43 mg\cdotpmin/L

Cardiac output: F=15.435​≈0.324 L/min​≈19.4 L/hr

Note: Normal cardiac output is ~5 L/min at rest; this example uses small numbers for clarity.

Clinical note: This formula is used in hospitals today. The integral cannot be computed analytically from real data — numerical integration (Simpson’s Rule) is essential. This is a direct application of numerical quadrature.

9. Survival & Hazard Functions

Theoretical Foundation

Survival function S(t)=P(T>t): probability that a subject survives past time t.

Hazard function λ(t): instantaneous risk of death at time t given survival to t: λ(t)=−S(t)S′(t)​=−dtd​lnS(t)

Key relationship (integrating the hazard): S(t)=e−∫0t​λ(u),du=e−Λ(t)​

where Λ(t)=∫0t​λ(u),du is the cumulative hazard function.

PDF of failure time: f(t)=−S′(t)=λ(t),S(t)=λ(t),e−Λ(t)

Mean lifetime (expected survival time): E[T]=∫0∞​S(t),dt

Example 9.1 — Weibull Hazard Model

Setup: The Weibull distribution has hazard λ(t)=ηβ​(ηt​)β−1. With β=2 (increasing hazard — aging), η=5 years.

Cumulative hazard: Λ(t)=∫0t​η2​⋅ηu​,du=∫0t​η22u​,du=η2t2​=25t2​

Survival function: S(t)=e−t2/25

Probability of surviving past 3 years: S(3)=e−9/25=e−0.36≈0.698

Mean survival time: E[T]=∫0∞​e−t2/25,dt=∫0∞​e−t2/25,dt

Let u=t/5: $= 5\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}, du = 5 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} = \frac{5\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \approx \boxed{4.43 \text{ years}}$$

The Gaussian integral appears again — this time computing the mean lifetime of a Weibull-distributed survival model. The connection to §9 of the physics/ML note is deep: the same ∫0∞​e−u2,du=2π​​ underlies both the normal distribution and the Weibull mean.

Example 9.2 — Median vs Mean Survival (Cancer Study)

For exponential survival S(t)=e−λt:

Mean: E[T]=∫0∞​e−λt,dt=λ1​

Median t0.5​: S(t0.5​)=0.5⟹e−λt0.5​=0.5⟹t0.5​=λln2​

Ratio: E[T]t0.5​​=ln2≈0.693

This explains why clinical trials often report median survival (robust to outliers) rather than mean — but the mean requires the integral ∫0∞​S(t),dt, which clinical statisticians compute via numerical integration from Kaplan-Meier curves.

10. Spread of Disease — The SIR Model

Theoretical Foundation

The SIR model (Kermack-McKendrick 1927) partitions a population N into:

  • S(t): Susceptible
  • I(t): Infectious
  • R(t): Recovered (immune)

ODEs: dtdS​=−βSI,dtdI​=βSI−γI,dtdR​=γI

Conservation: S+I+R=N (constant).

Total infected over the entire epidemic: R(∞)=γ∫0∞​I(t),dt

Final size equation (derived by integrating the SIR equations, dividing, and using separation of variables):

lnS(∞)S(0)​=γβ​(S(0)+I(0)−S(∞))=R0​⋅NS(0)−S(∞)​

where R0​=γβN​ is the basic reproduction number.

Example 10.1 — COVID-style Outbreak

Setup: A university of N=5000 students. Initially I0​=5, S0​=4995. Parameters: β=0.0003/person/day, γ=0.1/day.

R0​=γβN​=0.10.0003×5000​=15

Epidemic will occur since R0​>1.

Peak infection time (when dI/dt=0, i.e., βS=γ): S∗=βγ​=0.00030.1​=333⟹peak occurs when S drops to 333

Total fraction eventually infected r∞​=1−S(∞)/N, solving: lnS∞​S0​​=R0​(1−NS∞​​)≈R0​r∞​

For R0​=15: r∞​≈0.9999 (essentially the entire population). This is consistent with measles-like dynamics.

Total person-days of infection (cumulative burden, key for healthcare planning): Burden=∫0∞​I(t),dt=γR(∞)​=γNr∞​​≈0.14998​≈49,980 person-days

This integral is the fundamental healthcare capacity metric. Hospital planning requires knowing not just the peak I(t) but the area under the I(t) curve — the total patient-days. “Flattening the curve” means reducing the maximum of I(t) while the integral (total infections) may stay similar.

Example 10.2 — Herd Immunity Threshold via Integration

The epidemic stops growing when dI/dt≤0, i.e., S≤γ/β=N/R0​.

Herd immunity threshold (fraction that must be immune): pc​=1−R0​1​

For R0​=15: pc​=1−1/15=14/15≈93.3.

Integral verification: The integral ∫0∞​dtdS​,dt=S(∞)−S(0) gives the total reduction in susceptibles, which must equal total infections. This is the FTC applied to population dynamics: accumulating the flow dS/dt recovers the stock S(t).

ML & AI Connections

The same integrals from economics and biology recur directly in modern machine learning.

ML.1 — Survival Analysis & Deep Learning (Connection to §9)

Cox Proportional Hazards extended to neural networks (DeepSurv, 2018):

λ(t∣x)=λ0​(t)⋅efθ​(x)

where fθ​ is a deep neural network. The partial likelihood involves:

L(θ)=∏i:,δi​=1​∑j∈R(ti​)​efθ​(xj​)efθ​(xi​)​

The baseline survival S0​(t)=e−∫0t​λ0​(u),du is computed via numerical integration (Breslow estimator). The entire architecture depends on the cumulative hazard integral from §9.

ML.2 — Expected Reward in Reinforcement Learning (Connection to §2)

The discounted return in RL is: Gt​=∑k=0∞​γkrt+k​≈∫0∞​r(t+τ)e−λτ,dτ

This is exactly the present value formula (§2): PV=∫0T​f(t),e−rt,dt

with discount factor e−λτ playing the role of e−rt. The Bellman equation is the discrete version of the PV integral. Policy gradient methods optimize this integral.

ML.3 — Epidemic Modeling → Graph Neural Networks

The SIR integral ∫0∞​I(t),dt is approximated in modern epidemic GNNs (e.g., EpiGNN, 2021) which learn β and γ from data. The ODE constraints: S(T)=S(0)−β∫0T​S(t)I(t),dt

become physics-informed loss terms. This is neural ODE meets epidemiology — exactly the connection from the physics note’s §10.7 extended to the SIR system.

ML.4 — AUC as a Classification Metric (Connection to §7)

In machine learning, AUC-ROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) is: AUC=∫01​TPR(FPR−1(t)),dt=P(p^​(x+)>p^​(x−))

This is a direct integral — the area under the ROC curve. It measures the probability that the model ranks a random positive example higher than a random negative one. Just as pharmacokinetic AUC (∫C(t),dt) measures total drug exposure, ML AUC measures total discriminative ability.

ML.5 — Gini Impurity & Decision Trees (Connection to §4)

The Gini impurity used in decision tree splits: Gnode​=1−∑k​pk2​

is the discrete analogue of the Gini coefficient from §4. The Lorenz curve integral G=2∫01​(x−L(x)),dx measures inequality in income; Gini impurity measures “inequality” (mixing) of class labels at a node. Random Forests and XGBoost both use this integral-derived concept at every split.

ML.6 — Gaussian Processes & Integral Kernels

A Gaussian Process (GP) defines a distribution over functions via a covariance kernel k(x,x′). The posterior mean is: μ∗(x)=k(x,X)[K+σ2I]−1y

The RBF kernel k(x,x′)=e−∣x−x′∣2/2ℓ2 is the continuous generalization of the inner product. Its normalization constant ∫−∞∞​e−x2/2ℓ2,dx=ℓ2π​ comes directly from the Gaussian integral (§9.1 of the physics note). GPs underlie Bayesian optimization, which is used for hyperparameter tuning in deep learning.

Theorems That Recur Constantly

FTC (Stock-Flow duality): ∫0T​f′(t),dt=f(T)−f(0)
Investment → Capital; Current → Charge; dtdN​ → N(t); dtdR​ → R(t)

Separation of Variables: ∫g(N)dN​=∫f(t),dt — the engine of logistic growth, SIR analysis, pharmacokinetics.

Improper Integrals: ∫0∞​e−λt,dt=λ1​ — appears in present value, AUC, mean survival, pharmacokinetics. All require convergence of e−λt as t→∞.

Gaussian Integral: ∫0∞​e−u2,du=2π​​ — Weibull mean lifetime, all Gaussian models in ML.
