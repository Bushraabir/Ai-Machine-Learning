Prelude — Why Integration Is the Language of Accumulation

The central philosophical move of integral calculus is this:

A complicated whole = infinitely many simple pieces, summed.

Whenever a physical quantity varies continuously — force, pressure, density, voltage — you cannot use a single formula. You must accumulate. The integral is the rigorous formalization of that accumulation.

The Master Template (Stewart §5.2, MIT 18.02 L16):

Q=∫ab​f(x),dx=n→∞lim​i=1∑n​f(xi∗​),Δx​

Every application below is a specialization of this template. The art lies in correctly identifying f(x) and the appropriate measure dx (or dA, dV, ds…).

1. Work Done by a Variable Force

Theoretical Foundation

Newton’s definition of work for a constant force F over displacement d is W=Fd. This immediately fails when F varies.

Theorem (Stewart §6.4): If a force F(x) acts on an object along the x-axis from x=a to x=b, then the work done is

W=∫ab​F(x),dx​

Proof sketch: Partition [a,b] into n subintervals of width Δx. On each subinterval [xi−1​,xi​], approximate F as constant \approx F(x_i^_). Work done on piece i: \Delta W_i \approx F(x_i^_)\Delta x. Total: W≈∑i=1n​F(xi∗​)Δx. Taking n→∞ recovers the integral by definition of the Riemann integral. ■

Example 1.1 — Spring (Hooke’s Law)

Setup: A spring has natural length 1 m. It requires 40 N to stretch it 0.1 m beyond natural length. How much work is done stretching it from natural length to 0.4 m beyond?

Hooke’s Law: F(x)=kx, where x is displacement from natural length.

Step 1 — Find k: F(0.1)=40⟹k(0.1)=40⟹k=400 N/m

Step 2 — Integrate: W=∫00.4​400x,dx=400⋅2x2​​00.4​=200(0.16)=32 J​

Sanity check: The force at x=0.4 is F(0.4)=160 N. The average force is 20+160​=80 N. Work =80×0.4=32 J. ✓ (Linear force ⟹ average works here.)

Example 1.2 — Lifting a Leaky Bucket (Non-Constant Mass)

Setup: A 10 kg bucket is pulled up a 20 m shaft. Water leaks at a constant rate; the bucket starts with 30 kg of water and is empty at the top. What is the total work done against gravity?

Model: At height x meters, fraction of rope remaining = x/20… rather, fraction of rope pulled = x/20. Water mass remaining: mw​(x)=30(1−20x​)=30−23x​.

Total mass at height x: m(x)=10+30−23x​=40−23x​kg

Force (weight): F(x)=m(x)⋅g=(40−23x​)(9.8) N

Work: W=∫020​(40−23x​)(9.8),dx =9.8∫020​(40−23x​)dx=9.8[40x−43x2​]020​ =9.8[800−43(400)​]=9.8[800−300]=9.8×500=4900 J​

Annotation: Notice how the integral automatically handles the variable mass — no clever tricks, just correct modeling and accumulation.

2. Center of Mass & Moments

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §8.3, MIT 18.02 L20)

Definition: For a lamina (thin plate) with density ρ(x,y) over region R, the center of mass (xˉ,yˉ​) is

xˉ=mMy​​=∬R​ρ(x,y),dA∬R​x,ρ(x,y),dA​,yˉ​=mMx​​=∬R​ρ(x,y),dA∬R​y,ρ(x,y),dA​

For a 1D rod of length L with linear density ρ(x):

m=∫0L​ρ(x),dx,xˉ=∫0L​ρ(x),dx∫0L​x,ρ(x),dx​

Why this formula? The moment My​=∫x,dm measures the “turning tendency” about the y-axis, weighted by distance. The center of mass is where a single pivot would balance the distribution.

Example 2.1 — Non-Uniform Rod

Setup: A rod of length 4 m has linear density ρ(x)=2+sin(πx/4) kg/m. Find its center of mass.

Total mass: m=∫04​(2+sin4πx​)dx=[2x−π4​cos4πx​]04​ =[8−π4​cosπ]−[0−π4​cos0]=8+π4​+π4​=8+π8​≈10.546 kg

First moment: My​=∫04​x(2+sin4πx​)dx

Split: ∫04​2x,dx+∫04​xsin4πx​,dx

∫04​2x,dx=[x2]04​=16

For ∫04​xsin4πx​,dx, integrate by parts: u=x, dv=sin4πx​dx: =[−π4x​cos4πx​]04​+∫04​π4​cos4πx​,dx =[−π16​(−1)−0]+π4​[π4​sin4πx​]04​ =π16​+π216​[sinπ−sin0]=π16​

So My​=16+π16​.

Center of mass: xˉ=8+π8​16+π16​​=8(1+π1​)16(1+π1​)​=2 m​

Beautiful result: xˉ=2 m, the geometric midpoint! The sine density is symmetric about x=2, so the center of mass lands at the center. The integral “saw” this symmetry automatically.

Example 2.2 — 2D Lamina with Variable Density

Setup: A triangular lamina with vertices (0,0), (2,0), (0,3) has density ρ(x,y)=xy. Find (xˉ,yˉ​).

Region: 0≤x≤2, 0≤y≤3−23x​ (below the line 2x​+3y​=1).

Mass: m=∫02​∫03−3x/2​xy,dy,dx=∫02​x⋅2y2​​03−3x/2​dx =∫02​2x​(3−23x​)2dx=∫02​2x​⋅9(1−2x​)2dx =29​∫02​x(1−x+4x2​)dx=29​∫02​(x−x2+4x3​)dx =29​[2−38​+1]=29​⋅31​=23​

My​ (for xˉ): My​=∫02​∫03−3x/2​x2y,dy,dx=29​∫02​x2(1−2x​)2dx=29​⋅154​=56​

xˉ=mMy​​=3/26/5​=54​=0.8

(Finding yˉ​ analogously gives yˉ​=56​.)

(xˉ,yˉ​)=(0.8, 1.2)​

3. Fluid Pressure & Hydrostatic Force

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §8.3)

Pascal’s Principle: Pressure at depth d in a fluid of density ρ is P=ρgd.

The problem: find the total force on a submerged vertical plate. Since pressure varies with depth, we integrate.

Setup: Plate submerged with its top at depth a and bottom at depth b. At depth x, the plate has horizontal width w(x).

F=∫ab​ρgx⋅w(x),dx​

Derivation: Strip at depth x of height dx has area dA=w(x),dx. Pressure on strip: P(x)=ρgx. Force on strip: dF=P(x),dA=ρgx⋅w(x),dx. Integrate. ■

Example 3.1 — Trapezoidal Dam Face

Setup: A trapezoidal dam face has its top edge 4 m wide at water surface and bottom edge 2 m wide at depth 3 m. Water density ρ=1000 kg/m³, g=9.8 m/s². Find the total hydrostatic force.

Width function: At depth x, the width decreases linearly from 4 to 2 as x goes from 0 to 3: w(x)=4−32x​

Force: F=∫03​ρgx⋅w(x),dx=1000(9.8)∫03​x(4−32x​)dx =9800∫03​(4x−32x2​)dx=9800[2x2−92x3​]03​ =9800[18−6]=9800×12=117,600 N≈117.6 kN​

Engineering relevance: Dam engineers must ensure structural supports can withstand this force. The integral gives the exact force accounting for how both pressure and width change with depth.

Example 3.2 — Circular Porthole

Setup: A circular porthole of radius 1 m is centered at depth 5 m on a submarine hull. Find the net hydrostatic force on the porthole.

Setup coordinates: Center at depth 5. Use y measured from center: depth at height y from center is 5−y. Width at height y: w(y)=21−y2​.

F=ρg∫−11​(5−y)⋅21−y2​,dy

Split: =2ρg[5∫−11​1−y2​,dy−∫−11​y1−y2​,dy]

First integral = area of semicircle = 2π​. Second integral = 0 (odd function on symmetric interval).

F=2ρg⋅5⋅2π​=5πρg=5π(1000)(9.8)≈153,938 N​

Note: The 5 meters of depth contributes; the circular shape contributes πr2 area. The depth of the centroid times ρg times area gives the same answer — this is the centroid shortcut, but only valid when you already know the result! The integral is the derivation.

4. Arc Length & Surface Area of Revolution

Theoretical Foundation (Stewart §8.1–8.2)

Arc Length Formula:

L=∫ab​1+(dxdy​)2​,dx​

Derivation: For a curve y=f(x), partition [a,b]. Each piece has length ≈(Δx)2+(Δy)2​=Δx1+(ΔxΔy​)2​. Taking the limit as partition is refined gives the formula. ■

Surface Area of Revolution (rotating y=f(x) about x-axis):

S=∫ab​2πf(x)1+[f′(x)]2​,dx​

Each infinitesimal strip of arc length ds=1+[f′(x)]2​,dx traces a band of area 2πf(x),ds.

Example 4.1 — Arc Length of a Parabolic Cable

Setup: A suspension bridge cable follows y=x2 from x=0 to x=2 (units in km). Find its length.

y′=2x⟹L=∫02​1+4x2​,dx

Trigonometric substitution: 2x=tanθ, dx=21​sec2θ,dθ, 1+4x2​=secθ.

L=∫0arctan4​secθ⋅21​sec2θ,dθ=21​∫sec3θ,dθ

Using the reduction formula ∫sec3θ,dθ=21​[secθtanθ+ln∣secθ+tanθ∣]:

L=41​[secθtanθ+ln∣secθ+tanθ∣]0arctan4​

At θ=arctan4: tanθ=4, secθ=17​.

L=41​[417​+ln(4+17​)]≈41​[16.49+ln(8.123)]≈41​[16.49+2.094]≈4.65 km​

Example 4.2 — Surface Area of a Cooling Tower

Setup: A cooling tower profile is y=cosh(x/a) rotated about the y-axis. For a=1, from x=0 to x=1, find the surface area.

y′=sinhx⟹S=∫01​2πx1+sinh2x​,dx=2π∫01​xcoshx,dx

(Using cosh2x−sinh2x=1⟹1+sinh2x​=coshx.)

Integration by parts: u=x, dv=coshx,dx: =2π[xsinhx−coshx]01​=2π[sinh1−cosh1+1] =2π[1.1752−1.5431+1]=2π(0.632)≈3.97 m2​

5. Electric Charge & Current

Theoretical Foundation

Current I(t) is the rate of charge flow: I=dq/dt. To find total charge from current:

q=∫t1​t2​​I(t),dt​

Energy delivered to a resistor: E=∫t1​t2​​P(t),dt=∫t1​t2​​V(t)I(t),dt​

Electric potential (work per unit charge) from field E: V(b)−V(a)=−∫ab​E⋅dr

Example 5.1 — Capacitor Charging

Setup: A capacitor charges with current I(t)=2e−t/3 A. How much charge accumulates in the first 6 seconds?

q=∫06​2e−t/3,dt=2[−3e−t/3]06​=−6[e−2−1]=6(1−e−2) ≈6(1−0.1353)≈5.17 C​

Interpretation: The current is large at t=0 and decays exponentially as the capacitor fills. The integral accumulates this decaying flow.

Example 5.2 — RMS Voltage

Setup: AC voltage V(t)=V0​sin(ωt). Find the root-mean-square voltage over one period T=2π/ω.

Vrms​=T1​∫0T​V02​sin2(ωt),dt​

T1​∫0T​sin2(ωt),dt=T1​∫0T​21−cos(2ωt)​,dt=21​

(The cosine term integrates to zero over a full period.)

Vrms​=V0​21​​=2​V0​​​

This is why household 230 V (in Bangladesh/Europe) means V0​=2302​≈325 V peak amplitude.

6. Gravitational Potential Energy

Theoretical Foundation (MIT 18.02 L22)

The gravitational force between two masses M and m separated by r is: F(r)=r2GMm​

Work done lifting a mass from r=R (Earth’s surface) to r=∞:

W=∫R∞​r2GMm​,dr=GMm[−r1​]R∞​=RGMm​

This is the escape energy — the minimum energy required.

Example 6.1 — Escape Velocity

Setup: Find the minimum launch speed from Earth’s surface (R=6.371×106 m, M=5.972×1024 kg).

Set kinetic energy = potential energy: 21​mve2​=RGMm​⟹ve​=R2GM​​

ve​=6.371×1062(6.674×10−11)(5.972×1024)​​=6.371×1067.972×1014​​=1.251×108​≈11.18 km/s​

The integral’s role: The work integral from R to ∞ correctly accounts for the decreasing gravitational force — something a simple F×d calculation cannot do.

7. Moment of Inertia (Rotational Kinetic Energy)

Theoretical Foundation (MIT 18.02 L21)

Moment of inertia I measures resistance to rotational acceleration:

I=∫r2,dm​

where r is the perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation.

Kinetic energy of rotation: KE=21​Iω2.

Example 7.1 — Solid Cylinder

Setup: A solid cylinder of mass M, radius R, and height h rotates about its central axis. Find I.

Setup: Use cylindrical coordinates. A thin cylindrical shell at radius r, thickness dr: dm=ρ⋅2πrh,dr,ρ=πR2hM​

I=∫0R​r2,dm=∫0R​r2⋅πR2hM​⋅2πrh,dr=R22M​∫0R​r3,dr=R22M​⋅4R4​=2MR2​​

Application: A flywheel engine stores energy E=21​Iω2=4MR2ω2​. Increasing R is more efficient than increasing ω for energy storage.

Example 7.2 — Thin Rod About End vs. Center

For a uniform rod of mass M, length L, density λ=M/L:

About center: Ic​=∫−L/2L/2​x2λ,dx=λ⋅3x3​​−L/2L/2​=λ⋅12L3​=12ML2​​

About end: Ie​=∫0L​x2λ,dx=λ⋅3L3​=3ML2​​

Parallel Axis Theorem check: Ie​=Ic​+M(L/2)2=12ML2​+4ML2​=3ML2​. ✓

8. Signal Processing — Convolution

Theoretical Foundation

Convolution is one of the most powerful integral operations in engineering:

(f∗g)(t)=∫−∞∞​f(τ),g(t−τ),dτ​

Intuition: At time t, the output is the weighted sum of all past inputs f(τ), weighted by the impulse response g(t−τ).

Applications:

  • Signal filtering (audio, image)
  • System response (input ∗ impulse response = output)
  • Probability (sum of random variables)

Example 8.1 — RC Circuit Response

Setup: An RC circuit has impulse response g(t)=RC1​e−t/RC⋅1t≥0​. Input: rectangular pulse f(t)=10≤t≤T​. Find output y(t) for 0<t<T.

y(t)=∫0t​f(τ)g(t−τ),dτ=∫0t​RC1​e−(t−τ)/RC,dτ =RCe−t/RC​∫0t​eτ/RC,dτ=RCe−t/RC​⋅RC[eτ/RC]0t​ =e−t/RC(et/RC−1)=1−e−t/RC​

The output rises toward 1, the classic capacitor charging curve — derived directly from the convolution integral.

9. Probability & Statistics (Bridge to ML)

Theoretical Foundation

For a continuous random variable X with probability density function (PDF) f(x):

P(a≤X≤b)=∫ab​f(x),dx,∫−∞∞​f(x),dx=1

Expected value: E[X]=∫−∞∞​x,f(x),dx

Variance: Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2=∫−∞∞​x2f(x),dx−(∫−∞∞​x,f(x),dx)2

Example 9.1 — Gaussian (Normal) Distribution

PDF: f(x)=σ2π​1​e−(x−μ)2/(2σ2)

Verify it integrates to 1 (the Gaussian integral):

∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx=2π​

Proof using polar coordinates (MIT 18.02 L25 style):

Let I=∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx. Then: I2=∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​e−(x2+y2)/2,dx,dy

Convert to polar: x2+y2=r2, dA=r,dr,dθ: I2=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2/2r,dr,dθ=2π∫0∞​re−r2/2,dr

Substitution u=r2/2: =2π∫0∞​e−u,du=2π

∴I=2π​. ■

This double integral in polar coordinates is the engine of all probability theory for Gaussian distributions — and Gaussians are everywhere in ML.

10. Machine Learning & Deep Learning — Where It All Lives

Every topic above has a direct descendant in modern AI. Here we make the connections explicit.

10.1 Gradient Flow & Backpropagation via the Fundamental Theorem

Connection to Work integrals. In optimization, the loss landscape L(θ) is a surface. The gradient flow equation: dtdθ​=−∇θ​L(θ)

describes a “particle” rolling downhill. The total loss change along a path γ from θ0​ to θT​ is: ΔL=∫0T​∇θ​L⋅dtdθ​,dt=−∫0T​∣∇θ​L∣2dt

This is directly analogous to work done by a force along a path (§1). The gradient is the force; the parameter update is the displacement.

10.2 Convolution in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)

This is §8 directly. A 2D CNN layer computes:

(I∗K)(i,j)=∑m​∑n​I(i−m,j−n),K(m,n)

This is the discrete analogue of the continuous convolution integral: (f∗g)(t)=∫f(τ)g(t−τ),dτ

In a CNN:

  • I = input image (or feature map)
  • K = learned kernel (analogous to impulse response g)
  • Output = filtered/transformed version of input

Why does it work? Convolution is translation-equivariant: shifting the input shifts the output by the same amount. This is a direct consequence of the integral’s structure: ∫f(τ−a)g(t−τ),dτ=(f∗g)(t−a).

10.3 Expected Value & Loss Functions

Connection to §9. The empirical risk minimization principle:

L(θ)=E(x,y)∼D​[ℓ(fθ​(x),y)]=∫ℓ(fθ​(x),y),p(x,y),dx,dy

The true loss is an integral over the data distribution p(x,y). We cannot compute it exactly (we don’t know p), so we approximate:

L^(θ)=n1​∑i=1n​ℓ(fθ​(xi​),yi​)≈∫ℓ(fθ​(x),y),p(x,y),dx,dy

This is a Monte Carlo approximation to an integral — stochastic gradient descent is fundamentally a method for minimizing an integral you can’t compute in closed form.

10.4 Kullback-Leibler Divergence

The KL divergence used in variational autoencoders (VAEs), diffusion models, and information theory:

DKL​(P∣Q)=∫−∞∞​p(x)logq(x)p(x)​,dx

This is an integral of probability densities (§9). For Gaussians P=N(μ1​,σ12​), Q=N(μ2​,σ22​):

DKL​(P∣Q)=logσ1​σ2​​+2σ22​σ12​+(μ1​−μ2​)2​−21​

Derived by directly evaluating the integral using properties of the Gaussian (completing the square, the Gaussian integral result from §9.1).

10.5 Attention Mechanism as Weighted Average

The self-attention in Transformers (GPT, BERT):

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QKT​)V

In the continuous limit, this is a weighted integral:

Attention(q)=∫∫eq⋅k(x′),dx′eq⋅k(x)​v(x),dx

This is directly the expected value formula (§9): output = E[v(x)] under the attention distribution softmax(⋅). The softmax is a normalized exponential measure, and the attention output is a weighted average — an integral.

10.6 Moment of Inertia → Batch Normalization

Connection to §7. The variance of activations: Var[x]=E[x2]−(E[x])2=∫x2p(x),dx−(∫x,p(x),dx)2

This is the second central moment — structurally identical to the moment of inertia formula. Batch Normalization normalizes by Var[x]+ϵ​, which is precisely dividing by the “radius of gyration” of the activation distribution.

10.7 Neural ODEs — The Continuous Limit of Deep Networks

Connection to all of the above. A residual network block: ht+1​=ht​+f(ht​,θt​). As the number of layers →∞ with smaller steps, this becomes the ODE:

dtdh​=f(h(t),θ(t)),h(T)=h(0)+∫0T​f(h(t),θ(t)),dt

Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) make this the architecture itself. The forward pass is an integral. Training requires differentiating through an ODE solver — which requires the adjoint method, itself a line integral in function space.
