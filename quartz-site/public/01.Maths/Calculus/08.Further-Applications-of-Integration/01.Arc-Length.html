01.Arc Length

01.Arc Length

Apr 18, 202613 min read

You’ve been computing areas since Stewart Chapter 5. Areas measure how much a function accumulates vertically. Arc length asks a different question entirely: how far does the curve actually travel? The answer requires a genuinely new idea — and it leads somewhere beautiful.

Tags

#calculus #arc-length #integration #differential-geometry #curves #AI-ML

Motivation: The Straight-Line Approximation

Take a curve y=f(x) on [a,b]. How long is it?

The instinct: break [a,b] into n sub-intervals, approximate the curve on each piece by a straight line segment, sum the lengths, take n→∞.

On the k-th sub-interval [xk−1​,xk​] with Δxk​=xk​−xk−1​, the straight-line approximation has length: ℓk​=(Δxk​)2+(Δyk​)2​ where Δyk​=f(xk​)−f(xk−1​).

Factor out Δxk​: ℓk​=1+(Δxk​Δyk​​)2​,Δxk​.

By the Mean Value Theorem, since f is differentiable on [xk−1​,xk​], there exists xk∗​∈(xk−1​,xk​) such that: Δxk​Δyk​​=f′(xk∗​).

So: ℓk​=1+[f′(xk∗​)]2​,Δxk​.

Summing and taking the limit: L=limn→∞​∑k=1n​1+[f′(xk∗​)]2​,Δxk​.

This is exactly a Riemann sum for 1+[f′(x)]2​.

The Arc Length Formula

L=∫ab​1+(dxdy​)2​,dx​

Hypotheses: f′ is continuous on [a,b] (i.e., f∈C1[a,b]). This guarantees the integrand is continuous and the Riemann sums converge.

The condition f∈C1 is not just a formality. If f′ has jump discontinuities, the MVT argument above fails — the curve may have corners, and arc length still exists but requires more care. Lipschitz functions have arc length even without C1; fractal curves like the Koch snowflake do not.

The Differential of Arc Length

The formula above has an elegant infinitesimal interpretation. Define the arc length element:

ds=dx2+dy2​=1+(dxdy​)2​,dx.

Think of ds as the infinitesimal hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs dx (horizontal) and dy=f′(x),dx (vertical):

         * (x + dx, y + dy)
        /|
   ds /  | dy = f'(x) dx
      /  |
     *___| 
(x,y)   dx

  ds² = dx² + dy²  →  ds = √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx

Then arc length is simply: L=∫ab​ds.

This notation ds is not merely decorative. It becomes the foundation of:

  • Line integrals in multivariable calculus (MIT 18.02): ∫C​f,ds
  • Riemannian geometry: ds2=gij​,dxi,dxj
  • Geodesics in curved spaces (the straight lines of general relativity and of certain ML models)

Arc Length for Parametric Curves

When the curve is given parametrically as x=x(t), y=y(t) for t∈[α,β]:

ds=(dtdx​)2+(dtdy​)2​,dt

L=∫αβ​(dtdx​)2+(dtdy​)2​,dt​

Derivation: On [tk−1​,tk​]: ℓk​=(Δx)2+(Δy)2​=(ΔtΔx​)2+(ΔtΔy​)2​,Δt→x˙2+y˙​2​,dt.

The parametric form is more general: it handles curves that are not graphs (vertical tangents, self-intersections, closed curves).

Example — Circle of radius r: x=rcost, y=rsint, t∈[0,2π]. L=∫02π​r2sin2t+r2cos2t​,dt=∫02π​r,dt=2πr.✓

Arc Length in Polar Coordinates

For r=f(θ), θ∈[α,β], using x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ:

dθdx​=dθdr​cosθ−rsinθ,dθdy​=dθdr​sinθ+rcosθ.

Computing x˙2+y˙​2: (dθdx​)2+(dθdy​)2=(dθdr​)2+r2.

L=∫αβ​r2+(dθdr​)2​,dθ​

Arc Length for Space Curves (3D)

For r(t)=⟨x(t),y(t),z(t)⟩, t∈[α,β]:

L=∫αβ​∣r′(t)∣,dt=∫αβ​x˙2+y˙​2+z˙2​,dt​

where ∣r′(t)∣=∣v(t)∣ is the speed of a particle moving along r. This is the central formula of MIT 18.02 — arc length in 3D is the integral of speed.

The Arc Length Function and Natural Parametrization

Definition. Fix a base point a on the curve. The arc length function is: s(t)=∫at​∣r′(u)∣,du.

By FTC: dtds​=∣r′(t)∣ (the speed).

Natural (arc-length) parametrization: Reparametrize the curve by s itself. If we write r as a function of s: ​dsdr​​=1. The tangent vector has unit length. This is the intrinsic way to parametrize a curve — independent of how fast you traverse it.

In natural parametrization, the curve reveals its geometry without the distortion of speed. The curvature κ=∣dT^/ds∣ is defined using arc-length parametrization. This is where calculus meets differential geometry.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Catenary: y=coshx on [0,a]

y′=sinhx,1+(y′)2=1+sinh2x=cosh2x. L=∫0a​coshx,dx=sinha.

The catenary is the shape a hanging cable takes under gravity. Its arc length is sinha — a beautiful closed form that would be inaccessible without recognizing the hyperbolic identity 1+sinh2=cosh2.

Example 2 — Parabola: y=x2 on [0,1]

y′=2x,L=∫01​1+4x2​,dx.

Substitute 2x=tanθ, dx=21​sec2θ,dθ: ∫1+tan2θ​⋅21​sec2θ,dθ=21​∫sec3θ,dθ.

The integral of sec3θ is a classic: ∫sec3θ,dθ=21​[secθtanθ+ln∣secθ+tanθ∣]+C.

Back-substituting: tanθ=2x, secθ=1+4x2​: L=21​[1x1+4x2​​+2ln∣2x+1+4x2​∣​]01​=25​​+4ln(2+5​)​≈1.4789.

Even a parabola produces a non-trivial computation. This is why arc length integrals almost always require either a clever substitution or numerical methods. The integrand 1+(f′)2​ is rarely elementary.

Example 3 — Spiral: r=θ on [0,2π] (Archimedean Spiral)

dθdr​=1,L=∫02π​θ2+1​,dθ.

This is the same type of integral as Example 2. The result is: L=21​[2π4π2+1​+ln(2π+4π2+1​)]≈21.26.

Example 4 — Helix: r(t)=⟨cost,sint,t⟩, t∈[0,4π]

r′(t)=⟨−sint,cost,1⟩,∣r′(t)∣=sin2t+cos2t+1​=2​. L=∫04π​2​,dt=4π2​.

The helix is special: constant speed ∣r′∣=2​ means it winds around the cylinder at a steady rate. The extra 2​ versus 4π (the circumference of 2 circles) accounts for the vertical climb.

Why Arc Length Integrals Are Usually Hard

The integrand 1+[f′(x)]2​ is almost never elementary. For it to produce a nice antiderivative, you typically need 1+[f′(x)]2 to be a perfect square — which happens only for special functions:

f(x)f′(x)1+(f′)2Integrable?
coshxsinhxcosh2x✓ Yes
2x2​−4lnx​x−4x1​(x+4x1​)2✓ Yes
x22x1+4x2✓ (trig sub, messy)
sinxcosx1+cos2x✗ (elliptic integral)
exex1+e2x✓ (substitution)

The arc length of y=sinx involves an elliptic integral — it cannot be expressed in elementary functions. This is not a failure of technique; it is a theorem of differential Galois theory.

This is precisely why numerical methods (Gaussian quadrature, adaptive Simpson) are used whenever arc length appears in applications.

Curvature (The Next Step)

Arc length is not the end — it is the gateway to curvature.

Definition. The curvature κ of a curve r(t) measures how fast the unit tangent vector T^ turns with respect to arc length: κ=​dsdT^​​=∣r′∣3∣r′×r′′∣​.

For y=f(x): κ=(1+(f′)2)3/2∣f′′(x)∣​.

The denominator (1+(f′)2)3/2 is the arc length element cubed — curvature is intrinsically tied to how arc length is measured. A circle of radius r has constant curvature κ=1/r.

The radius of curvature is R=1/κ — the radius of the “best-fit circle” at that point.

Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

Arc length and the related differential geometry machinery appear in ML in ways that are deep, not superficial.

1. Geodesics in Latent Space (VAEs and Riemannian ML)

A Variational Autoencoder encodes data into a latent space Z⊂Rd. The latent space is not flat in any meaningful probabilistic sense — regions with high data density are “closer together” than regions with low density.

Riemannian latent space methods (Arvanitidis et al., 2018) equip the latent space with a metric tensor G(z) derived from the decoder’s Jacobian: G(z)=Jf​(z)TJf​(z)+ϵI, where Jf​ is the Jacobian of the decoder f:Z→X.

The arc length of a curve γ(t) in latent space under this metric is: L[γ]=∫01​γ˙​(t)TG(γ(t)),γ˙​(t)​,dt. This is the Riemannian arc length — a direct generalization of ∫∣r′∣,dt where the inner product is twisted by G.

Geodesics (shortest paths under this metric) are found by minimizing L[γ] over all curves connecting two latent points. Interpolating between two data points along a geodesic produces smoother, more realistic interpolations than straight lines in latent space.

2. Neural ODEs and Curve Length in State Space

A Neural ODE defines a trajectory h(t) satisfying h˙=fθ​(h,t). The arc length of this trajectory in state space is: L=∫t0​t1​​∣fθ​(h(t),t)∣,dt, which is just ∫∣h˙∣,dt — the integral of speed. This quantity is directly related to the computational cost of the ODE solver (the solver must take small steps where the trajectory curves sharply). Regularizing the arc length (penalizing it in the loss) encourages the neural ODE to follow straighter paths, which reduces the number of function evaluations at inference time. The FFJORD model and regularized neural ODEs (Finlay et al., 2020) explicitly minimize trajectory arc length as part of training.

3. Fisher Information and the Statistical Manifold

The space of probability distributions pθ​:θ∈Θ is a statistical manifold. The Fisher information metric: gij​(θ)=Epθ​​[∂θi​∂logpθ​​∂θj​∂logpθ​​] defines a Riemannian metric on parameter space. The arc length under this metric is: L=∫01​θ˙(t)T,G(θ(t)),θ˙(t)​,dt.

This is the information-geometric distance between distributions. It underlies:

  • Natural gradient descent (Amari, 1998): gradient descent where the step direction accounts for the curvature of the statistical manifold — i.e., moving a fixed arc-length step in distribution space rather than Euclidean parameter space.
  • K-FAC and second-order optimizers: they approximate the Fisher metric to precondition gradients.
  • The Cramér-Rao bound: the minimum variance of an estimator is related to the Fisher metric.

Natural gradient descent is to ordinary gradient descent what geodesic motion is to straight-line motion — the path that respects the geometry of the space.

4. Sequence-to-Sequence Models and Dynamic Time Warping

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) aligns two time series by finding the optimal warping path — a curve in the (i,j) index plane. The cost of a warping path is an analogue of arc length in a weighted 2D grid. DTW is used in time-series classification, speech recognition, and as a differentiable loss for sequence models (soft-DTW, Cuturi & Blondel, 2017).

5. Path Length Regularization in GANs

The path length regularization in StyleGAN2 (Karras et al., 2020) penalizes the arc length of the mapping from latent codes to generated images varying unpredictably: LPL​=E[(∣Jg​(z)Tn∣−a)2], where n is a random unit vector and a is a moving average. This encourages the generator to move the same distance in image space for the same arc-length step in latent space — making latent interpolation smooth and controllable. In geometric terms: the generator mapping g:Z→X should be isometric (arc-length-preserving).

6. Curve Length in Attention Mechanisms

In continuous-time sequence models (e.g., S4, Mamba), the hidden state traces a trajectory through state space over time. The arc length of this trajectory characterizes the model’s “expressive capacity” — how much state it can change to process a sequence. Long trajectories correspond to high dynamical complexity; short trajectories correspond to smooth, slowly-varying representations.

Common Mistakes and Traps

  1. Forgetting the square root. Arc length is ∫1+(f′)2​,dx, not ∫(1+(f′)2),dx.

  2. Confusing arc length with area. They use different integrands. Area under f: ∫f,dx. Arc length of f: ∫1+(f′)2​,dx.

  3. Wrong parametric formula. L=∫x˙2+y˙​2​,dt. Do not differentiate with respect to x when you have a parametric curve.

  4. Traversing the curve more than once. If a parametric curve retraces itself, the integral counts the arc length multiple times. Check whether the parametrization is injective.

  5. Applying the Cartesian formula to a non-graph. If the curve has vertical tangents or self-intersections, use the parametric form instead.

  6. Forgetting that most arc length integrals have no closed form. When you cannot find an antiderivative, use numerical integration. This is expected — not a sign of error.
