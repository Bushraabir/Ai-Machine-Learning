1. The Natural Logarithm — Setup and Motivation

Recall that the natural logarithm lnx is defined for x>0 as the inverse of ex:

elnx=xfor all x>0

Equivalently, using the integral definition (MIT 18.02 perspective):

lnx:=∫1x​t1​,dt,x>0

This integral definition is sometimes taken as the definition. From it, all properties of ln follow — including its derivative, which becomes almost tautological. But let us proceed from the inverse function approach, as Stewart does, since it illuminates the technique.

Remark. The domain is x>0. We will extend to ln∣x∣ shortly, which gives domain x=0.

2. The Core Theorem

Theorem (Derivative of lnx). The function f(x)=lnx is differentiable on (0,∞), and dxd​[lnx]=x1​

This is arguably the most beautiful derivative in all of calculus. A transcendental function whose derivative is a rational function.

3. Rigorous Proof via Implicit Differentiation

Proof.

Let y=lnx. By definition of the natural logarithm as the inverse of ex:

ey=x

Differentiate both sides with respect to x, using the Chain Rule on the left:

dxd​[ey]=dxd​[x]

ey⋅dxdy​=1

Solve for dxdy​:

dxdy​=ey1​

Substitute back ey=x:

dxdy​=x1​■

What this proof requires:

  • The derivative of ex (established separately: (ex)′=ex).
  • The Chain Rule.
  • The fact that ey is differentiable and its inverse is defined on (0,∞).
  • Invertibility is guaranteed because ex is strictly increasing.

4. Alternative Proof via the Definition of e

Here is a second proof, using the limit definition of the derivative directly. This proof is illuminating because it reveals why e is the right base.

Proof.

By definition:

dxd​[lnx]=limh→0​hln(x+h)−lnx​

Use the logarithm law lnA−lnB=ln(A/B):

=limh→0​h1​ln!(xx+h​)=limh→0​h1​ln!(1+xh​)

Let u=h/x, so h=ux and h→0⇔u→0:

=limu→0​ux1​ln(1+u)=x1​limu→0​uln(1+u)​

Now use the continuity of ln and the key limit u→0lim​(1+u)1/u=e:

limu→0​uln(1+u)​=limu→0​ln(1+u)1/u=ln!(limu→0​(1+u)1/u)=lne=1

Therefore:

dxd​[lnx]=x1​⋅1=x1​■

Note. This is exactly why e is the “natural” base. Any other base a would leave a lingering factor of lna (see §5 below). Base e makes that factor equal to 1, giving the cleanest possible derivative.

5. The General Logarithm loga​x

Theorem. For a>0, a=1:

dxd​[loga​x]=xlna1​

Proof.

Use the change-of-base formula:

loga​x=lnalnx​

Since lna is a constant (with respect to x):

dxd​[loga​x]=lna1​⋅dxd​[lnx]=lna1​⋅x1​=xlna1​■

Special cases:

  • a=e: xlne1​=x1​ ✓
  • a=10: dxd​[log10​x]=xln101​≈x0.4343​

Remark. This factor lna1​ is why base-10 logarithms are “unnatural” for calculus — they carry dead weight. Engineers use log10​; mathematicians and everything downstream (information theory, ML) prefer ln.

6. Logarithmic Differentiation

Logarithmic differentiation is a technique, not just a formula. It is the tool of choice whenever:

  1. The function is a product/quotient of many terms.
  2. The exponent involves the variable (variable-base, variable-exponent).

Procedure:

  1. Set y=f(x).
  2. Take ln of both sides: lny=lnf(x).
  3. Differentiate implicitly with respect to x.
  4. Solve for y′ and substitute back y=f(x).

Why it works: ln converts multiplicative structure into additive structure, which is far easier to differentiate:

ln(u⋅v)=lnu+lnvln(u/v)=lnu−lnvln(un)=nlnu

7. The General Power Rule (Finally, Complete)

Previously you knew dxd​[xn]=nxn−1 for n∈Z, and later for n∈Q. Now, with logarithmic differentiation, we can prove it for all real n.

Theorem (General Power Rule). For x>0 and n∈R:

dxd​[xn]=nxn−1

Proof via logarithmic differentiation.

Let y=xn. Then:

lny=nlnx

Differentiate:

yy′​=xn​

y′=y⋅xn​=xn⋅xn​=nxn−1■

8. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Chain Rule with ln

Differentiate f(x)=ln(x2+1).

f′(x)=x2+11​⋅2x=x2+12x​

Pattern: dxd​[lng(x)]=g(x)g′(x)​ — the logarithmic derivative of g.

Example 2 — Absolute Value Extension

Differentiate f(x)=ln∣x∣ on x=0.

Case x>0: f(x)=lnx, so f′(x)=x1​.

Case x<0: f(x)=ln(−x), so f′(x)=−x1​⋅(−1)=x1​.

Therefore:

dxd​[ln∣x∣]=x1​,x=0​

This is why in integration: ∫x1​,dx=ln∣x∣+C.

Example 3 — Logarithmic Differentiation with a Product

Differentiate y=(3x+2)5x3/4x2+1​​.

Taking ln:

lny=43​lnx+21​ln(x2+1)−5ln(3x+2)

Differentiating:

yy′​=4x3​+x2+1x​−3x+215​

Therefore:

y′=(3x+2)5x3/4x2+1​​(4x3​+x2+1x​−3x+215​)

Try differentiating that directly without logarithms — you’ll appreciate the technique.

Example 4 — Variable-Base, Variable-Exponent

Differentiate y=xx for x>0.

lny=xlnx

yy′​=lnx+x⋅x1​=lnx+1

y′=xx(lnx+1)

This cannot be handled by the power rule (exponent is not constant) or the exponential rule (base is not constant). Logarithmic differentiation is the only clean path.

Example 5 — ln inside trigonometry

Differentiate f(x)=ln(sinx) on (0,π).

f′(x)=sinxcosx​=cotx

9. Visualization and Geometric Intuition

9.1 The graph of lnx and its derivative

y = ln(x)                        y' = 1/x

    |    /                            |
    |   /                             |\ 
    |  /                              | \
    | /                               |  \
----+---------> x               ------+-------> x
    |                                 |    \___
    (1, 0) is where ln x = 0          (1, 1) is the peak of 1/x
    slope there is exactly 1          then slope decreases

Key observations:

  • At x=1: ln(1)=0 and (lnx)′=1/1=1. The tangent line at (1,0) has slope 1.
  • As x→∞: lnx→∞ but slowly (sublinear growth). Its derivative 1/x→0.
  • As x→0+: lnx→−∞ and 1/x→+∞ — the function plunges but its instantaneous rate of change becomes very steep.

9.2 Concavity

dx2d2​[lnx]=dxd​![x1​]=−x21​<0for all x>0

lnx is strictly concave on its entire domain. This will be deeply significant in machine learning (§10).

9.3 Tangent Line at x=1

At x0​=1: f(1)=0, f′(1)=1. The tangent line is y=x−1.

This gives the fundamental inequality:

lnx≤x−1for all x>0, equality iff x=1

Proof: g(x)=x−1−lnx, g′(x)=1−1/x, g′(1)=0 (minimum), g(1)=0. Since g≥0 everywhere, lnx≤x−1. ■

This single inequality is used in dozens of proofs in information theory and ML.

10. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

Logarithms are not decoration in ML — they are structurally fundamental. Here is where dxd​[lnx]=x1​ appears throughout the field.

10.1 Cross-Entropy Loss

The workhorse loss function for classification is cross-entropy:

L=−∑i=1C​yi​lnp^​i​

where yi​∈0,1 are true labels and p^​i​∈(0,1) are predicted probabilities.

For binary classification (C=2):

L=−[ylnp^​+(1−y)ln(1−p^​)]

Why the gradient is clean:

∂p^​∂L​=−p^​y​+1−p^​1−y​

This is dxd​[lnx]=x1​ directly. The gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logit (before softmax) simplifies to p^​−y — a single subtraction — precisely because ln and exp are inverses and their derivatives interact perfectly.

10.2 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Given data x1​,…,xn​ drawn i.i.d. from a distribution with parameter θ, the likelihood is:

L(θ)=∏i=1n​p(xi​;θ)

Maximizing L is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood:

ℓ(θ)=lnL(θ)=∑i=1n​lnp(xi​;θ)

Why take ln? Because:

  1. Products become sums (numerically stable, analytically tractable).
  2. dθd​[lnp]=pp′​ — the score function — has mean zero under the true distribution. This is the Fisher information identity.
  3. The ln is monotone, so argmax is preserved.

Setting ∇θ​ℓ=0 uses dxd​[lnx] at every step.

10.3 KL Divergence and Information Theory

The Kullback-Leibler divergence measures how one distribution Q differs from another P:

DKL​(P∣Q)=∑x​P(x)lnQ(x)P(x)​

This appears in:

  • Variational autoencoders (VAE): the regularization term in the ELBO.
  • RL (policy gradient methods): trust region constraints (TRPO, PPO).
  • Bayesian inference: as a measure of information gained.

The concavity of ln (proven in §9.2) directly implies DKL​≥0 via Jensen’s inequality:

E[lnX]≤lnE[X]

(Jensen applies because ln is concave. Equality iff X is a.s. constant.)

10.4 Softmax and Numerical Stability

The softmax function is:

σi​(z)=∑j​ezj​ezi​​

In practice, ezi​ overflows for large zi​. The stable computation uses:

lnσi​(z)=zi​−ln!(∑j​ezj​)

This log-softmax trick, combined with cross-entropy, gives log_softmax + NLLLoss in PyTorch. The gradient through log-softmax uses dzd​[lnσ]=1−σ — again flowing from (lnx)′=1/x.

10.5 The Log-Sum-Exp Trick

\mathrm{LSE}(\mathbf{z}) = \ln!\sum_j e^{z_j} = z^* + \ln!\sum_j e^{z_j - z^_}, \quad z^_ = \max_j z_j

This is used everywhere numerical stability matters (HMMs, CRFs, attention mechanisms). Its gradient:

∂zi​∂,LSE​=∑j​ezj​ezi​​=σi​(z)

The softmax is the gradient of log-sum-exp. This geometric fact underlies the connection between energy-based models and attention.

10.6 Logarithmic Derivative in Natural Gradient Descent

In standard gradient descent, the update is θ←θ−η∇θ​L.

Natural gradient descent (Amari, 1998) uses the Fisher information matrix F(θ) to account for the geometry of the parameter space:

θ←θ−ηF(θ)−1∇θ​L

The Fisher matrix is:

Fij​(θ)=E![∂θi​∂lnp(x;θ)​⋅∂θj​∂lnp(x;θ)​]

The score ∂θ∂lnp​ is the logarithmic derivative of the model density. Natural gradient methods (including K-FAC, used in large-scale training) are built entirely on this structure.
