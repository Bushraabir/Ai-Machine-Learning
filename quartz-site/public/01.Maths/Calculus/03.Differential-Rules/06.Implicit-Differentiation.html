06.Implicit Differentiation

Apr 16, 202610 min read

“Most curves in nature are not graphs of functions. Implicit differentiation is how we differentiate them anyway.”

1. Motivation — When y Cannot Be Solved For

Consider the unit circle: x2+y2=1.

This is not a function — it fails the vertical line test. We can split it into y=1−x2​ and y=−1−x2​, but this is ugly, loses global structure, and becomes completely intractable for more complex curves like:

x5+y5−5xy=0(folium-like) ey+sin(xy)=x2(transcendental)

In these cases, isolating y is algebraically impossible. Yet the curve is smooth, and we expect tangent lines to exist. Implicit differentiation extracts dxdy​ directly from the equation F(x,y)=0, without ever solving for y.

The key insight: even if we cannot write y=f(x) explicitly, we can treat y as a function of x locally (by the Implicit Function Theorem, §7), and differentiate both sides of the equation accordingly.

2. The Method — Mechanics

Procedure

Given F(x,y)=0 where y is implicitly a function of x:

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to x.
  2. Every time y appears, apply the chain rule: dxd​[expression in y]=dyd​[⋯]⋅dxdy​.
  3. Collect all terms involving dxdy​ on one side.
  4. Solve algebraically for dxdy​.

The Formula

If F(x,y)=0, differentiating:

Fx​+Fy​⋅dxdy​=0

dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​​

where Fx​=∂x∂F​ and Fy​=∂y∂F​.

This is exact — no approximation, no loss of generality. It is a direct consequence of the chain rule applied to F(x,y(x))=0.

Reference: Stewart §3.5; MIT 18.02 Lecture 12

3. Examples

Example 1 — The Unit Circle

F(x,y)=x2+y2−1=0

dxd​[x2+y2]=0⟹2x+2ydxdy​=0

dxdy​=−yx​

Geometric check: At (1/2​,,1/2​): dy/dx=−1. The tangent is a −45° line — correct by symmetry. At (1,0) the formula gives −1/0, i.e., vertical tangent — again correct.

Example 2 — Folium of Descartes

F(x,y)=x3+y3−3xy=0

Differentiate with respect to x:

3x2+3y2dxdy​−3y−3xdxdy​=0

Factor:

dxdy​(3y2−3x)=3y−3x2

dxdy​=y2−xy−x2​

The folium has a node at the origin and a loop passing through (3/2,3/2). At that point: dy/dx=(3/2−9/4)/(9/4−3/2)=(−3/4)/(3/4)=−1. The curve has slope −1 there — geometrically, it’s crossing its own axis of symmetry y=x at that point, which makes perfect sense.

Example 3 — Transcendental Equation

exy=x+y

Differentiate both sides:

exy(y+xdxdy​)=1+dxdy​

Expand and collect:

yexy+xexydxdy​=1+dxdy​

dxdy​(xexy−1)=1−yexy

dxdy​=xexy−11−yexy​

No algebraic approach could have isolated y here. The implicit method cuts through directly.

Example 4 — Second Derivative

Find dx2d2y​ for x2+y2=r2.

First derivative: dxdy​=−yx​.

Differentiate again (quotient rule, remembering y depends on x):

dx2d2y​=−y2y⋅1−x⋅dxdy​​=−y2y−x(−x/y)​=−y2y+x2/y​=−y3y2+x2​

Since x2+y2=r2:

dx2d2y​=−y3r2​

For the upper semicircle (y>0), d2y/dx2<0 — the circle is concave down. Exactly right.

At any point (x0​,y0​) on the curve, the gradient ∇F=(Fx​,Fy​) is perpendicular to the level curve F=0. The tangent line is therefore perpendicular to ∇F.

This is not incidental — it is the geometric heart of implicit differentiation and gradient descent.

The Gradient is Always Normal to the Level Set

F(x,y)=c⟹∇F⊥tangent to curve

Proof: Let r(t)=(x(t),y(t)) be a curve lying on F=c. Then F(r(t))=c for all t. Differentiating:

dtd​F(r(t))=∇F⋅r′(t)=0

So ∇F⊥r′(t) — the gradient is orthogonal to every tangent vector. ■

This is the foundation of constrained optimization, Lagrange multipliers, and support vector machines.

5. Multivariable Implicit Differentiation

Two Variables, One Constraint

Given F(x,y,z)=0 with z=z(x,y):

∂x∂z​=−Fz​Fx​​,∂y∂z​=−Fz​Fy​​

Example: x2+y2+z2=1 (unit sphere).

∂x∂z​=−2z2x​=−zx​,∂y∂z​=−zy​

The tangent plane to the sphere at (x0​,y0​,z0​) has slope −x0​/z0​ in the x-direction and −y0​/z0​ in the y-direction.

The Cyclic Identity (Thermodynamics, Statistical Mechanics)

For F(x,y,z)=0, the three partial derivatives satisfy:

(∂y∂x​)z​⋅(∂z∂y​)x​⋅(∂x∂z​)y​=−1

This is not +1 (as a naive cancellation might suggest), and the minus sign is essential. It appears in the Maxwell relations in thermodynamics and the triple product rule in physical chemistry.

Reference: MIT 18.02 Lecture 13 — Gradient and Level Curves

6. The Implicit Function Theorem

This is the rigorous foundation for why implicit differentiation is valid.

Theorem (Implicit Function Theorem — IFT)

Let F:Rn+1→R be C1 near a point (x0​,y0​)∈Rn×R satisfying:

  1. F(x0​,y0​)=0
  2. ∂y∂F​(x0​,y0​)=0

Then there exists an open set U∋x0​ and a unique C1 function y:U→R such that:

  • y(x0​)=y0​
  • F(x,y(x))=0 for all x∈U
  • dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​

What This Means

The IFT says: locally, near any point where Fy​=0, the curve F(x,y)=0 really is the graph of a function y=f(x). So implicit differentiation is not a trick — it is a theorem-backed operation.

The condition Fy​=0 is what fails at vertical tangents (like (1,0) on the unit circle), and precisely where the formula dy/dx=−Fx​/Fy​ breaks down.

Generalization

The full IFT handles systems: given F(x,y)=0 with F:Rn+m→Rm, the condition for locally solving for y(x) is:

det(∂y∂F​)=0

i.e., the Jacobian of F with respect to y must be invertible. The derivative formula becomes:

∂x∂y​=−(∂y∂F​)−1∂x∂F​

This is matrix-valued implicit differentiation.

7. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

7.1 Implicit Layers in Deep Learning

A standard layer computes y=f(x;W) — an explicit map. But some modern architectures define layers implicitly via a fixed-point equation:

z=f(z,x;θ)(find z such that this holds)

This is called an implicit layer or deep equilibrium model (DEQ). Examples include:

  • Deep Equilibrium Models (Bai et al., 2019): the output z⋆ satisfies z⋆=fθ​(z⋆,x).
  • Neural ODEs: the output is defined by an ODE, not a finite sequence of operations.

To train these, we need ∂θ∂L​. But we never “unrolled” the computation — there’s no explicit computational graph.

Solution: The implicit function theorem. At the fixed point z⋆, define F(z,θ)=z−fθ​(z,x)=0. Then:

dθdz⋆​=−(∂z∂F​)−1∂θ∂F​=−(I−∂z∂f​)−1∂θ∂f​

The gradient propagates through the fixed-point equation, not through the iterations used to find it. This is the implicit differentiation trick — and it’s more memory-efficient than backpropagating through thousands of unrolled steps.

7.2 Constraint Satisfaction and Lagrange Multipliers

Training under constraints (e.g., fairness, energy, physics) involves:

minθ​L(θ)subject to G(θ)=0

The optimality condition is ∇L=λ∇G — the gradient of the loss must be normal to the constraint surface. This is the geometry of implicit curves in high dimensions.

The constraint surface G(θ)=0 is a level set of G, and its tangent hyperplane is characterized by ∇G⊥tangent vectors — exactly the implicit differentiation picture from §4.

7.3 Meta-Learning and Implicit Gradients

In MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning) and bilevel optimization, we differentiate through the solution of an inner optimization:

θ⋆(ϕ)=argminθ​Linner​(θ,ϕ)

At a minimum, ∇θ​Linner​=0. Define F(θ,ϕ)=∇θ​Linner​(θ,ϕ)=0. Then by the IFT:

dϕdθ⋆​=−(∂θ2∂2Linner​​)−1∂θ,∂ϕ∂2Linner​​

This requires the Hessian inverse — computationally expensive, but analytically exact. Approximations to this drive much of modern meta-learning research.

7.4 Energy-Based Models

In energy-based models (EBMs), the output is:

y⋆(x)=argminy​Eθ​(x,y)

At the minimum, ∂y∂E​=0 — an implicit equation. Differentiating this with respect to x:

dxdy⋆​=−(∂y2∂2E​)−1∂y,∂x∂2E​

The gradient of the predicted output with respect to the input involves the Hessian of the energy — a second-order implicit derivative.

8. Implicit vs. Explicit Differentiation — When to Use What

SituationUse
y can be isolated easilyExplicit — just solve and differentiate
y cannot be isolated (most curves)Implicit differentiation
Need dy/dx at a specific point onlyImplicit is faster even when y can be isolated
Multivariable constraint F(x,y,z)=0Always implicit
Neural network with implicit layers/DEQsIFT-based implicit differentiation
Bilevel optimization / meta-learningIFT applied to optimality conditions

9. Common Errors

  1. Forgetting the chain rule on y. Writing dxd​[y2]=2y instead of 2y,dxdy​. This is the single most common mistake.

  2. Treating y as a constant. When differentiating xy, the product rule gives y+x,dxdy​, not just y.

  3. Dividing by Fy​=0. The formula dy/dx=−Fx​/Fy​ fails exactly where Fy​=0. Check for vertical tangents separately.

  4. In the multivariable case, forgetting the cyclic minus sign: (∂y∂x​)z​=(∂x∂y​)z−1​ in general for constrained variables.

  5. In ML contexts, confusing the Jacobian of the constraint with the Jacobian of the output — when applying IFT to implicit layers, the matrix to invert is (I−∂f/∂z), not ∂f/∂z itself.

10. Summary

F(x,y)=0⟹dxdy​=−Fy​Fx​​

The formula is simple. Its depth comes from:

  • The chain rule is doing all the work invisibly.
  • The Implicit Function Theorem guarantees the formula is valid wherever Fy​=0.
  • In higher dimensions, this becomes Jy−1​Jx​ — matrix implicit differentiation.
  • In machine learning, this enables gradients through fixed points, constraints, equilibria, and bilevel programs — things standard backprop cannot reach.
