14.Inverse Hyperbolic Functions

A Professor’s Personal Notebook

References: Stewart, Calculus §3.11, §6.7; MIT 18.02 Multivariable Calculus
Prerequisite: Hyperbolic Functions

§ 0 — Why Bother With Inverses?

The honest answer: integrals.

The forms x2+1​1​, x2−1​1​, 1−x21​ appear constantly in calculus, physics, and machine learning. Without inverse hyperbolic functions, you are forced into trigonometric substitution — doable, but slower and more error-prone.

With inverse hyperbolic functions, these integrals become one-line antiderivatives.

A second reason: inverse hyperbolic functions are not transcendental mysteries — they collapse to logarithms. Every one of them is expressible in closed form. This makes them simultaneously computable, differentiable, and elegant.

§ 2 — Logarithmic Closed Forms

This is the core theorem. Every inverse hyperbolic function reduces to a logarithm.

Theorem 2.1

sinh−1x=ln!(x+x2+1​),x∈R​

Proof. Let y=sinh−1x, so sinhy=x:

2ey−e−y​=x⟹ey−e−y=2x

Multiply through by ey:

e2y−2xey−1=0

This is a quadratic in ey. Quadratic formula:

ey=22x±4x2+4​​=x±x2+1​

Since ey>0 for all y, and x2+1​>∣x∣, the expression x−x2+1​<0 always. So we must take +:

ey=x+x2+1​⟹y=ln!(x+x2+1​)□

Theorem 2.2

cosh−1x=ln!(x+x2−1​),x≥1​

Proof. Let y=cosh−1x≥0, so coshy=x:

2ey+e−y​=x⟹e2y−2xey+1=0

Quadratic formula:

ey=22x±4x2−4​​=x±x2−1​

Both roots are positive when x≥1, but they correspond to y≥0 and y≤0 respectively. Since we restrict cosh−1 to the branch y≥0, we need ey≥1, which requires ey=x+x2−1​:

y=ln!(x+x2−1​)□

Sanity check: At x=1: cosh−1(1)=ln(1+0)=0. ✓

Theorem 2.3

tanh−1x=21​ln!(1−x1+x​),∣x∣<1​

Proof. Let y=tanh−1x, so tanhy=x:

ey+e−yey−e−y​=x

Cross-multiply: ey−e−y=x(ey+e−y). Rearrange:

ey(1−x)=e−y(1+x)⟹e2y=1−x1+x​

Take logarithm:

2y=ln!(1−x1+x​)⟹y=21​ln!(1−x1+x​)□

Observe: The argument 1−x1+x​ must be positive, which requires ∣x∣<1 — consistent with tanh:R→(−1,1).

The Remaining Three

By analogous quadratic arguments (left as exercises):

coth−1x=21​ln!(x−1x+1​),∣x∣>1

sech−1x=ln!(x1+1−x2​​),0<x≤1

csch−1x=ln!(x1​+∣x∣1+x2​​),x=0

§ 4 — Derivatives: The Algebraic Computation

These are among the most useful derivatives in calculus.

Theorem 4.1 (Derivatives of Inverse Hyperbolics)

dxd​sinh−1x=x2+1​1​

dxd​cosh−1x=x2−1​1​,x>1

dxd​tanh−1x=1−x21​,∣x∣<1

dxd​sech−1x=x1−x2​−1​,0<x<1

dxd​csch−1x=∣x∣1+x2​−1​,x=0

dxd​coth−1x=1−x21​,∣x∣>1

Striking observation: tanh−1 and coth−1 have the same formal derivative 1−x21​, but on disjoint domains (∣x∣<1 vs ∣x∣>1). Together they provide the complete antiderivative of 1−x21​ on R∖±1.

Full Proof: Three Methods for (sinh−1x)′

Method 1: Implicit Differentiation (cleanest)

Let y=sinh−1x, so sinhy=x. Differentiate both sides with respect to x:

coshy⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=coshy1​

Now use the fundamental identity cosh2y−sinh2y=1:

coshy=1+sinh2y​=1+x2​

(positive square root since coshy>0 always). Therefore:

dxd​sinh−1x=1+x2​1​□

Method 2: Differentiate the Logarithm Directly (confirms the formula)

dxd​ln!(x+x2+1​)=x+x2+1​1​⋅dxd​!(x+x2+1​)

The inner derivative:

dxd​!(x+x2+1​)=1+x2+1​x​=x2+1​x2+1​+x​

So:

dxd​sinh−1x=x+x2+1​1​⋅x2+1​x+x2+1​​=x2+1​1​□

Method 3: Inverse Function Theorem (most general)

If f is differentiable and bijective, (f−1)′(x)=f′(f−1(x))1​.

Here f=sinh, f′=cosh, f−1=sinh−1:

(sinh−1)′(x)=cosh(sinh−1x)1​=1+x2​1​□

Proof for (cosh−1x)′

Let y=cosh−1x≥0, so coshy=x. Implicit differentiation:

sinhy⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=sinhy1​

From the identity: sinhy=cosh2y−1​=x2−1​ (positive root since y≥0⇒sinhy≥0):

dxd​cosh−1x=x2−1​1​,x>1□

Proof for (tanh−1x)′

Let y=tanh−1x, so tanhy=x. Implicit differentiation:

sech2y⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=sech2y1​=cosh2y

Use 1−tanh2y=sech2y:

sech2y=1−x2⟹dxdy​=1−x21​□

§ 5 — Integration: The Payoff

The derivatives immediately give antiderivatives. These are the standard integral forms that appear throughout calculus.

Standard Forms

∫x2+a2​dx​=sinh−1!(ax​)+C=ln!(x+x2+a2​)+C,a>0

∫x2−a2​dx​=cosh−1!(ax​)+C=ln!(x+x2−a2​)+C,x>a>0

∫a2−x2dx​=a1​tanh−1!(ax​)+C,∣x∣<a

∫a2−x2​dx​=sin−1!(ax​)+C(circular, for contrast)

The pattern: x2+a2​ → use sinh−1. x2−a2​ → use cosh−1. a2−x2 in denominator → use tanh−1.

Proof of the first form. Let u=x/a:

∫x2+a2​dx​=∫a2u2+a2​a,du​=∫au2+1​a,du​=∫u2+1​du​=sinh−1(u)+C□

Worked Examples

Example 5.1. Evaluate ∫02​x2+4​dx​.

=[sinh−1!(2x​)]02​=sinh−1(1)−sinh−1(0)=ln(1+2​)−0=ln(1+2​)

Numerically: ≈0.8814.

Example 5.2. Evaluate ∫4−x2dx​.

Here a=2, ∣x∣<2:

=21​tanh−1!(2x​)+C=41​ln!(2−x2+x​)+C

Double-check by partial fractions:

4−x21​=(2−x)(2+x)1​=41​(2−x1​+2+x1​)

Integrating: 41​(−ln∣2−x∣+ln∣2+x∣)+C=41​ln2−x2+x​+C. ✓

Example 5.3. Evaluate ∫x4+1​x,dx​.

Let u=x2, du=2x,dx:

=21​∫u2+1​du​=21​sinh−1(x2)+C=21​ln!(x2+x4+1​)+C

Example 5.4. (Completing the square) Evaluate ∫x2−4x+5​dx​.

Complete the square: x2−4x+5=(x−2)2+1. Let u=x−2:

=∫u2+1​du​=sinh−1(u)+C=sinh−1(x−2)+C=ln!((x−2)+x2−4x+5​)+C

§ 6 — Identities and Relations

Reflection Identities

sinh−1(−x)=−sinh−1(x)(odd)

tanh−1(−x)=−tanh−1(x)(odd)

cosh−1(x)≥0(not odd — domain is [1,∞))

Reciprocal Relations

csch−1x=sinh−1!(x1​),x=0

sech−1x=cosh−1!(x1​),0<x≤1

coth−1x=tanh−1!(x1​),∣x∣>1

Proof of the first: Let y=csch−1x, so cschy=x, i.e., sinhy=1/x. This means y=sinh−1(1/x). □

Linking tanh−1 to coth−1

Note tanh−1 and coth−1 share the derivative 1−x21​ on complementary domains. Together:

∫1−x2dx​={tanh−1x+C​∣x∣<1 coth−1x+C​∣x∣>1​=21​ln​1−x1+x​​+C

The last form is valid on all of R∖±1 and unifies both cases.

§ 7 — Taylor Series of Inverse Hyperbolic Functions

For ∣x∣<1:

sinh−1x=∑n=0∞​4n(n!)2(2n+1)(−1)n(2n)!​x2n+1=x−6x3​+403x5​−33615x7​+⋯

This follows from the binomial series applied to (1+x2)−1/2, then integrated term by term:

1+x2​1​=∑n=0∞​(n−1/2​)x2n=1−2x2​+83x4​−⋯

Integrating:

sinh−1x=∫0x​1+t2​dt​=x−6x3​+403x5​−⋯

For ∣x∣<1:

tanh−1x=∑n=0∞​2n+1x2n+1​=x+3x3​+5x5​+⋯

Proof (from the logarithm form):

tanh−1x=21​ln1−x1+x​=21​[ln(1+x)−ln(1−x)]

Using ln(1+u)=u−u2/2+u3/3−⋯:

=21​[(x−2x2​+3x3​−⋯)−(−x−2x2​−3x3​−⋯)]=x+3x3​+5x5​+⋯□

Personal note: This is the Leibniz-Gregory series for π/4: setting x=1 (formally, as the series converges at the boundary) gives tanh−1(1)… but tanh−1(1)=+∞. The analogous identity for arctan gives π/4=1−1/3+1/5−⋯ — note the alternating signs, reflecting the circular vs. hyperbolic difference.

§ 9 — Connection to AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning

9.1 — Inverse tanh in Policy Optimization (Reinforcement Learning)

In reinforcement learning, particularly Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and similar algorithms, the agent’s policy must output actions in a bounded range, say [−1,1]. A common trick:

  1. Sample z∼N(0,I) (Gaussian, unbounded)
  2. Apply a=tanh(z) to squash to (−1,1)

But training requires computing the log-probability of a under the original distribution. By the change-of-variables formula:

logp(a)=logp(z)−∑i​log!​dzi​d(tanhzi​)​​=logp(z)−∑i​log(1−tanh2zi​)

During inference, given observed action a, we need z=tanh−1(a) — the inverse! This is numerically computed as:

z=tanh−1(a)=21​ln1−a1+a​

9.2 — sinh−1 as a Variance-Stabilizing Transform

In financial machine learning and regression with heavy-tailed targets, the transform:

y~​=sinh−1(y)=ln!(y+y2+1​)

is preferred over log(y) because:

  • Defined for all reals (returns can be negative)
  • Behaves like ln(2y) for large y — compresses scale
  • Behaves like y near zero — preserves small values

This is used in Kaggle competitions involving financial targets, where the evaluation metric is often computed on transformed values.

9.3 — tanh−1 and Fisher’s Z-Transformation

In statistics (which underpins all of ML evaluation), Fisher’s Z-transformation of a correlation coefficient r∈(−1,1) is:

Z=tanh−1(r)=21​ln1−r1+r​

This stabilizes the variance of the sample correlation, making it approximately normally distributed. Used in:

  • Hypothesis testing for correlations
  • Confidence intervals for model performance metrics
  • Comparing feature correlations across datasets in exploratory data analysis

9.4 — Gradient Flow Through sinh−1 Activations

Recently (2020s), sinh−1 (arcsinh) has been proposed as an activation function in neural networks. Its derivative is:

dxd​sinh−1(x)=x2+1​1​

Key properties as an activation:

  • Not bounded (unlike tanh) — avoids saturation and vanishing gradients
  • Sublinear growth — gradient x2+1​1​→0 slowly as ∣x∣→∞ — does not explode like ReLU for large inputs
  • Smooth and odd — zero-centered, differentiable everywhere (unlike ReLU)

Compared to popular activations:

| Activation | Range | Gradient at large ∣x∣ | Smooth? | |---|---|---|---| | ReLU | [0,∞) | 0 or 1 | No (kink at 0) | | tanh | (−1,1) | →0 (saturates) | Yes | | GELU | ≈R | →1 | Yes | | sinh−1 (arcsinh) | R | →0 slowly | Yes |

9.5 — Hyperbolic Geometry in Representation Learning

The Poincaré disk model of hyperbolic geometry uses the metric:

ds2=(1−∣x∣2)24(dx2+dy2)​

Distances in this space involve cosh−1:

d(u,v)=cosh−1!(1+2(1−∣u∣2)(1−∣v∣2)∣u−v∣2​)

Why does ML care? Hyperbolic space has exponentially growing volume — perfect for embedding trees and hierarchies. The number of nodes at depth d in a tree grows as bd; hyperbolic space can accommodate this exponential branching with low distortion.

Poincaré embeddings (Nickel & Kiela, NeurIPS 2017) use cosh−1 in the loss function to embed WordNet noun hierarchy with far fewer dimensions than Euclidean embeddings require.

Euclidean space:  volume grows as r^n  (polynomial)
Hyperbolic space: volume grows as e^r  (exponential)
Trees:            nodes grow as b^d    (exponential)
→ Hyperbolic space is the natural home for hierarchical data

§ 10 — A Subtlety: Two Antiderivative Conventions

Consider ∫1−x2dx​.

Via partial fractions:

1−x21​=(1−x)(1+x)1​=21​(1+x1​+1−x1​)

∫1−x2dx​=21​ln1−x1+x​+C=21​ln!​1−x1+x​​+C

Via inverse hyperbolic: On ∣x∣<1: =tanh−1(x)+C. On ∣x∣>1: =coth−1(x)+C.

Both are correct — they differ by a constant on each connected component of the domain. The absolute value version is the most general single formula.

Personal note: This subtlety trips up many students. The issue is that ln requires a positive argument, but 1−x1+x​ is negative when ∣x∣>1. The absolute value fixes this while keeping the formula unified. Always specify the domain when writing antiderivatives of 1−x21​.
