Core Idea: The Fundamental ODE

All exponential phenomena trace back to a single differential equation:

dtdy​=ky

This equation says: the rate of change of a quantity is proportional to the quantity itself.

Solving by separation of variables:

ydy​=k,dt⟹ln∣y∣=kt+C⟹y(t)=y0​ekt

where y0​=y(0) is the initial condition.

  • If k>0: exponential growth
  • If k<0: exponential decay

This is the master formula. Every application below is a specialization of it.

1. Population Growth

Setup

Let P(t) = population at time t.

Malthusian (unlimited) growth assumes birth rate minus death rate is constant r:

dtdP​=rP

Solution

P(t)=P0​ert

where P0​=P(0) is the initial population.

Doubling Time

Set P(t)=2P0​:

2P0​=P0​ert⟹ert=2⟹tdouble​=rln2​

Worked Example

A bacterial colony starts at 1000 cells. The growth rate is r=0.3 per hour.

  1. Population after 5 hours: P(5)=1000⋅e0.3⋅5=1000⋅e1.5≈4482
  2. Doubling time: td​=ln2/0.3≈2.31 hours

Limitation

This model breaks down when resources are finite. The logistic model extends it:

dtdP​=rP(1−KP​)

where K is the carrying capacity. For this note, we stay in the pure exponential regime (early-stage growth, or P≪K).

2. Radioactive Decay

Setup

Radioactive nuclei decay at a rate proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present. Let N(t) = number of nuclei.

dtdN​=−λN

where λ>0 is the decay constant (units: time−1). The negative sign enforces decay.

Solution

N(t)=N0​e−λt

Half-Life

The half-life t1/2​ is the time for half the nuclei to decay:

2N0​​=N0​e−λt1/2​⟹t1/2​=λln2​

Equivalently: λ=t1/2​ln2​

Relationship: λ and t1/2​

QuantityFormula
Decay constantλ=ln2/t1/2​
Half-lifet1/2​=ln2/λ
Activity (decays/sec)A(t)=λN(t)

Worked Example

Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. A fossil has 30% of original C-14 remaining. Find its age.

0.30=e−λt,λ=5730ln2​

t=λ−ln(0.30)​=−ln2ln(0.30)⋅5730​=0.69311.2040⋅5730​≈9953 years

Key Insight

The half-life is independent of the initial quantity. Whether you start with 106 or 1012 nuclei, the same fraction remains after each half-life. This is a direct consequence of the linear ODE structure.

3. Newton’s Law of Cooling

Setup

The rate of heat loss of an object is proportional to the difference between the object’s temperature and the surrounding ambient temperature Ta​.

Let T(t) = temperature of the object at time t:

dtdT​=−k(T−Ta​),k>0

Solving the ODE

Let u(t)=T(t)−Ta​. Then dtdu​=dtdT​=−ku.

This is the standard decay ODE:

u(t)=u0​e−kt⟹T(t)−Ta​=(T0​−Ta​)e−kt

T(t)=Ta​+(T0​−Ta​)e−kt​

Interpretation

  • As t→∞: T(t)→Ta​ (object reaches ambient temperature)
  • The temperature difference decays exponentially
  • k depends on the object’s surface area, material, and fluid properties (from heat transfer physics)

Worked Example

A cup of coffee at 90∘C is left in a 20∘C room. After 10 minutes it cools to 70∘C. Find its temperature after 30 minutes.

Step 1: Find k.

70=20+(90−20)e−10k⟹50=70e−10k⟹e−10k=75​

−10k=ln!(75​)⟹k=10ln(7/5)​≈0.03365

Step 2: Temperature at t=30.

T(30)=20+70⋅e−0.03365⋅30=20+70⋅(75​)3=20+70⋅343125​≈45.5∘C

Forensic Application

Newton’s Law of Cooling is used in forensics to estimate the time of death. If the body temperature is measured at two times post-discovery, the system of equations can be solved for both k and the time elapsed since death.

4. Continuously Compounded Interest

Setup

In standard compound interest with n compounding periods per year:

A=P(1+nr​)nt

As n→∞ (continuous compounding), recall the definition of e:

limn→∞​(1+nr​)n=er

Therefore:

A(t)=Pert​

This is exactly the exponential growth equation with the ODE:

dtdA​=rA

meaning: the instantaneous rate of growth of money equals the rate r times the current amount.

Comparison: Discrete vs Continuous

CompoundingFormulaEffective Annual Rate
Annual (n=1)A=P(1+r)tr
Monthly (n=12)A=P(1+r/12)12t(1+r/12)12−1
ContinuousA=Perter−1

Worked Example

$$5000isinvestedat6%$ annual interest continuously compounded. Find its value after 10 years, and the time to double.

Value after 10 years:

A(10) = 5000 \cdot e^{0.06 \cdot 10} = 5000 \cdot e^{0.6} \approx 5000 \cdot 1.8221 \approx $9110.59

Time to double:

2P=Pert⟹t=rln2​=0.060.6931​≈11.55 years

Rule of 70

An approximation: at interest rate r, doubling time ≈70/r years. This comes from ln2≈0.693≈0.70.

Unified Structure: All Four Models

ApplicationODESolutionKey Parameter
Population growthdP/dt=rPP0​ertGrowth rate r>0
Radioactive decaydN/dt=−λNN0​e−λtDecay constant λ>0
Newton’s coolingdT/dt=−k(T−Ta​)Ta​+(T0​−Ta​)e−ktCooling constant k>0
Compound interestdA/dt=rAPertInterest rate r>0

Newton’s cooling is the only one where the quantity of interest (T−Ta​, not T itself) follows the basic ODE.

Finding the Constant k: General Strategy

Given two data points (t1​,y1​) and (t2​,y2​):

y1​y2​​=y0​ekt1​y0​ekt2​​=ek(t2​−t1​)

k=t2​−t1​ln(y2​/y1​)​

This does not require knowing y0​. Once k is found, plug back into y1​=y0​ekt1​ to find y0​ if needed.

Linearization: Why Semi-log Plots Matter

If y(t)=y0​ekt, take the natural log of both sides:

lny=lny0​+kt

This is linear in t. On a semi-log plot (log scale on y-axis, linear on x-axis), exponential data becomes a straight line.

  • Slope of the line = k
  • y-intercept = lny0​

This is how scientists verify whether a process is truly exponential and extract k from experimental data.

Common Errors and Misconceptions

Error 1: Confusing half-life and time constant.

The time constant τ=1/k (or 1/λ) is the time for the quantity to fall to 1/e≈36.8 of its initial value. It is not the half-life. Half-life =τln2≈0.693τ.

Error 2: Applying growth/decay to the wrong quantity.

In Newton’s cooling, the exponential law applies to T(t)−Ta​, not to T(t) directly. Forgetting to subtract Ta​ before modeling gives wrong answers.

Error 3: Unit inconsistency.

If r is in percent per year and t is in months, the formula breaks. Always ensure r and t have consistent units.

Connections to the Broader Curriculum

  • Stat 110 / Probability: The exponential distribution f(t)=λe−λt is the continuous analog of geometric distribution, and governs the waiting time between events in a Poisson process. The decay constant λ here is precisely the same as radioactive decay.
  • MIT 18.02 / Calculus: Separation of variables technique used here is foundational. The integrating factor method generalizes this to non-separable first-order ODEs.
  • CS229 / ML: Exponential families, learning rate schedules (αt​=α0​e−λt), and the softmax function all embed exponential structure.
  • Pattern Recognition and ML (Bishop): Gaussian distributions contain exponential terms; the log-likelihood linearizes them, exactly as the semi-log trick above.

Practice Problems

  1. A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8 days. What fraction remains after 20 days? At what time does only 10% remain?

  2. A body is found at 25∘C in a 15∘C room. One hour later it is 22∘C. When did death occur, assuming body temperature at death was 37∘C?

  3. At what continuously compounded rate does money triple in 12 years?

  4. Two populations A and B start at sizes 500 and 2000 respectively. A grows at rA​=0.05/yr, B declines at rB​=0.02/yr. At what time are they equal?

  5. Show algebraically that the half-life is independent of the initial quantity N0​. What property of the ODE makes this true?

Summary

All four applications (population growth, radioactive decay, Newton’s cooling, continuously compounded interest) are governed by the single ODE dy/dt=ky. The solution is always an exponential. The sign of k determines growth versus decay. Newton’s cooling differs only in that the relevant quantity is the temperature surplus above ambient, not the temperature itself. Mastery of this note reduces to: recognizing the ODE in disguise, solving for the constant from data, and applying the formula precisely.
