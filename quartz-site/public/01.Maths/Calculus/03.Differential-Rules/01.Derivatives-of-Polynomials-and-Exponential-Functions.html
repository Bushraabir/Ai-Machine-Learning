Why These Two Functions First?

Polynomials and exponentials are the two families where differentiation produces a closed-form answer from first principles without too much suffering. They are also:

  • The building blocks of Taylor approximations (every smooth function looks like a polynomial locally)
  • The eigenfunctions of the derivative operator (eax satisfies dxd​f=af)
  • The backbone of every neural network computation

We start here because the limit definition is still tractable, and the patterns we discover generalize everywhere.

Part I: Derivatives of Polynomials

The Constant Rule

dxd​[c]=0for any constant c∈R

Proof from the definition:

limh→0​hc−c​=limh→0​0=0■

Geometric meaning: The graph of f(x)=c is a horizontal line. Its slope is zero everywhere. No surprise.

The Power Rule

dxd​[xn]=nxn−1

This holds for all n∈R (integers, rationals, irrationals — we prove the integer case here and extend later via logarithmic differentiation).

Proof for n∈N (from the definition)

dxd​[xn]=limh→0​h(x+h)n−xn​

Expand (x+h)n by the Binomial Theorem:

(x+h)n=∑k=0n​(kn​)xn−khk=xn+nxn−1h+(2n​)xn−2h2+⋯+hn

Subtract xn and divide by h:

h(x+h)n−xn​=nxn−1+(2n​)xn−2h+⋯+hn−1

Every term except the first has at least one factor of h. As h→0, all these vanish:

limh→0​[nxn−1+(2n​)xn−2h+⋯+hn−1]=nxn−1■

The Pattern

📌 Mnemonic: “Bring the exponent down, reduce it by one.” The exponent becomes the coefficient, and the new exponent is one less.

The Power Rule for Negative and Fractional Exponents

The rule dxd​[xn]=nxn−1 holds for all real n, not just positive integers.

dxd​[x1​]=dxd​[x−1]=−1⋅x−2=−x21​

dxd​[x​]=dxd​[x1/2]=21​x−1/2=2x​1​

dxd​[xπ]=πxπ−1

Proof for n=−1 directly from the definition (worth seeing):

limh→0​hx+h1​−x1​​=limh→0​hx(x+h)x−(x+h)​​=limh→0​h⋅x(x+h)−h​=limh→0​x(x+h)−1​=−x21​■

The Constant Multiple Rule

dxd​[c⋅f(x)]=c⋅f′(x)

Proof:

limh→0​hc⋅f(x+h)−c⋅f(x)​=climh→0​hf(x+h)−f(x)​=c⋅f′(x)■

This is just the linearity of limits — constants factor out.

The Sum and Difference Rules

dxd​[f(x)±g(x)]=f′(x)±g′(x)

Proof:

limh→0​h[f(x+h)±g(x+h)]−[f(x)±g(x)]​ =limh→0​hf(x+h)−f(x)​±limh→0​hg(x+h)−g(x)​=f′(x)±g′(x)■

Differentiation is a linear operator: dxd​[αf+βg]=αf′+βg′.

Putting It Together: Differentiating Any Polynomial

For p(x)=an​xn+an−1​xn−1+⋯+a1​x+a0​:

p′(x)=nan​xn−1+(n−1)an−1​xn−2+⋯+a1​

Example:

f(x)=5x4−32​x3+2​,x2−7x+11

f′(x)=20x3−2x2+22​,x−7

The constant 11 vanishes. The coefficient 2​ is just a constant multiple — it survives unchanged.

Part II: The Derivative of ex

The Central Fact

dxd​[ex]=ex

The exponential function is its own derivative. This is the defining property that makes e special among all bases.

Proof from the Definition

dxd​[ex]=limh→0​hex+h−ex​=limh→0​ex⋅heh−1​=exlimh→0​heh−1​

Everything hinges on the limit h→0lim​heh−1​.

Evaluating the key limit. Using the Taylor series eh=1+h+2!h2​+⋯:

heh−1​=hh+2!h2​+3!h3​+⋯​=1+2!h​+3!h2​+⋯h→0​1

Therefore:

dxd​[ex]=ex⋅1=ex■

⚠️ Circularity concern: If you define ex by its Taylor series, then the above is clean. If you define e=limn→∞​(1+1/n)n, you need to verify that limh→0​heh−1​=1 independently — this requires showing the exponential function is differentiable and using the squeeze theorem or L’Hôpital. Different textbooks take different routes. Stewart builds toward this carefully.

Why e and No Other Base?

Consider f(x)=ax for arbitrary base a>0:

dxd​[ax]=limh→0​hax+h−ax​=axlimh→0​hah−1​

Define L(a)=h→0lim​hah−1​. Then dxd​[ax]=L(a)⋅ax.

What is L(a)?

Using ah=ehlna and the limit we just computed:

L(a)=limh→0​hehlna−1​=limh→0​h(hlna)+O(h2)​=lna

So:

dxd​[ax]=(lna)⋅ax​

The special role of e: e is the unique base for which lna=1, making L(e)=1. With any other base, differentiation introduces a multiplicative factor of lna.

The Chain Rule Extension (Preview)

By the chain rule (proved later):

dxd​[eg(x)]=eg(x)⋅g′(x)

Examples:

dxd​[e3x]=3e3x

dxd​[ex2]=2xex2

dxd​[e−x]=−e−x

This last one is why e−x is a decreasing exponential — its derivative is the negative of itself.

The Derivative Operator as a Linear Map

Let Pn​ denote the vector space of polynomials of degree ≤n. The derivative D=dxd​ is a linear map Pn​→Pn−1​.

With basis 1,x,x2,…,xn, the matrix representation of D is:

[D]=(0​1​0​0​⋯ 0​0​2​0​⋯ 0​0​0​3​⋯ ⋮​​​​⋱​)

This is a nilpotent matrix: Dn+1=0 on Pn​ (differentiate n+1 times, get zero). This is the algebraic version of “differentiating a degree-n polynomial n+1 times gives zero.”

For the exponential, D acts on the infinite-dimensional space of smooth functions. The operator equation Df=f has solution space spanex — the eigenspace with eigenvalue 1. More generally, Df=λf has solution eλx, the eigenfunction for eigenvalue λ.

📌 This eigenfunction perspective is the deep reason why exponentials appear everywhere in physics, ODEs, signal processing, and deep learning — they are the basis in which differentiation is diagonal.

Key Identities and Special Values

At x=0

dxndn​[xk]​x=0​={n!​k=n 0​k=n​

This is exactly why Taylor coefficients are n!f(n)(0)​ — the power xn contributes only to the n-th derivative at 0.

Exponential at Any Point

dxndn​[ex]​x=a​=ea

All derivatives agree at every point. The Taylor series of ex at a:

ex=ea∑n=0∞​n!(x−a)n​

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Straight differentiation

f(x)=3x5−x24​+3x​

Rewrite with exponents: f(x)=3x5−4x−2+x1/3.

f′(x)=15x4+8x−3+31​x−2/3=15x4+x38​+3x2/31​

Example 2 — Tangent line to y=x3 at (2,8)

f′(x)=3x2. Slope at x=2: f′(2)=12.

Tangent line: y−8=12(x−2)⟹y=12x−16.

Example 3 — Where is the tangent line horizontal?

f(x)=x3−12x+5. Horizontal tangent ⇔ f′(x)=0.

f′(x)=3x2−12=3(x2−4)=3(x−2)(x+2)=0

At x=2: f(2)=8−24+5=−11. At x=−2: f(−2)=−8+24+5=21.

Horizontal tangents at (2,−11) and (−2,21).

Example 4 — Exponential tangent

f(x)=ex+x2. Find the tangent at x=0.

f(0)=1+0=1. f′(x)=ex+2x. f′(0)=1+0=1.

Tangent: y−1=1(x−0)⟹y=x+1.

Example 5 — Finding where two curves have equal slopes

Where does f(x)=x3+x have the same slope as g(x)=3x2−1?

f′(x)=3x2+1, g′(x)=6x.

Set equal: 3x2+1=6x⟹3x2−6x+1=0⟹x=66±36−12​​=1±36​​.

Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

1. The Exponential as the Fundamental Building Block

Nearly every probability distribution and activation function in ML reduces to an exponential:

  • Softmax: softmax(zi​)=∑j​ezj​ezi​​ — converts logits to probabilities
  • Sigmoid: σ(x)=1+e−x1​=ex+1ex​
  • Exponential family distributions: Gaussian, Poisson, Bernoulli, Gamma — all have density ∝eη(θ)TT(x)

Their derivatives all flow from dxd​[ex]=ex:

σ′(x)=σ(x)(1−σ(x))

Proof: Let f(x)=(1+e−x)−1. By chain rule and power rule:

f′(x)=−(1+e−x)−2⋅(−e−x)=(1+e−x)2e−x​=1+e−x1​⋅1+e−xe−x​=σ(x)(1−σ(x))

2. Polynomial Feature Maps

Kernel methods and feature engineering often use polynomial features. Given input x∈R, the degree-d polynomial map is:

ϕ(x)=(1,x,x2,…,xd)

The derivative of the model f(θ,x)=∑k=0d​θk​xk with respect to parameters:

∂θk​∂f​=xk

And with respect to input (used in saliency maps):

∂x∂f​=∑k=1d​kθk​xk−1

The power rule is literally the gradient computation for polynomial models.

3. Weight Initialization and Exponential Growth/Decay

When you stack L layers in a neural network, the output is roughly a product of L matrices. The eigenvalues of these products grow or shrink exponentially in L. If all eigenvalues are >1: exploding gradients. If <1: vanishing gradients.

This is because dxd​[aL]=(lna)⋅aL — derivative grows at the same exponential rate as the function. The fix (batch normalization, careful initialization) is explicitly designed to keep the effective base a≈1, so lna≈0 and the exponential is controlled.

4. Learning Rate Schedules

Common learning rate schedulers use polynomial or exponential decay:

Polynomial decay: η(t)=η0​(1−Tt​)p⟹η′(t)=−Tpη0​​(1−Tt​)p−1

Exponential decay: η(t)=η0​e−λt⟹η′(t)=−λη0​e−λt=−λη(t)

The exponential schedule has the property that its rate of decrease is proportional to its current value — the derivative is a constant multiple of the function itself. This self-similar decay is exactly what makes exponential schedules “smooth” and predictable.

5. Softmax Jacobian

The gradient of the softmax is a matrix (the Jacobian). For si​=∑k​ezk​ezi​​:

∂zj​∂si​​=si​(δij​−sj​)

Proof: For i=j, use the quotient rule with f=ezi​, g=∑k​ezk​:

∂zi​∂si​​=(∑k​ezk​)2ezi​⋅∑k​ezk​−ezi​⋅ezi​​=si​(1−si​)=si​(δii​−si​)

For i=j:

∂zj​∂si​​=(∑k​ezk​)20⋅∑k​ezk​−ezi​⋅ezj​​=−si​sj​=si​(δij​−sj​)

Every step uses only dxd​[ex]=ex and the power/quotient rules. The entire forward pass of a classification network ends here.

6. Polynomial Complexity and the Big-O Connection

When we say an algorithm runs in O(nk) time, the derivative with respect to problem size behaves like knk−1 — the power rule tells you how quickly the cost grows. The distinction between polynomial time (nk) and exponential time (en) is precisely the difference between these two families of functions: polynomial growth is eventually dominated by exponential growth for any fixed base.

limn→∞​ennk​=0for any fixed k

This follows from L’Hôpital applied k times — reducing nk to a constant via the power rule, while en reproduces itself.

Things to Think About

  • Prove the power rule for n∈Z− directly from the definition, using the trick from the n=−1 proof.
  • Prove dxd​[x1/2]=21​x−1/2 directly from the definition (rationalize the numerator).
  • What is dxd​[xx]? (It’s neither purely a power rule nor purely an exponential — try xx=exlnx.)
  • Show that e is the unique number satisfying limh→0​heh−1​=1.
  • The function f(x)=ex is characterized (up to scaling) by the ODE f′=f. What function satisfies f′′=f? What about f′′=−f?
