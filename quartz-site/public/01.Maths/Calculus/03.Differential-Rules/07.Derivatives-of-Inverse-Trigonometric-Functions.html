07.Derivatives of Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Apr 16, 20268 min read

“Every inverse trig derivative is secretly the chain rule wearing an algebraic disguise — and the algebra always hides a Pythagorean identity.”

Tags

#calculus #differentiation #inverse-trig #chain-rule #implicit-differentiation #analysis #machine-learning

Prerequisites

  • Chain Rule
  • Implicit differentiation
  • The identity sin2θ+cos2θ=1 and its cousins
  • The definition and domain/range of inverse trig functions

1. Why Inverse Trig Functions Need Their Own Treatment

The function sin(x) is not injective on R — it repeats every 2π. To define an inverse, we restrict the domain. This restriction is not a technicality; it determines the sign convention in every derivative formula below. Getting the domain wrong means getting the formula wrong.

FunctionRestricted DomainRange
sin−1x (arcsin)[−π/2,,π/2][−1,,1]
cos−1x (arccos)[0,,π][−1,,1]
tan−1x (arctan)(−π/2,,π/2)R
cot−1x (arccot)(0,,π)R
sec−1x (arcsec)[0,π]∖π/2(−∞,−1]∪[1,∞)
csc−1x (arccsc)[−π/2,π/2]∖0(−∞,−1]∪[1,∞)

The strategy for every derivative is the same:

  1. Write y=sin−1(x), i.e., sin(y)=x.
  2. Differentiate both sides implicitly.
  3. Solve for dxdy​.
  4. Express everything back in terms of x using a Pythagorean identity.

2. The Master Strategy — Implicit Differentiation

Let me do the arcsin case in full detail so the template is clear.

Deriving dxd​sin−1(x)

Step 1. Let y=sin−1(x). By definition, sin(y)=x, with y∈[−π/2,π/2].

Step 2. Differentiate both sides with respect to x:

cos(y)⋅dxdy​=1

Step 3. Solve:

dxdy​=cos(y)1​

Step 4. We need cos(y) in terms of x. From sin2(y)+cos2(y)=1:

cos(y)=±1−sin2(y)​=±1−x2​

Step 5. Choose the sign. Since y∈[−π/2,π/2], we have cos(y)≥0. So we take the positive root:

cos(y)=1−x2​

Therefore:

dxd​sin−1(x)=1−x2​1​,x∈(−1,1)​

The sign choice in Step 5 is the entire content of the domain restriction. If we had used the cos−1 branch instead, we would pick the negative root and get a minus sign. That’s exactly how cos−1 works.

3. All Six Derivatives — Derived

3.1 dxd​cos−1(x)

Let sin(y)+cos(y)… more cleanly: let y=cos−1(x), so cos(y)=x, y∈[0,π].

−sin(y)⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=−sin(y)1​

From sin2(y)+cos2(y)=1: sin(y)=±1−x2​. Since y∈[0,π], sin(y)≥0, so positive root:

dxd​cos−1(x)=−1−x2​1​,x∈(−1,1)​

Observe: dxd​sin−1(x)+dxd​cos−1(x)=0. This is because sin−1(x)+cos−1(x)=2π​ is a constant — differentiating a constant gives zero.

3.2 dxd​tan−1(x)

Let y=tan−1(x), so tan(y)=x, y∈(−π/2,π/2).

sec2(y)⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=sec2(y)1​=cos2(y)

Use sec2(y)=1+tan2(y)=1+x2:

dxd​tan−1(x)=1+x21​,x∈R​

No square root — no sign issue. This is the cleanest of the six.

3.3 dxd​cot−1(x)

By the same logic (or by noting cot−1(x)+tan−1(x)=π/2):

dxd​cot−1(x)=−1+x21​,x∈R​

Derivation: y=cot−1(x), cot(y)=x, y∈(0,π).

−csc2(y)⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=−csc2(y)1​=−1+cot2(y)1​=−1+x21​

3.4 dxd​sec−1(x)

This one is subtle because of the two-piece domain.

Let y=sec−1(x), so sec(y)=x, y∈[0,π]∖π/2.

sec(y)tan(y)⋅dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=sec(y)tan(y)1​=xtan(y)1​

From tan2(y)=sec2(y)−1=x2−1, so tan(y)=±x2−1​.

For the standard branch: sec(y)=x, and on [0,π/2), tan(y)≥0; on (π/2,π], tan(y)≤0. Combined with the sign of x, the product xtan(y) is always positive (a careful case analysis confirms this). Therefore:

dxd​sec−1(x)=∣x∣x2−1​1​,∣x∣>1​

The ∣x∣ is forced by the domain split. Stewart uses this form; some texts use xx2−1​1​ for x>1 only.

3.5 dxd​csc−1(x)

By symmetry (or csc−1(x)+sec−1(x)=π/2):

dxd​csc−1(x)=−∣x∣x2−1​1​,∣x∣>1​

4. The Complete Reference Table

\begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline \textbf{Function} & \textbf{Derivative} & \textbf{Domain} \ \hline \sin^{-1}(x) & \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} & (-1,1) \[10pt] \cos^{-1}(x) & -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} & (-1,1) \[10pt] \tan^{-1}(x) & \dfrac{1}{1+x^2} & \mathbb{R} \[10pt] \cot^{-1}(x) & -\dfrac{1}{1+x^2} & \mathbb{R} \[10pt] \sec^{-1}(x) & \dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}} & |x|>1 \[10pt] \csc^{-1}(x) & -\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}} & |x|>1 \ \hline \end{array}

Memory pattern: The co-functions (cos−1, cot−1, csc−1) are always the negative of their counterpart. If you know three, you know all six.

In practice, these never appear alone. The real skill is handling sin−1(g(x)).

Example 1

Let u=x2: =1+(x2)21​⋅2x=1+x42x​

Example 2

dxd​[x2tan−1(x)]

Example 4 — Logarithm composed with arctan

This is why these derivatives matter for integration — each formula is an antiderivative.

6. Geometric Interpretation

For sin−1(x)

Think of the right triangle: if sin(θ)=x, then the opposite side is x, hypotenuse is 1, adjacent side is 1−x2​.

cos(θ)=hypotenuseadjacent​=11−x2​​

So dxd​sin−1(x)=cos(θ)1​=1−x2​1​ — exactly the ratio we derived algebraically.

The triangle encodes the Pythagorean identity geometrically:

        1 (hypotenuse)
       /|
      / |
     /  |  x  (opposite = sin θ)
    /   |
   /θ   |
  -------
  √(1-x²) (adjacent)

For tan−1(x): if tan(θ)=x=x/1, then opposite =x, adjacent =1, hypotenuse =1+x2​. So cos2(θ)=1+x21​, which is exactly dxd​tan−1(x).

7. The Inverse Function Theorem — The Deeper Why

There is a general principle underlying all six formulas. If f is differentiable and injective on an interval with f′(x)=0, then f−1 is differentiable and:

(f−1)′(y)=f′(f−1(y))1​

Applied to f=sin, f−1=sin−1, f′(y)=cos(y):

(f−1)′(x)=cos(sin−1(x))1​=1−x2​1​

This is not a coincidence — it’s the Inverse Function Theorem, which works in any dimension. The single-variable formula above is its one-dimensional specialization.

Reference: Stewart §3.4 (implicit differentiation), §3.5 (inverse trig derivatives)

8. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

8.1 Activation Functions — arctan and softsign

The function σ(x)=tan−1(x) was historically used as an activation function because:

  • It is smooth everywhere on R
  • It is bounded: tan−1(x)∈(−π/2,π/2)
  • Its derivative 1+x21​ is always positive and never zero

Compare to sigmoid σ(x)=1+e−x1​: both saturate at ±∞ and both have vanishing gradient issues in deep networks, but arctan’s derivative is clean and algebraically simple.

The softsign activation f(x)=1+∣x∣x​ has a derivative structurally similar to (1+x2)1​ near zero.

8.2 Directional Encodings — Angle Representations

In robotics and reinforcement learning, angles appear constantly (joint positions, orientations). Gradients of loss with respect to angle parameters involve tan−1 and its derivative, since θ=tan−1(y/x) converts Cartesian to polar coordinates.

The gradient of θ=atan2(y,x) with respect to x and y:

∂x∂θ​=−x2+y2y​,∂y∂θ​=x2+y2x​

Both formulas are disguised versions of dxd​tan−1(x)=1+x21​.

8.3 Normalizing Flows and Change of Variables

In generative modeling, normalizing flows require computing the log-determinant of a Jacobian. When the flow involves arcsin or arctan transformations (common in bounded-support flows), the derivatives in §4 are exactly what enters the log-det computation:

log∣detJ∣=∑i​log​∂xi​∂Ti​​​

For an element-wise arctan layer: Ti​(xi​)=tan−1(xi​), so ∂xi​∂Ti​​=1+xi2​1​, and the log-det becomes −∑i​log(1+xi2​).

8.4 Differentiable Geometry in Neural Networks

Graph neural networks and point-cloud models (PointNet, 3D vision) must handle angular features. Backpropagating through angle computations — which involve sin−1, cos−1, tan−1 — requires exactly these derivatives. PyTorch and JAX implement torch.asin, torch.acos, torch.atan with correct autodiff rules precisely derived from the formulas above.

9. Standard Integrals — The Payoff

Every derivative formula yields an integral formula by reading it backwards:

∫1−u2​du​=sin−1(u)+C

∫1+u2du​=tan−1(u)+C

∫uu2−1​du​=sec−1(u)+C

More generally, by substitution:

∫a2−u2​du​=sin−1!(au​)+C

∫a2+u2du​=a1​tan−1!(au​)+C

These are essential in integral calculus and arise when completing the square in the denominator.

Example: Evaluate ∫x2+4x+5dx​.

Complete the square: x2+4x+5=(x+2)2+1. Let u=x+2:

∫u2+1du​=tan−1(u)+C=tan−1(x+2)+C

10. Summary — The Structural Picture

The six inverse trig derivatives fall into three pairs. Each pair sums to zero (derivative of a constant π/2):

dxd​sin−1(x)+dxd​cos−1(x)=0

dxd​tan−1(x)+dxd​cot−1(x)=0

dxd​sec−1(x)+dxd​csc−1(x)=0

All six derivations follow the same template:

Implicit differentiation → solve for dy/dx → Pythagorean identity → domain-appropriate sign choice

And at the bottom of every derivation, the same machinery appears: the chain rule, applied to an identity.
