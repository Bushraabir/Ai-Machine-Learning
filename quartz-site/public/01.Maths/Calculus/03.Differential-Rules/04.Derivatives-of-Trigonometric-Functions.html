04.Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Apr 16, 202612 min read

“The derivative of sine is cosine — a fact so clean it feels like it must be obvious. It is not. It is a theorem, and its proof requires real analytic work: a geometric squeeze, a careful limit, and only then the reward.”

Tags

#calculus #differentiation #trigonometry #analysis #machine-learning #deep-learning

The Six Derivatives — Master Table

\begin{array}{|c|c|} \hline f(x) & f'(x) \ \hline \sin x & \cos x \ \cos x & -\sin x \ \tan x & \sec^2 x \ \cot x & -\csc^2 x \ \sec x & \sec x \tan x \ \csc x & -\csc x \cot x \ \hline \end{array}

The pattern to observe: sin→cos→−sin→−cos→sin→⋯ — differentiating cycles with period 4. The “co-” functions (cosine, cotangent, cosecant) all carry a minus sign. This is not coincidence — it reflects the fact that cosx=sin(π/2−x), so differentiating introduces a chain rule factor of −1.

The Two Fundamental Limits

Everything in this section rests on two limits. They must be proved before any derivative is claimed.

Limit 1

limθ→0​θsinθ​=1

Proof by the Squeeze Theorem.

Consider 0<θ<π/2. Inscribe and circumscribe a unit circle with triangles:

          B
         /|
        / |
       /  |
      /   | sin θ
     / θ  |
    O-----A-----C
    |←1→| |←tan θ→|

Area of △OAB ≤ Area of sector OAB ≤ Area of △OAC

Computing each area explicitly:

21​sinθcosθ≤21​θ≤21​tanθ

Divide through by 21​sinθ>0:

cosθ≤sinθθ​≤cosθ1​

Invert (reversing inequalities since all terms are positive):

cosθ≤θsinθ​≤cosθ1​

As θ→0+: cosθ→1 and 1/cosθ→1. By the Squeeze Theorem:

limθ→0+​θsinθ​=1

The limit from the left follows by evenness of sinθ/θ. ■

Limit 2

limθ→0​θcosθ−1​=0

Proof. Multiply numerator and denominator by (cosθ+1):

θcosθ−1​=θ(cosθ+1)cos2θ−1​=θ(cosθ+1)−sin2θ​=−θsinθ​⋅cosθ+1sinθ​

As θ→0: the first factor →1 (Limit 1), the second factor →0/2=0. Hence the product →0. ■

These two limits are the only non-algebraic facts needed. Everything else follows from the product rule, quotient rule, and chain rule.

Proof: (sinx)′=cosx

dxd​sinx=limΔx→0​Δxsin(x+Δx)−sinx​

Apply the angle addition formula sin(x+Δx)=sinxcosΔx+cosxsinΔx:

=limΔx→0​ΔxsinxcosΔx+cosxsinΔx−sinx​

=limΔx→0​[sinx⋅ΔxcosΔx−1​+cosx⋅ΔxsinΔx​]

Apply Limits 1 and 2:

=sinx⋅0+cosx⋅1=cosx■

Proof: (cosx)′=−sinx

dxd​cosx=limΔx→0​Δxcos(x+Δx)−cosx​

Apply cos(x+Δx)=cosxcosΔx−sinxsinΔx:

=limΔx→0​[cosx⋅ΔxcosΔx−1​−sinx⋅ΔxsinΔx​]

=cosx⋅0−sinx⋅1=−sinx■

Proofs: The Four Derived Trigonometric Functions

These follow from the quotient rule — no new limits required.

(tanx)′=sec2x

(tanx)′=(cosxsinx​)′=cos2xcosx⋅cosx−sinx⋅(−sinx)​=cos2xcos2x+sin2x​=cos2x1​=sec2x

(cotx)′=−csc2x

(cotx)′=(sinxcosx​)′=sin2x−sinx⋅sinx−cosx⋅cosx​=sin2x−(sin2x+cos2x)​=−sin2x1​=−csc2x

(secx)′=secxtanx

(secx)′=(cosx1​)′=cos2x0⋅cosx−1⋅(−sinx)​=cos2xsinx​=cosx1​⋅cosxsinx​=secxtanx

(cscx)′=−cscxcotx

(cscx)′=(sinx1​)′=sin2x0⋅sinx−1⋅cosx​=−sin2xcosx​=−sinx1​⋅sinxcosx​=−cscxcotx

The Differentiation Cycle

Differentiating sinx repeatedly cycles through four functions:

sinxd/dx​cosxd/dx​−sinxd/dx​−cosxd/dx​sinxd/dx​⋯

The n-th derivative of sinx:

dxndn​sinx=sin!(x+2nπ​)

Proof. Each differentiation adds π/2 to the phase argument:

dxd​sin(x+ϕ)=cos(x+ϕ)=sin!(x+ϕ+2π​)

Applying this n times starting from ϕ=0 gives the result. ■

Implication for differential equations: y=sinx satisfies y′′=−y, i.e., y′′+y=0. This is the simple harmonic oscillator equation. Trig functions are eigenfunctions of d2/dx2 with eigenvalue −1.

Key observations:

  • Where sinx is at its maximum (x=π/2), cosx=0 — the slope of sine is zero at its peak. ✓
  • Where sinx is increasing most steeply (x=0), cosx=1 — the slope is exactly 1. ✓
  • Where sinx crosses zero from positive to negative (x=π), cosx=−1 — maximum descent rate. ✓

This is the geometric content of (sinx)′=cosx.

  ··· sec²x (always ≥ 1, spikes near asymptotes)

Observation: sec2x≥1 for all x in the domain. This means tanx is strictly increasing wherever it is defined — it has no local extrema.

Computed Examples

Example 1 — Chain Rule with Trig

h(x)=sin(3x2+1)

Let u=3x2+1. Then:

h′(x)=cos(3x2+1)⋅6x=6xcos(3x2+1)

Example 2 — Product Rule with Trig

h(x)=excosx

h′(x)=excosx+ex(−sinx)=ex(cosx−sinx)

Critical points: h′(x)=0⇒cosx=sinx⇒x=π/4+nπ.

This function arises in damped oscillations — the envelope ex grows while cosx oscillates.

Example 3 — Quotient Rule + Trig

h(x)=1+cosxsinx​

h′(x)=(1+cosx)2cosx(1+cosx)−sinx(−sinx)​=(1+cosx)2cosx+cos2x+sin2x​=(1+cosx)2cosx+1​=1+cosx1​

A surprisingly clean result — the Pythagorean identity sin2x+cos2x=1 does the heavy lifting.

Example 4 — Higher-Order Derivative

Find f(47)(x) where f(x)=cosx.

The cycle for cosx has period 4:

cosx, −sinx, −cosx, sinx, cosx, …

47=4×11+3, so f(47)=f(3)=−sinx.

dx47d47​cosx=−sinx

Example 5 — Implicit Differentiation

Given sin(xy)=x, find dy/dx.

Differentiate both sides with respect to x (chain + product rule on the left):

cos(xy)⋅(y+xdxdy​)=1

xcos(xy)dxdy​=1−ycos(xy)

dxdy​=xcos(xy)1−ycos(xy)​

Taylor Expansions — What the Derivatives Encode

The Taylor series at x=0:

sinx=x−3!x3​+5!x5​−7!x7​+⋯=∑n=0∞​(2n+1)!(−1)nx2n+1​

cosx=1−2!x2​+4!x4​−6!x6​+⋯=∑n=0∞​(2n)!(−1)nx2n​

The derivative relationship is self-consistent: differentiating the series for sinx term-by-term yields exactly the series for cosx. This is not a coincidence — it is the definition of the exponential function in disguise, via Euler’s formula: eix=cosx+isinx.

The small-angle approximation sinx≈x (first-order Taylor term) is one of the most used approximations in physics and engineering — and in the linearization arguments underlying neural network gradient flow analysis.

Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

1. Activation Functions — Sinusoidal Representations (SIREN)

The paper Implicit Neural Representations with Periodic Activation Functions (Sitzmann et al., 2020) introduced SIREN networks, which use sin as the activation function:

ϕi​(x)=sin(Wi​x+bi​)

The derivative of a SIREN layer is:

∂x∂ϕi​​=Wi⊤​cos(Wi​x+bi​)

This is the chain rule applied to sin: the gradient is cos of the pre-activation, scaled by the weights. SIREN networks can represent signals, images, and solutions to PDEs with extraordinary precision — precisely because sin is infinitely differentiable and its derivative is itself (up to phase).

2. Positional Encodings in Transformers

The original Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) encodes sequence position using:

PE(pos,,2i)​=sin!(100002i/dpos​),PE(pos,,2i+1)​=cos!(100002i/dpos​)

The gradient of the loss with respect to the position encoding — needed during training — involves exactly (sinx)′=cosx and (cosx)′=−sinx via the chain rule. Understanding how position information flows through backpropagation requires knowing these derivatives.

3. Fourier Features and Random Fourier Features

Random Fourier Features (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) approximate kernel functions by mapping inputs through:

z(x)=2​cos(ω⊤x+b),ω∼p(ω)

The gradient used in feature learning:

∇x​z(x)=−2​,ωsin(ω⊤x+b)

Again: (cosu)′=−sinu via the chain rule, u=ω⊤x+b.

4. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs solve differential equations by training a neural network to satisfy the PDE. Many PDEs involve trig functions (wave equation, heat equation with periodic boundary conditions). The residual loss includes terms like:

Lpde​=​∂t2∂2uθ​​−c2∂x2∂2uθ​​​2

where uθ​ is the network output. Computing these second-order derivatives through a network with sinusoidal activations requires the full differentiation cycle of trig functions — including (sinx)′′=−sinx.

5. Gradient Flow in Recurrent Networks — Vanishing Gradients

In RNNs, the hidden state update often involves tanh — which is related to sin via:

tanh(x)=ex+e−xex−e−x​,tanh′(x)=1−tanh2(x)=sech2(x)

The hyperbolic secant squared sech2(x) is the hyperbolic analogue of sec2(x)=(tanx)′. The vanishing gradient problem in RNNs is partly explained by sech2(x)∈(0,1] — gradients are always compressed, exactly as sec2(x)≥1 means tan‘s gradient is always expanded.

6. Optimization Landscapes — Loss Surface Periodicity

Some loss surfaces in neural networks exhibit approximate periodicity due to:

  • Weight sharing (CNNs, attention heads)
  • Sinusoidal positional encodings
  • Fourier-based architectures

Analyzing these landscapes requires understanding how sin and cos behave under differentiation — specifically, that their derivatives are bounded (∣sin′∣≤1, ∣cos′∣≤1), which implies Lipschitz continuity with constant 1. This is a regularity property that affects convergence guarantees in optimization.

The Pythagorean Identity as a Derivative Constraint

The identity sin2x+cos2x=1 is not independent of the derivative relationship — it is preserved by differentiation in a precise sense.

Differentiate both sides:

2sinxcosx+2cosx(−sinx)=0✓

The derivative rules are consistent with the algebraic identity. This is a non-trivial self-consistency check: if the derivative formulas were wrong, this identity would fail under differentiation.

The radian caveat is serious. The limit limθ→0​sinθ/θ=1 holds only in radians. In degrees, the limit is π/180. This is why radians are the natural unit for calculus — and why neural network implementations always work in radians internally.
