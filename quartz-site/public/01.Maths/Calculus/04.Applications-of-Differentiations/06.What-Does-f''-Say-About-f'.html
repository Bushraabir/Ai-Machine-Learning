06.What Does f'' Say About f'

06.What Does f'' Say About f'

Apr 17, 202610 min read

“The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative — but it speaks a deeper language: it tells you how fast the slope itself is changing.” — Personal note, drawn from Stewart §4.3 and MIT 18.02

0. Setup & Notation

Let f:I→R be twice differentiable on an open interval I. Define:

f′(x)=limh→0​hf(x+h)−f(x)​,f′′(x)=(f′)′(x)=limh→0​hf′(x+h)−f′(x)​

The question is: what does knowing f′′ tell us about f′?

The answer operates on two levels:

  1. Analytical: f′′ is the rate of change of f′ — just as f′ is the rate of change of f.
  2. Geometric: f′′ controls the concavity of f, i.e., whether the slope f′ is increasing or decreasing.

1. The Fundamental Relationship: f′′ as the Rate of Change of f′

Theorem 1.1 (Sign of f′′ and Monotonicity of f′)

Let f be twice differentiable on (a,b).

  • If f′′(x)>0 for all x∈(a,b), then f′ is strictly increasing on (a,b).
  • If f′′(x)<0 for all x∈(a,b), then f′ is strictly decreasing on (a,b).
  • If f′′(x)=0 for all x∈(a,b), then f′ is constant on (a,b) (i.e., f is linear).

Proof. Apply the First Derivative Test to f′: since (f′)′=f′′, the sign of f′′ determines whether f′ is monotone, by the standard theorem that a positive derivative implies a strictly increasing function. ■

This is the core insight. Everything else follows from this.

2. Concavity: The Geometric Translation

Definition 2.1 (Concavity)

f is concave up on (a,b) if for all x,y∈(a,b) and t∈(0,1): f(tx+(1−t)y)≤tf(x)+(1−t)f(y)

Geometrically: the chord lies above the curve. The tangent lines lie below the curve.

f is concave down if the reverse inequality holds.

Theorem 2.2 (Second Derivative Test for Concavity) (Stewart §4.3)

  • f′′(x)>0 on (a,b) ⇒ f is concave up on (a,b).
  • f′′(x)<0 on (a,b) ⇒ f is concave down on (a,b).

Proof sketch. Fix c∈(a,b). The tangent line at c is L(x)=f(c)+f′(c)(x−c). Define g(x)=f(x)−L(x). Then g(c)=0, g′(c)=0, and g′′(x)=f′′(x)>0. By Theorem 1.1, g′ is increasing near c. Since g′(c)=0: g′(x)<0 for x<c and g′(x)>0 for x>c near c. So g has a local minimum at c, hence g(x)≥g(c)=0. This means f(x)≥L(x): the tangent line lies below f. ■

3. Inflection Points: Where f′′ Changes Sign

Definition 3.1 (Inflection Point)

A point c is an inflection point of f if:

  1. f is continuous at c, and
  2. f′′ changes sign at c (concavity reverses).

Critical nuance: f′′(c)=0 is necessary but not sufficient for an inflection point.

Example 3.2 (The Classic Failure)

f(x)=x4,f′′(x)=12x2

f′′(0)=0, yet f′′ does not change sign at 0 — it remains ≥0 everywhere. So x=0 is not an inflection point. The curve is concave up on all of R.

Example 3.3 (Genuine Inflection)

f(x)=x3,f′(x)=3x2,f′′(x)=6x

  • f′′(x)<0 for x<0: concave down, f′ is decreasing.
  • f′′(0)=0: inflection point.
  • f′′(x)>0 for x>0: concave up, f′ is increasing.

The slope f′(x)=3x2 reaches its minimum at x=0 (it decreases then increases), consistent with f′′ changing from − to +.

4. The Second Derivative Test for Local Extrema

This is perhaps the most used consequence. It exploits f′′‘s information about f′ near a critical point.

Theorem 4.1 (Second Derivative Test) (Stewart §4.3)

Suppose f′(c)=0 and f′′(c) exists.

  • If f′′(c)>0: f has a local minimum at c.
  • If f′′(c)<0: f has a local maximum at c.
  • If f′′(c)=0: the test is inconclusive.

Proof. If f′(c)=0 and f′′(c)>0: by continuity of f′′, there exists δ>0 such that f′′(x)>0 on (c−δ,c+δ). By Theorem 1.1, f′ is increasing on this interval. Since f′(c)=0:

  • For x∈(c−δ,c): f′(x)<f′(c)=0, so f is decreasing.
  • For x∈(c,c+δ): f′(x)>f′(c)=0, so f is increasing.

Hence f(c) is a local minimum. ■

Why the test fails when f′′(c)=0: Consider f(x)=x4 (local min at 0), g(x)=−x4 (local max at 0), h(x)=x3 (saddle/inflection at 0). All have f′′(0)=0.

6. Worked Example: Complete Analysis of f(x)=x3−3x

(This is the standard example from Stewart §4.3 and MIT 18.02)

f(x)=x3−3x f′(x)=3x2−3=3(x−1)(x+1) f′′(x)=6x

Step 1. Critical points: f′(x)=0⇒x=±1.

Step 2. Apply Second Derivative Test:

  • f′′(−1)=−6<0: local maximum at x=−1, value f(−1)=2.
  • f′′(1)=6>0: local minimum at x=1, value f(1)=−2.

Step 3. Concavity and inflection:

  • f′′(x)<0 for x<0: concave down (slope decreasing).
  • f′′(0)=0 and sign change: inflection point at x=0.
  • f′′(x)>0 for x>0: concave up (slope increasing).

Sketch of f′(x)=3x2−3 alongside f′′(x)=6x:

f'(x):                     f''(x):
      *           *                   /
       *         *              -----/-----
        *       *              /
         *     *          (f'' = 0 at origin)
          *   *
           * *
            *   ← minimum of f' at x=0
                  (where f'' changes sign)

The minimum of f′ occurs at x=0, which is precisely where f′′=0 and changes sign. This is the inflection point of f. Perfect consistency.

7. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is not a detour — this is where the second derivative becomes genuinely powerful in modern computational mathematics.

7.1 Gradient Descent and the Role of f′′

In machine learning, we minimize a loss function L(θ) over parameters θ∈Rn. The standard update rule is:

θt+1​=θt​−η∇θ​L(θt​)

This uses only ∇L (the first derivative). But the curvature — governed by f′′ — critically affects convergence:

  • If f′′≫0 (high curvature): a fixed learning rate η causes oscillation or divergence.
  • If f′′≈0 (flat region): gradient descent moves painfully slowly.

The ideal step size at each point is η≈f′′(x)1​ — larger curvature demands a smaller step.

7.2 Newton’s Method: Explicitly Using f′′

Newton’s method for optimization is:

θt+1​=θt​−f′′(θt​)f′(θt​)​

In higher dimensions, this becomes: θt+1​=θt​−H−1∇L(θt​)

where H=∇2L is the Hessian matrix — the multivariable analogue of f′′. Each entry Hij​=∂θi​∂θj​∂2L​ describes how the gradient in direction i changes as you move in direction j.

Newton’s method converges quadratically (vs. linearly for gradient descent) because it accounts for curvature. The cost: computing and inverting H for a network with n parameters is O(n2) storage and O(n3) inversion — infeasible for modern deep networks with n∼109.

7.3 Saddle Points in Deep Learning

A critical point where f′(c)=0 but f′′(c)=0 is called a saddle point in higher dimensions (or inflection-type point in 1D). In the loss landscapes of deep neural networks:

  • Local minima are relatively rare in high dimensions.
  • Saddle points are ubiquitous — the gradient vanishes, the Hessian has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

Standard gradient descent can get stuck near saddle points because the gradient becomes tiny. This is one reason why momentum methods (Adam, RMSProp) were developed — they use accumulated gradient history to escape flat regions.

The sign of f′′ (or eigenvalues of the Hessian) determines whether a critical point is:

  • A minimum: all eigenvalues >0 (positive definite Hessian — f′′-analogue)
  • A maximum: all eigenvalues <0
  • A saddle: mixed signs

7.4 Convexity and Loss Landscapes

A function L is convex if and only if f′′(θ)≥0 everywhere (in 1D). Convexity guarantees:

  • Every local minimum is a global minimum.
  • Gradient descent converges to the global optimum.

Deep neural network loss functions are highly non-convex — f′′ changes sign constantly, creating a rugged landscape of hills, valleys, and saddle points. Understanding where f′′>0 vs. f′′<0 in parameter space is an active research problem (loss landscape geometry).

8. Rigorous Note: The Converse and Edge Cases

Remark 8.1. The converse of Theorem 2.2 is false: a function can be concave up at a point where f′′(c)=0. Example: f(x)=x4 is concave up on all of R, yet f′′(0)=0.

Remark 8.2. A function can have an inflection point where f′′ fails to exist. Example: f(x)=x1/3 has an inflection at x=0 but f′′(0) does not exist.

Remark 8.3. The condition ”f′′ changes sign” is the correct definition of inflection — not merely "f′′(c)=0". Always verify sign change.
