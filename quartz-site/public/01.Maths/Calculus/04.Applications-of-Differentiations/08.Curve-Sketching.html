“A picture is worth a thousand symbols — but only if you know what to look for.”

Preface: Why Bother Sketching?

Before numerical solvers and plotting libraries existed, mathematicians had to understand a function before they could draw it. Curve sketching is the discipline of extracting the complete qualitative story of f(x) from its formula alone — without evaluating hundreds of points. It is, at its heart, a systematic interrogation of a function.

This note follows the classical treatment of Stewart §4.5 and the spirit of MIT 18.02, building up the full algorithmic pipeline from domain analysis to asymptotic behavior to the precise shape of local extrema.

The Seven-Step Protocol

Given f:D⊆R→R, a complete sketch requires answering these questions in order:

StepQuestionTool
1Where is f defined?Domain analysis
2Does f have symmetry?Even/odd test
3Where does f cross the axes?Intercepts
4What happens at the boundary of D?Asymptotes
5Where is f increasing/decreasing?Sign of f′
6Where are the peaks and valleys?Critical points + 1st/2nd derivative test
7Where does f bend?Sign of f′′, inflection points

Work through all seven before committing a single curve to paper.

Step 1 — Domain

The domain D is the maximal subset of R on which f(x) is defined. Standard obstructions:

  • Division by zero: f(x)=x−21​ excludes x=2.
  • Even roots of negatives: f(x)=x2−1​ requires ∣x∣≥1.
  • Logarithms: f(x)=ln(x) requires x>0.

Principle. Determine the domain first. Every subsequent step is restricted to D.

Step 2 — Symmetry

Definition. A function f is:

  • Even if f(−x)=f(x) for all x∈D (symmetric about the y-axis).
  • Odd if f(−x)=−f(x) for all x∈D (symmetric about the origin).

Exploiting symmetry reduces the domain you need to analyze by half — sketch on [0,∞), then reflect.

Examples:

  • f(x)=x4−3x2+1 is even (all even powers).
  • f(x)=x3−x is odd (all odd powers).
  • f(x)=x3+x2 is neither.

Step 3 — Intercepts

y-intercept: Set x=0, compute f(0) (only if 0∈D).

x-intercepts (zeros): Solve f(x)=0. This can be hard in general; use what algebra allows.

Remark. Do not spend excessive time hunting zeros unless they are obvious. The shape of the curve matters more than precise root locations for a sketch.

Step 4 — Asymptotes

4.1 Vertical Asymptotes

x=a is a vertical asymptote if limx→a+​f(x)=±∞ or limx→a−​f(x)=±∞.

Candidates: points where the denominator vanishes and the numerator does not.

Example. f(x)=x2−1x​. The denominator vanishes at x=±1.

limx→1+​x2−1x​=+∞,limx→1−​x2−1x​=−∞

So x=1 is a vertical asymptote (similarly x=−1).

4.2 Horizontal Asymptotes

y=L is a horizontal asymptote if limx→±∞​f(x)=L.

Rational function rule: f(x)=bm​xm+⋯an​xn+⋯​⟹{y=0​n<m y=an​/bm​​n=m no H.A.​n>m​

4.3 Oblique (Slant) Asymptotes

When the degree of the numerator exceeds the denominator by exactly 1, divide:

f(x)=x−1x2+1​=x+1+x−12​

The line y=x+1 is an oblique asymptote as x→±∞.

Step 5 — Intervals of Increase and Decrease

Theorem (Monotonicity Test). Let f be differentiable on (a,b).

  • If f′(x)>0 on (a,b), then f is strictly increasing on [a,b].
  • If f′(x)<0 on (a,b), then f is strictly decreasing on [a,b].

Procedure:

  1. Compute f′(x).
  2. Find all critical numbers c where f′(c)=0 or f′(c) does not exist.
  3. Use these to partition D into intervals.
  4. Test the sign of f′ on each interval (pick any sample point).

Step 6 — Local Extrema

Definition. f has a local maximum at c if f(c)≥f(x) for all x near c, and a local minimum if f(c)≤f(x) for all x near c.

First Derivative Test

Suppose c is a critical number of f.

  • If f′ changes from + to − at c: local max.
  • If f′ changes from − to + at c: local min.
  • If f′ does not change sign: neither (saddle).

Second Derivative Test

Suppose f′(c)=0 and f′′(c) exists.

  • f′′(c)>0⇒ local minimum (curve is concave up, so c is the bottom of a bowl).
  • f′′(c)<0⇒ local maximum (curve is concave down, so c is the top of a hill).
  • f′′(c)=0⇒ inconclusive (must revert to first derivative test).

Mnemonic. A smiley face ∪ is concave up (f′′>0); a frowny face ∩ is concave down (f′′<0).

Step 7 — Concavity and Inflection Points

Definition. f is concave up on (a,b) if f′′(x)>0 there; concave down if f′′(x)<0 there.

Definition. c is an inflection point if f is continuous at c and the concavity changes at c.

Warning. f′′(c)=0 is necessary but not sufficient for an inflection point. Check that concavity actually changes.

Example. f(x)=x4: f′′(x)=12x2, so f′′(0)=0, but concavity is up on both sides — x=0 is not an inflection point.

Complete Worked Example

f(x)=x2−4x2−1​

Step 1: Domain

Denominator =0 when x=±2. So D=R∖−2,2.

Step 2: Symmetry

f(−x)=(−x)2−4(−x)2−1​=f(x). Even function — sketch for x≥0 and reflect.

Step 3: Intercepts

  • y-intercept: f(0)=−4−1​=41​.
  • x-intercepts: x2−1=0⇒x=±1.

Step 4: Asymptotes

Vertical: x=±2.

Check signs near x=2: limx→2+​x2−4x2−1​:numerator→3>0,denominator→0+⇒+∞ limx→2−​x2−4x2−1​:denominator→0−⇒−∞

Horizontal: limx→±∞​x2−4x2−1​=limx→∞​1−4/x21−1/x2​=1 So y=1 is a horizontal asymptote.

Step 5: Monotonicity

f′(x)=(x2−4)22x(x2−4)−(x2−1)(2x)​=(x2−4)22x[(x2−4)−(x2−1)]​=(x2−4)2−6x​

Critical point: f′(x)=0⇒x=0.

Sign analysis of f′(x)=(x2−4)2−6x​ (denominator always ≥0, denominator =0 excluded):

IntervalSign of −6xf′Behavior
(−∞,−2)++increasing
(−2,0)++increasing
(0,2)−−decreasing
(2,∞)−−decreasing

Step 6: Extrema

f′ changes from + to − at x=0: local maximum at (0,1/4).

Confirm: f′′(0) — or just note the sign change.

Step 7: Concavity

f′′(x)=(x2−4)4−6(x2−4)2−(−6x)⋅2(x2−4)(2x)​=(x2−4)3−6(x2−4)+24x2​=(x2−4)318x2+24​

The numerator 18x2+24>0 always. So the sign of f′′ is the sign of (x2−4)3=(x−2)3(x+2)3:

Region(x2−4)3Concavity
$x> 2$
$x< 2$

No inflection points (concavity doesn’t change within a continuous piece of the domain).

Visualization: Reading the Derivative Portrait

One of the most powerful mental models in curve sketching is the derivative portrait — aligning f, f′, and f′′ visually.

f(x):  rises /    peaks ⌒    falls \    valley ⌣    rises /
f'(x): positive    zero      negative     zero      positive
f''(x): ?         negative    ?          positive      ?

Wherever f′′ switches sign, that’s an inflection point — the graph transitions from bending left to bending right (or vice versa). In practice, when sketching by hand, draw the f′ sign chart and f′′ sign chart on the same number line as f to keep track.

The graph is an S-curve through the origin, rising steeply on both ends, with a local max on the left and local min on the right.

Example 2: Rational with Hole — f(x)=x−2x2−4​

f(x)=x−2(x−2)(x+2)​=x+2,x=2

This is a line with a hole (removable discontinuity) at (2,4). No asymptote — merely a gap. The sketch is a straight line with an open circle at x=2.

This example is a reminder: factor before jumping to conclusions about asymptotes.

Example 3: f(x)=xe−x

f′(x)=e−x−xe−x=e−x(1−x)

Critical point: x=1 (local max since f′ changes +→−). f(1)=e−1≈0.368.

f′′(x)=e−x(x−2)

Inflection at x=2: f(2)=2e−2≈0.271.

As x→+∞: f(x)→0 (exponential beats polynomial). As x→−∞: f(x)→−∞.

This curve starts from −∞ on the left, rises through the origin, peaks at (1,e−1), inflects at (2,2e−2), then decays to 0 — a beautiful unimodal shape that appears constantly in probability (it’s related to the Gamma distribution) and in ML.

Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is not a detour — it is the whole point of why any of this matters.

1. Loss Landscapes and Gradient Descent

The central problem in training a neural network is minimizing a loss function L(θ) over a high-dimensional parameter space θ∈Rn. In 1D, this is exactly curve sketching.

Gradient descent updates: θt+1​=θt​−η⋅L′(θt​)

This is navigating the curve L by following −f′. The algorithm:

  • follows f′ to find descent directions (Step 5 above)
  • gets stuck at f′(c)=0 — which can be a minimum, maximum, or saddle point (Step 6 above)
  • uses f′′ (curvature) in second-order methods like Newton’s method and natural gradient descent

The entire theory of optimization in ML is the multivariable generalization of Steps 5–7.

2. The Sigmoid and Its Derivative

The sigmoid function: σ(x)=1+e−x1​

is one of the most important activation functions. Its curve-sketching analysis:

  • Domain: R
  • Range: (0,1)
  • Horizontal asymptotes: y=0 as x→−∞, y=1 as x→+∞
  • Inflection point at x=0 (where σ(0)=1/2)
  • σ′(x)=σ(x)(1−σ(x)), which peaks at x=0 with value 1/4

The vanishing gradient problem in deep networks is visible directly from the sketch: away from x=0, σ′(x)→0. When gradients are multiplied across many layers, this near-zero derivative causes the gradient signal to vanish. This is why ReLU (f(x)=max(0,x), with f′(x)=1 for x>0) replaced sigmoid in deep networks — its derivative does not decay.

3. Convexity and Optimization Guarantees

Definition. f is convex if for all x,y in the domain and λ∈[0,1]: f(λx+(1−λ)y)≤λf(x)+(1−λ)f(y)

Equivalently (for twice-differentiable f): f′′≥0 everywhere.

Why it matters in ML: If L is convex, every local minimum is a global minimum, and gradient descent is guaranteed to converge. Logistic regression and SVMs have convex losses. Deep neural networks do not — their loss landscapes are highly non-convex, which is why training requires careful initialization, momentum, and learning rate scheduling.

Understanding convexity is Step 7 of curve sketching applied to loss functions.

4. Newton’s Method — The Second-Derivative Optimizer

Newton’s method for finding zeros of f′ (i.e., critical points of f): θt+1​=θt​−f′′(θt​)f′(θt​)​

In n dimensions, this becomes: θt+1​=θt​−H−1∇L

where H is the Hessian (matrix of second derivatives). This is the basis of quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS), natural gradient methods, and Fisher information in deep learning — all of which use curvature information (f′′) to take smarter steps than vanilla gradient descent.

5. Activation Function Design

Every nonlinear activation function in deep learning is designed with curve-sketching principles:

Each design decision traces back to understanding what the curve looks like — its range, its derivative behavior, its inflection structure.

6. Fourier Analysis and Neural Networks (a bridge)

MIT 18.02 motivates much of its analysis through the idea that complex curves can be decomposed into simpler components. Modern deep networks approximate arbitrary functions by composing simple nonlinearities — a continuous analog of the same idea. Universal approximation theorems guarantee that sufficiently wide networks with non-polynomial activations can approximate any continuous function on a compact set — but the quality of that approximation depends on the smoothness structure of the target function, which is precisely the language of curve sketching.

The Derivative Sign Chart: A Practical Tool

For any function, maintain a sign chart as you work:

x:         -∞    -2    -1     0     1     2    +∞
f'(x):           +++   +++   ---   ---  
f''(x):          ---   +++   +++   ---   
f:          ↗     ↗     ↘     ↘
concavity:   ∩     ∪     ∪     ∩

This single diagram encodes the entire qualitative behavior of the function before you draw a single point.

Summary: The Checklist

  • Domain — where is f defined?
  • Symmetry — even, odd, or neither?
  • Intercepts — f(0) and zeros of f
  • Vertical asymptotes — zeros of denominator
  • Horizontal/oblique asymptotes — limits at ±∞
  • f′(x) — compute, find critical numbers, sign chart
  • Local extrema — first or second derivative test
  • f′′(x) — compute, find inflection candidates, sign chart
  • Inflection points — confirm sign change of f′′
  • Sketch — place all features, connect with smooth curve
