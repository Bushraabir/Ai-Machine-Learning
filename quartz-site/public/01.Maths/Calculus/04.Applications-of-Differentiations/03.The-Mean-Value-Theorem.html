03.The Mean Value Theorem

Apr 17, 202611 min read

  • calculus
  • analysis
  • MVT
  • optimization
  • deeplearning

“The Mean Value Theorem is the midwife of calculus — it delivers the deep connection between a function’s local behavior and its global change.” — Personal marginal note

§1. Motivation — Why Should We Care?

Before the theorem, a question:

If you drive from Dhaka to Chattogram — a distance of roughly 250 km — in 3 hours, must there have been some instant when your speedometer read exactly 3250​≈83.3 km/h?

Intuitively: yes. You can’t average 83.3 km/h without ever hitting that speed.

The Mean Value Theorem is precisely the theorem that makes this rigorous. It is, at its core, a theorem about the relationship between average rate of change (slope of a secant) and instantaneous rate of change (slope of a tangent).

§2. Prerequisites

Before the MVT, we need two foundational results. I’ll state them and prove the first in full.

2.1 Rolle’s Theorem

Theorem (Rolle, 1691). Let f be a function satisfying:

  1. f is continuous on [a,b]
  2. f is differentiable on (a,b)
  3. f(a)=f(b)

Then there exists at least one c∈(a,b) such that f′(c)=0.

Proof.

Since f is continuous on the closed bounded interval [a,b], by the Extreme Value Theorem (Stewart §4.1), f attains both an absolute maximum M and an absolute minimum m on [a,b].

Case 1: M=m. Then f is constant on [a,b], so f′(x)=0 for all x∈(a,b). Any c∈(a,b) works. ✓

Case 2: M=m. Since f(a)=f(b), at least one of M or m must be attained at an interior point c∈(a,b) (not just at the endpoints).

At such a c, f has a local extremum. Since f is differentiable at c, by Fermat’s Theorem (Stewart §4.1): f′(c)=0

□

Geometric picture:

    f(a) = f(b)
      *-----------*
     /  horizontal \
    /   tangent     \
   /        *        \
  /        (c, f(c))  \
 a                    b

Somewhere between a and b, the curve must “turn around,” and at that turning point the tangent is horizontal.

2.2 The Mean Value Theorem

Theorem (Lagrange’s MVT). Let f be a function satisfying:

  1. f is continuous on [a,b]
  2. f is differentiable on (a,b)

Then there exists at least one c∈(a,b) such that: f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​​

This is the theorem. Elegant. The right-hand side is the slope of the secant line through (a,f(a)) and (b,f(b)). The theorem asserts: the tangent line at c is parallel to this secant.

§3. Proof of the MVT

The proof is a beautiful reduction to Rolle’s Theorem. The key idea: subtract the secant line from f to create a new function that satisfies Rolle’s hypotheses.

Construction. Define the secant line through (a,f(a)) and (b,f(b)): L(x)=f(a)+b−af(b)−f(a)​(x−a)

Now define: g(x)=f(x)−L(x)=f(x)−f(a)−b−af(b)−f(a)​(x−a)

Verify Rolle’s conditions:

  1. g is continuous on [a,b] — yes, f is continuous and L is a polynomial (hence continuous). ✓
  2. g is differentiable on (a,b) — yes, f is differentiable and L′(x)=b−af(b)−f(a)​ exists. ✓
  3. Check endpoints: g(a)=f(a)−f(a)−0=0 g(b)=f(b)−f(a)−b−af(b)−f(a)​(b−a)=f(b)−f(a)−(f(b)−f(a))=0

So g(a)=g(b)=0. ✓

Apply Rolle’s Theorem: ∃,c∈(a,b) such that g′(c)=0.

Compute g′: g′(x)=f′(x)−b−af(b)−f(a)​

Setting g′(c)=0: f′(c)−b−af(b)−f(a)​=0⟹f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​

□

The geometric story of the proof:

         f(b)
          *
         /|  ← secant line L(x)
        / |
   f(c)/  |
  ....*.  |   ← tangent parallel to secant
      /   |
     /    |
f(a)*     |
    a  c  b

g(x) measures the vertical gap between f and the secant. At both endpoints this gap is 0. So somewhere in between, the gap must reach a maximum or minimum — and Rolle tells us g′(c)=0 there, which is exactly f′(c)= slope of secant.

§4. Worked Examples

Example 1 — The Driving Analogy Made Rigorous

Let s(t) = position (km) at time t (hours), with s(0)=0, s(3)=250.

Assume s is continuous on [0,3] and differentiable on (0,3) (reasonable physical assumption).

By MVT: ∃,c∈(0,3) such that: s′(c)=3−0s(3)−s(0)​=3250​≈83.3 km/h

Since s′(t) is velocity, the speedometer must have read exactly 83.3 km/h at some moment t=c.

This is not just intuition anymore — it’s a theorem.

Example 2 — A Polynomial

Let f(x)=x3−x on [0,2].

Secant slope: 2−0f(2)−f(0)​=2(8−2)−0​=26​=3

MVT guarantees ∃,c∈(0,2) with f′(c)=3.

f′(x)=3x2−1. Solve: 3c2−1=3⟹3c2=4⟹c=3​2​=323​​≈1.155

Indeed c≈1.155∈(0,2). ✓

Note: MVT guarantees existence — it doesn’t say c is unique. Here it happens to be unique in (0,2), but for other functions there could be multiple such c.

Example 3 — Using MVT to Prove an Inequality

Claim: ∣sinb−sina∣≤∣b−a∣ for all a,b∈R.

Proof. If a=b this is trivial. Assume WLOG a<b.

f(x)=sinx is differentiable everywhere. By MVT, ∃,c∈(a,b): sinb−sina=f′(c)(b−a)=cos(c)⋅(b−a)

Taking absolute values: ∣sinb−sina∣=∣cosc∣⋅∣b−a∣≤1⋅∣b−a∣=∣b−a∣

since ∣cosc∣≤1 always. □

This is the power of MVT for inequalities: you convert a statement about a function’s values into a statement about its derivative, which is often easier to bound.

Example 4 — Uniqueness of Solutions

Claim: The equation x3+x=2 has exactly one real solution.

Existence: f(x)=x3+x−2 has f(0)=−2<0 and f(1)=0. So x=1 is a solution.

Uniqueness (by MVT / Rolle): Suppose there were two solutions a<b with f(a)=f(b)=0. By Rolle’s Theorem, ∃,c∈(a,b) with f′(c)=0.

But f′(x)=3x2+1≥1>0 for all x. Contradiction. ✗

Therefore x=1 is the unique solution. □

§5. Important Corollaries

These follow quickly from MVT and are used constantly.

Corollary 1 — Zero Derivative Implies Constant

If f′(x)=0 for all x∈(a,b), then f is constant on (a,b).

Proof. Take any x1​,x2​∈(a,b) with x1​<x2​. By MVT: f(x2​)−f(x1​)=f′(c)(x2​−x1​)=0

So f(x1​)=f(x2​). Since x1​,x2​ were arbitrary, f is constant. □

Corollary 2 — Equal Derivatives Imply Functions Differ by a Constant

If f′(x)=g′(x) for all x∈(a,b), then f(x)=g(x)+C for some constant C.

Proof. Apply Corollary 1 to h(x)=f(x)−g(x). □

This is the reason antiderivatives are defined up to a constant — a fact that underlies all of integral calculus.

Corollary 3 — Monotonicity

  • If f′(x)>0 on (a,b), then f is strictly increasing on [a,b].
  • If f′(x)<0 on (a,b), then f is strictly decreasing on [a,b].

Proof (increasing case). Take x1​<x2​ in [a,b]. By MVT, ∃,c with: f(x2​)−f(x1​)=f′(c)(x2​−x1​)>0

since f′(c)>0 and x2​−x1​>0. So f(x2​)>f(x1​). □

§6. The Generalized (Cauchy) MVT

Theorem (Cauchy’s MVT). Let f,g be continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and g′(x)=0 on (a,b). Then ∃,c∈(a,b): g′(c)f′(c)​=g(b)−g(a)f(b)−f(a)​

Taking g(x)=x recovers Lagrange’s MVT.

This is the key lemma behind L’Hôpital’s Rule. When computing limx→a​g(x)f(x)​ of the form 00​, Cauchy’s MVT provides the justification for replacing the ratio of functions with the ratio of derivatives.

§7. MVT in Higher Dimensions — A Glimpse (MIT 18.02)

In multivariable calculus, the MVT generalizes but requires care. For a function f:Rn→R:

MVT for scalar fields. If f is differentiable, then for points a,b∈Rn: f(b)−f(a)=∇f(c)⋅(b−a) for some c on the segment joining a and b.

This is a direct generalization: the gradient ∇f replaces f′, and the dot product replaces ordinary multiplication.

For vector-valued f:Rn→Rm, the exact MVT fails in general (there need not exist a single c). Instead, one uses the MVT inequality: ∣f(b)−f(a)∣≤supc∈ab​∣Df(c)∣⋅∣b−a∣

where Df is the Jacobian matrix. This inequality is fundamental in numerical analysis and functional analysis.

§8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is where the theorem stops being “pure math” and starts running your GPU.

8.1 Gradient Descent — The MVT Beneath Every Update

The backbone of training neural networks is gradient descent. Given a loss function L(θ) where θ∈Rp are the parameters:

θt+1​=θt​−η∇θ​L(θt​)

Why does this work? MVT tells us exactly:

L(θt+1​)−L(θt​)=∇L(c)⋅(θt+1​−θt​)

for some c between θt​ and θt+1​.

When the step is small, ∇L(c)≈∇L(θt​), so: L(θt+1​)−L(θt​)≈∇L(θt​)⋅(−η∇L(θt​))=−η∣∇L(θt​)∣2≤0

The loss decreases. The MVT is the mathematical reason gradient descent works — it licenses the approximation that a small step in the negative gradient direction reduces the objective.

8.2 Learning Rate and the Lipschitz Condition

The MVT motivates one of the most important practical choices in deep learning: choosing the learning rate η.

If ∇L is Lipschitz continuous with constant L: ∣∇L(θ)−∇L(ϕ)∣≤L∣θ−ϕ∣

Then by MVT-type reasoning, one can show: L(θt+1​)≤L(θt​)−(η−2Lη2​)∣∇L(θt​)∣2

The right-hand side is decreasing only when η<L2​.

Practical implication: If your learning rate is too large (bigger than L2​), gradient descent is theoretically not guaranteed to converge. This is why learning rate schedules and adaptive methods (Adam, RMSProp) exist — they are heuristic attempts to stay in the MVT-safe zone.

8.3 Backpropagation and the Chain Rule

The chain rule — the engine of backpropagation — is proved using the MVT. Recall:

dxd​[f(g(x))]=f′(g(x))⋅g′(x)

One rigorous proof of this fact proceeds by writing: hf(g(x+h))−f(g(x))​=g(x+h)−g(x)f(g(x+h))−f(g(x))​⋅hg(x+h)−g(x)​

and applying MVT to control the first factor as h→0.

In deep learning, every layer’s gradient computation is an application of this chain rule. The entire backpropagation algorithm is the chain rule applied recursively — and the chain rule’s proof leans on MVT.

8.4 Loss Landscape Analysis

Modern deep learning theory studies the loss landscape L:Rp→R of neural networks. Questions like:

  • Are there saddle points? Local minima? (Answered using critical point theory — ∇L=0, directly from MVT corollaries.)
  • How “flat” are the minima? (Studied via the Hessian H=∇2L, which is the second-order MVT — Taylor’s theorem.)

The second-order MVT (Taylor with remainder): L(θ+δ)=L(θ)+∇L(θ)⊤δ+21​δ⊤H(c)δ

is used in the analysis of Newton’s method, natural gradient descent, and second-order optimizers like K-FAC.

8.5 Generalization and Lipschitz Neural Networks

In learning theory, the generalization gap (difference between training and test error) can be bounded using the Lipschitz constant of the network. By MVT:

∣fθ​(x)−fθ​(x′)∣≤Lip(fθ​)⋅∣x−x′∣

Networks with small Lipschitz constants generalize better — this is the theoretical motivation behind spectral normalization (used in GANs) and gradient penalty methods. All of these bound or control the Lipschitz constant, which is defined via MVT.

§10. Hypotheses Are Not Optional

A common exam trap — and a deep conceptual point.

The MVT requires both continuity on [a,b] and differentiability on (a,b).

Counterexample 1 — Continuity fails: f(x)={0​x∈[0,1) 1​x=1​ 1−0f(1)−f(0)​=1, but f′(x)=0 wherever it exists. No c works.

Counterexample 2 — Differentiability fails: f(x)=∣x∣ on [−1,1] 2f(1)−f(−1)​=0, but f′(x)=±1=0 everywhere f′ is defined; f′(0) doesn’t exist.

The hypotheses in theorems are not bureaucratic formalities — they are load-bearing walls.
