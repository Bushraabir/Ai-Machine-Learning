14.Arc Length in Polar Coordinates

“Length is the most primitive geometric quantity. Before area, before curvature — you must be able to measure how long a curve is. In polar coordinates, this requires genuinely more work than in Cartesian, and the reward is a formula of surprising elegance.”

0. Motivation and Setup

The arc length of a curve is defined as the limit of the total length of inscribed polygonal approximations as the segments become infinitesimally fine. In Cartesian coordinates, this produces the familiar formula L=∫ab​1+(y′)2​,dx. In polar coordinates, we re-derive everything from the parametric viewpoint — and the derivation is the understanding.

A polar curve r=f(θ) is a parametric curve with parameter θ:

x(θ)=f(θ)cosθ,y(θ)=f(θ)sinθ

The parametric arc length formula is:

L=∫αβ​(dθdx​)2+(dθdy​)2​,dθ

Everything reduces to computing the integrand.

1. Derivation of the Polar Arc Length Formula

From the polar parametrization, compute the derivatives using the product rule:

dθdx​=f′(θ)cosθ−f(θ)sinθ

dθdy​=f′(θ)sinθ+f(θ)cosθ

Now square and add:

(dθdx​)2+(dθdy​)2

=[f′(θ)cosθ−f(θ)sinθ]2+[f′(θ)sinθ+f(θ)cosθ]2

Expand each square:

=[f′(θ)]2cos2θ−2f′(θ)f(θ)sinθcosθ+[f(θ)]2sin2θ +[f′(θ)]2sin2θ+2f′(θ)f(θ)sinθcosθ+[f(θ)]2cos2θ

The cross terms cancel. Collect:

=[f′(θ)]2(cos2θ+sin2θ)+[f(θ)]2(sin2θ+cos2θ)

=[f′(θ)]2+[f(θ)]2

Therefore:

L=∫αβ​r2+(dθdr​)2​,dθ​

where r=f(θ) and dr/dθ=f′(θ).

The cross-term cancellation is the key algebraic miracle. It is exactly the same identity that makes ∣eiθ∣2=cos2θ+sin2θ=1: the Pythagorean theorem absorbs all the mixed terms.

2. The Infinitesimal Arc Length Element

The integrand has a clean geometric interpretation. Define the arc length element in polar coordinates:

ds=r2+(dθdr​)2​,dθ

This can be understood via a polar coordinate version of the Pythagorean theorem. Consider a small change dθ:

  • The point moves along the circle r=const by arc r,dθ (tangential component).
  • The radius changes by dr=(dr/dθ),dθ (radial component).

These two infinitesimal displacements are perpendicular (the radial direction and the tangential direction on a circle are orthogonal). So by the Pythagorean theorem:

ds2=(r,dθ)2+(dr)2=r2,dθ2+(dθdr​)2dθ2=[r2+(dθdr​)2]dθ2

⇒ds=r2+(dθdr​)2​,dθ

All four are instances of the same idea: integrate the magnitude of the velocity vector along the curve.

4. Important Caution: Retracing

The arc length formula measures the total length of the path traced, including any retracing. If the curve r=f(θ) retraces itself as θ varies over [α,β], the formula overcounts the geometric length of the curve.

Example. The circle r=cosθ is fully traced for θ∈[0,π] (since r(0)=1, r(π/2)=0, r(π)=−1 → same as r=1 at θ=0 via alternative representation). If you integrate over [0,2π], you traverse the circle twice, getting L=2π instead of the correct circumference π.

Rule: Always identify the minimal period over which the curve is traced exactly once, and integrate over that interval.

For r=cosθ (circle): integrate over [0,π]. For r=cos(nθ) (one petal): integrate over [0,π/(2n)] (where r goes from 0 to 1 and back to 0… actually, let’s be careful below).

5. Worked Example 1: Circumference of the Circle r=a

r=a (constant), dr/dθ=0.

L=∫02π​a2+0​,dθ=a⋅2π=2πa

Trivially correct — the circumference of a circle of radius a. ✓

6. Worked Example 2: Arc Length of the Cardioid r=a(1+cosθ)

This is the canonical non-trivial example. It requires a half-angle identity.

r=a(1+cosθ), dθdr​=−asinθ.

Compute r2+(dr/dθ)2:

r2+(dθdr​)2=a2(1+cosθ)2+a2sin2θ

=a2[1+2cosθ+cos2θ+sin2θ]

=a2[2+2cosθ]=2a2(1+cosθ)

Now apply the half-angle identity: 1+cosθ=2cos2(θ/2).

r2+(dθdr​)2=4a2cos2!(2θ​)

Therefore:

L=∫02π​4a2cos2!(2θ​)​,dθ=∫02π​2a​cos!(2θ​)​,dθ

For θ∈[0,2π]: θ/2∈[0,π], and cos(θ/2)≥0 on [0,π]. So ∣cos(θ/2)∣=cos(θ/2) throughout. No absolute value issue here.

L=2a∫02π​cos!(2θ​)dθ=2a[2sin!(2θ​)]02π​=4a[sin(π)−sin(0)]=4a⋅1=4a⋅1

Wait: sin(π)=0. Let me recheck:

2a∫02π​cos!(2θ​)dθ=2a⋅[2sin!(2θ​)]02π​=4a[sin(π)−sin(0)]=4a[0−0]=0?

That cannot be right. Recheck the antiderivative: ∫cos(θ/2),dθ=2sin(θ/2)+C. So:

L=2a[2sin!(2θ​)]02π​=4a![sin(π)−sin(0)]=4a[0−0]=0

This is wrong because sin(π)=0, not 1. Let me redo more carefully.

∫02π​cos(θ/2),dθ=[2sin(θ/2)]02π​=2sin(π)−2sin(0)=0−0=0.

This is wrong because cosine is not always positive on [0,2π] after the substitution… but we said ∣cos(θ/2)∣=cos(θ/2) since θ/2∈[0,π] and cos is not always non-negative there.

Careful: cos(θ/2)≥0 iff θ/2∈[0,π/2], i.e., θ∈[0,π]. For θ∈(π,2π]: θ/2∈(π/2,π], so cos(θ/2)≤0.

Therefore ∣cos(θ/2)∣=cos(θ/2) everywhere. We must split the integral:

L=2a∫02π​​cos!(2θ​)​dθ=2a∫0π​cos!(2θ​)dθ+2a∫π2π​(−cos!(2θ​))dθ

By symmetry of ∣cos(θ/2)∣ about θ=π:

=2⋅2a∫0π​cos!(2θ​)dθ=4a[2sin!(2θ​)]0π​=4a[2sin(π/2)−2sin(0)]=4a[2⋅1]=8a

Lcardioid​=8a​

Sanity check. For a=1: the cardioid fits inside a circle of radius 2. Its perimeter 8 is less than 2π⋅2≈12.57. Plausible. ✓

7. Worked Example 3: Arc Length of One Petal of r=cos(2θ)

One petal of the 4-petal rose lies over θ∈[−π/4,π/4] (where r≥0).

dθdr​=−2sin(2θ).

r2+(dθdr​)2=cos2(2θ)+4sin2(2θ)=cos2(2θ)+4(1−cos2(2θ))

=4−3cos2(2θ)

Lpetal​=∫−π/4π/4​4−3cos2(2θ)​,dθ=2∫0π/4​4−3cos2(2θ)​,dθ

(used symmetry about θ=0). This integral does not reduce to elementary functions — it is an elliptic integral of the second kind.

Specifically, with the substitution u=2θ:

Lpetal​=∫0π/2​4−3cos2u​,du=2∫0π/2​1−43​cos2u​,du

=2∫0π/2​1−43​(1−sin2u)​,du=2∫0π/2​41​+43​sin2u​,du

This is 2E(k) for a specific modulus k — an elliptic integral. The numerical value is approximately Lpetal​≈1.211.

The lesson: most polar arc length integrals are not elementary. The cardioid is special because the half-angle identity collapses the integrand to a pure absolute value of cosine. Rose curves generically lead to elliptic integrals.

8. Worked Example 4: Arc Length of the Logarithmic Spiral r=aebθ, θ∈[0,Θ]

dθdr​=abebθ=br.

r2+(dθdr​)2=r2+b2r2=r2(1+b2)=a2e2bθ(1+b2)

L=∫0Θ​a2e2bθ(1+b2)​,dθ=a1+b2​∫0Θ​ebθ,dθ

=a1+b2​⋅b1​[ebθ]0Θ​=ba1+b2​​(ebΘ−1)

L=ba1+b2​​(ebΘ−1)​

Geometric interpretation. Note r(Θ)−r(0)=aebΘ−a=a(ebΘ−1). So:

L=b1+b2​​(r(Θ)−r(0))

The arc length is proportional to the change in radius. Since tanψ=1/b (the angle between the tangent and radius is constant), the factor 1+b2​/b=secψ/tanψ=cscψ… actually: tanψ=1/b, so sec2ψ=1+tan2ψ=1+1/b2, and secψ=1+1/b2​. Hmm — let me restate cleanly.

Since ψ=arctan(1/b): the radial displacement when moving along the curve by arc length ds is dr=ds⋅cosψ (the radial component of the arc). So ds=dr/cosψ=dr⋅secψ, and:

L=secψ⋅(r(Θ)−r(0))

This is the simplest possible relationship: the arc length is just the radial increment scaled by secψ.

9. Worked Example 5: Arc Length of the Archimedean Spiral r=aθ, θ∈[0,Θ]

dθdr​=a.

r2+(dθdr​)2=a2θ2+a2=a2(1+θ2)

L=a∫0Θ​1+θ2​,dθ

This requires the standard integral ∫1+θ2​,dθ=21​[θ1+θ2​+ln(θ+1+θ2​)]+C, derived by trigonometric (or hyperbolic) substitution θ=tanu or θ=sinhu.

L=2a​[Θ1+Θ2​+ln(Θ+1+Θ2​)]

For large Θ: 1+Θ2​≈Θ, so L≈2a​[Θ2+ln(2Θ)]≈2aΘ2​. The arc length grows quadratically in Θ — each additional revolution (increasing Θ by 2π) adds more length than the previous one, because the spiral is further from the origin.

10. Surface Area of Revolution (Extension)

When a polar curve r=f(θ) is revolved around the polar axis (the x-axis), it sweeps out a surface. The surface area element is dS=2πy,ds, giving:

S=2π∫αβ​y,ds=2π∫αβ​f(θ)sinθ⋅[f(θ)]2+[f′(θ)]2​,dθ

(Valid when y=rsinθ≥0 throughout [α,β], i.e., θ∈[0,π].)

Example. Surface area of the sphere generated by revolving r=a (semicircle, θ∈[0,π]) around the polar axis:

S=2π∫0π​asinθ⋅a2+0​,dθ=2πa2∫0π​sinθ,dθ=2πa2[−cosθ]0π​=2πa2⋅2=4πa2

The surface area of a sphere of radius a is 4πa2. ✓

11. The Role of the Half-Angle Identity: A Pattern

The cardioid calculation succeeds because r2+(r′)2 collapses to 4a2cos2(θ/2) — a perfect square. This happens because the half-angle identity 1+cosθ=2cos2(θ/2) removes the square root.

When does this pattern occur? For limaçons r=a+bcosθ:

r2+(r′)2=(a+bcosθ)2+b2sin2θ=a2+2abcosθ+b2

This is a perfect square only when a=b (the cardioid), giving (a+acosθ)2+a2sin2θ=a2(2+2cosθ)=4a2cos2(θ/2).

For a=b: r2+(r′)2=a2+b2+2abcosθ. This is NOT a perfect square in general, and the integral becomes an elliptic integral. The cardioid is the unique limaçon whose arc length is expressible in elementary functions.

WHEN DOES POLAR ARC LENGTH HAVE A CLOSED FORM?

  Curve             r² + (r')²              Integrable?
  ─────────────     ──────────────────────  ────────────
  Circle r = a      a²                      Yes (trivial)
  Cardioid          4a²cos²(θ/2)            Yes (half-angle)
  Log spiral        r²(1 + b²)              Yes (exponential)
  Archimedean       a²(1 + θ²)             Yes (trig sub)
  Rose r=cos(nθ)    4-3cos²(2θ) etc.        No (elliptic)
  Gen. limaçon      a²+b²+2ab cosθ          No (elliptic)

12. Numerical Arc Length

When a closed form is unavailable (rose curves, general limaçons), numerical integration applies directly.

Simpson’s Rule for L=∫αβ​g(θ),dθ where g(θ)=r2+(r′)2​:

Partition [α,β] into n (even) subintervals of width h=(β−α)/n:

L≈3h​[g(θ0​)+4g(θ1​)+2g(θ2​)+4g(θ3​)+⋯+4g(θn−1​)+g(θn​)]

Error bound: ∣ES​∣≤180n4(β−α)5​maxθ∈[α,β]​∣g(4)(θ)∣.

For most polar curves, n=100 gives arc length accurate to 5+ decimal places.

15. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

15.1 Arc Length as a Regularizer

The arc length of a curve measures its total variation — how much it “wiggles.” In machine learning, regularization is the practice of penalizing overly complex models. The arc length integral is one of the oldest regularizers.

In 1D regression, if you want a function y=g(x) that is both close to data and smooth, you might minimize:

L(g)=∑i​(yi​−g(xi​))2+λ∫1+(g′(x))2​,dx

The second term is exactly the arc length of the graph of g. This is a total variation regularizer — it penalizes length, not just curvature or derivative magnitude. The polar analogue ∫r2+(r′)2​,dθ would regularize a polar function r=f(θ) by its arc length.

15.2 Geodesics and Riemannian Metrics

The arc length element ds2=dr2+r2,dθ2 is the Riemannian metric of the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates. Minimizing arc length between two points gives the geodesic — which in Euclidean space is just a straight line, but in curved spaces (manifolds) is genuinely complex.

In Riemannian optimization (used in training models on curved parameter spaces — e.g., networks with orthogonal weight matrices, probabilistic models on the simplex, or transformers with unit-norm attention heads), the geodesic distance is the correct notion of “how far apart” two parameter settings are. The natural gradient descent algorithm follows geodesics in the Fisher information metric, which on the probability simplex produces arc length integrals of the form ∫∑i​(p˙​i​)2/pi​​,dt — a curved-space generalization of the polar arc length formula.

15.3 Curve Length in Latent Space

In Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and other generative models, the latent space has a learned metric. Interpolating between two latent codes z0​ and z1​ along the straight line z(t)=(1−t)z0​+tz1​ is not the same as interpolating along the geodesic — the straight line may pass through low-probability regions of latent space where the decoder produces poor outputs.

The correct interpolation minimizes the arc length in the pullback metric: the metric on latent space induced by the decoder map. Computing this arc length requires integrating gij​(z(t))z˙i(t)z˙j(t)​,dt along the path — exactly the structure of ∫r2+(r′)2​,dθ in polar coordinates. VAE papers (Arvanitidis et al., 2018) compute these geodesics to produce better interpolations.

15.4 Path Length in Reinforcement Learning

In reinforcement learning, the agent’s trajectory through state space is a curve. The total path length influences sample efficiency: a policy that reaches the goal via a shorter path has explored less of the state space and tends to generalize better (shorter description length ↔ lower complexity). Intrinsic motivation methods like curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017) implicitly reward shorter arc length paths to novel states.

In continuous control (robotics, physics simulations), the arc length of the agent’s trajectory in polar coordinates (for circular motion tasks like orbit stabilization or wheel control) is the relevant cost function. Minimizing ∫r2+(r′)2​,dθ is literally the control objective.

15.5 The Metric Tensor and Transformer Geometry

The polar arc length element ds2=dr2+r2,dθ2 is written in matrix form as:

ds2=(dr​dθ​)(1​0 0​r2​)(dr dθ​)

The matrix G=(1​0 0​r2​) is the metric tensor in polar coordinates. It encodes how distance is measured — and it depends on position (specifically on r).

The attention matrix A=softmax(QKT/d​) in a Transformer can be interpreted as an approximation to the metric tensor of the input data manifold. In this view, the attention mechanism computes a position-dependent inner product — exactly the role played by G(r,θ) in the polar metric. The arc length of a sequence of tokens through the attention geometry is ∫vTAv​,dt where v is the token velocity — the metric-weighted arc length formula.

16. Exercises

Exercise 1. (Warm-up) Compute the arc length of the circle r=4sinθ over a full tracing. What is the minimal interval for a single tracing? What goes wrong if you integrate over [0,2π]?

Exercise 2. Compute the total arc length of the cardioid r=3(1−sinθ). (Hint: use the identity 1−sinθ=1−cos(θ−π/2) to reduce to the standard cardioid, or redo the computation directly with the analogous half-angle identity 1−sinθ=?.)

Exercise 3. (Stewart §10.4) Find the arc length of r=e2θ for θ∈[0,π].

Exercise 4. Set up (but do not evaluate) the arc length integral for one petal of r=sin(2θ). Show that the integrand simplifies to 4−3sin2(2θ)​ (or similar), and identify this as an elliptic integral.

Exercise 5. (Archimedean spiral) For r=2θ, θ∈[0,2π]: set up the arc length integral and evaluate it using the formula from §9. What is the arc length of the first complete revolution?

Exercise 6. (Surface area) The curve r=asinθ, θ∈[0,π], is a circle of radius a/2. Revolving it about the polar axis generates a sphere of radius a/2. Verify this by computing the surface area using the polar surface area formula and confirming you get 4π(a/2)2=πa2.

Exercise 7. (Half-angle identity challenge) For r=a(1−cosθ) (cardioid opening left), compute r2+(r′)2 and simplify using the identity 1−cosθ=2sin2(θ/2). What absolute value issue arises, and where must you split the integral?

Exercise 8. (ML-flavored) A gradient descent trajectory in a 2D loss landscape (in polar coordinates centered at the minimum) follows r(θ)=r0​e−0.05θ (a contracting log spiral) for θ∈[0,10π] (5 full revolutions).
