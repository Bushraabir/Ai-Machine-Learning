“de Casteljau’s algorithm is one of those rare things in mathematics: a construction so natural that the theorem proves itself as you draw it.”

§0 — Historical Prelude

Pierre Bézier (Renault, 1962) and Paul de Casteljau (Citroën, 1959) independently discovered these curves while designing automobile body panels — a problem where you need smooth, controllable curves that an engineer can adjust by pulling on “handles.”

De Casteljau’s algorithm came first but was classified by Citroën. Bézier published, got his name on the curves. The algorithm remains de Casteljau’s.

The setting: we want a curve in R2 (or Rn) that:

  1. Passes through two endpoints (interpolates boundary data)
  2. Is “attracted to” intermediate control points (approximates, not interpolates)
  3. Is smooth (infinitely differentiable, in fact polynomial)
  4. Is easy to compute, subdivide, and reason about geometrically

§1 — Linear Bézier: The Foundation

The simplest case: two control points P0​,P1​∈R2.

B(t)=(1−t),P0​+t,P1​,t∈[0,1]

This is just linear interpolation (lerp). As t goes from 0 to 1:

  • t=0: B=P0​
  • t=1: B=P1​
  • t=1/2: B=2P0​+P1​​ (midpoint)

The curve is a straight line segment. This is the atomic building block of everything that follows.

Notation: Write lerp(P,Q,t)=(1−t)P+tQ.

§2 — Quadratic Bézier: Three Control Points

Three points P0​,P1​,P2​.

2.1 de Casteljau’s Construction

At parameter t:

Step 1 — Lerp between adjacent pairs: Q0​(t)=(1−t)P0​+tP1​,Q1​(t)=(1−t)P1​+tP2​

Step 2 — Lerp between the results: B(t)=(1−t)Q0​(t)+tQ1​(t)

2.2 Explicit Formula

Substitute Step 1 into Step 2: B(t)=(1−t)2P0​+2t(1−t)P1​+t2P2​

Recognize: these are the binomial coefficients (02​),(12​),(22​) weighted by (1−t)2−ktk.

de Casteljau at t = 0.5:

P0 -----Q0----- P1
         \
          B(t)       ← the curve point
         /
P1 -----Q1----- P2

§3 — Cubic Bézier: Four Control Points

The workhorse of computer graphics. Four points P0​,P1​,P2​,P3​.

3.1 de Casteljau’s Algorithm

Level 1 (3 points): Qi​(t)=(1−t)Pi​+tPi+1​,i=0,1,2

Level 2 (2 points): Ri​(t)=(1−t)Qi​(t)+tQi+1​(t),i=0,1

Level 3 (1 point — the curve): B(t)=(1−t)R0​(t)+tR1​(t)

3.2 Explicit Formula

B(t)=(1−t)3P0​+3t(1−t)2P1​+3t2(1−t)P2​+t3P3​​

3.3 Matrix Form

Write B(t)=T,MBeˊzier​,P:

B(t)=[1​t​t2​t3​][1​0​0​0 −3​3​0​0 3​−6​3​0 −1​3​−3​1​][P0​ P1​ P2​ P3​​]

This matrix form is used in GPU shader programs for fast evaluation.

§4 — The General Degree-n Bézier Curve

4.1 Bernstein Polynomials

Define the Bernstein basis polynomials of degree n:

Bk,n​(t)=(kn​)tk(1−t)n−k,k=0,1,…,n

Key properties:

  • Bk,n​(t)≥0 for t∈[0,1] → non-negativity
  • k=0∑n​Bk,n​(t)=1 for all t → partition of unity (follows from the Binomial Theorem: [(1−t)+t]n=1)
  • Bk,n​(t) peaks near t=k/n → localization

4.2 The Bézier Curve

Given control points P0​,…,Pn​∈Rd:

B(t)=k=0∑n​Bk,n​(t),Pk​=k=0∑n​(kn​)(1−t)n−ktk,Pk​,t∈[0,1]​

This is a weighted average of the control points — the weights Bk,n​(t) sum to 1 (partition of unity) and are all non-negative. The curve is always inside the convex hull of the control points.

§5 — Fundamental Properties (with Proofs)

5.1 Endpoint Interpolation

Claim: B(0)=P0​ and B(1)=Pn​.

Proof. At t=0: Bk,n​(0)=(kn​)0k⋅1n−k=0 for k≥1, and B0,n​(0)=1. So B(0)=P0​. Similarly at t=1. □

5.2 Convex Hull Property

Claim: B(t) lies in the convex hull of P0​,…,Pn​ for all t∈[0,1].

Proof. The Bk,n​(t) are non-negative and sum to 1, so B(t) is a convex combination of the control points. □

Consequence. This is the fundamental containment guarantee — the curve can never escape the convex hull of its control points. Essential for collision detection in graphics.

5.3 Tangent at Endpoints

Claim: B′(0)=n(P1​−P0​) and B′(1)=n(Pn​−Pn−1​).

Proof (for t=0). Differentiate Bk,n​(t)=(kn​)tk(1−t)n−k: Bk,n′​(t)=(kn​)[ktk−1(1−t)n−k−(n−k)tk(1−t)n−k−1] At t=0: only the k=1 term survives (all k≥2 terms have tk−1 with k−1≥1, so vanish; the k=0 term has −n(1−t)n−1 which at t=0 gives −n): B0,n′​(0)=−n,B1,n′​(0)=n,Bk,n′​(0)=0 for k≥2 Therefore: B′(0)=−nP0​+nP1​=n(P1​−P0​).□

Geometric meaning: The tangent at P0​ points directly toward P1​. Moving P1​ steers the curve’s initial direction.

5.4 Affine Invariance

Claim: Applying any affine map ϕ(x)=Ax+b to the control points gives the transformed curve: ϕ(B(t))=Beˊzier(ϕ(P0​),…,ϕ(Pn​))(t).

Proof. Affine maps commute with convex combinations (partition of unity): ϕ(B(t))=ϕ!(∑k​Bk,n​(t)Pk​)=A∑k​Bk,n​(t)Pk​+b=∑k​Bk,n​(t)(APk​+b)=∑k​Bk,n​(t)ϕ(Pk​).□

Consequence. To rotate/translate/scale a Bézier curve, just transform the control points. You don’t need to re-evaluate the curve. This is why Bézier curves dominate computer graphics.

5.5 Variation Diminishing Property

A Bézier curve crosses any line no more times than its control polygon does. Intuitively: the curve is “smoother” than the jagged polygon.

§6 — de Casteljau’s Algorithm: The Full Picture

For degree n with control points P0(0)​,…,Pn(0)​, define the triangular array:

Pi(r)​(t)=(1−t),Pi(r−1)​+t,Pi+1(r−1)​,r=1,…,n,i=0,…,n−r

The curve point at parameter t is: B(t)=P0(n)​(t)

For cubic (n=3), the full triangle:

Level 0 (given):   P0    P1    P2    P3
                     \   / \   / \   /
Level 1:            Q0    Q1    Q2
                      \   / \   /
Level 2:              R0    R1
                        \   /
Level 3 (curve pt):      B(t)

Each step is a lerp. The algorithm uses 2n(n+1)​ lerp operations total.

6.1 Subdivision Property ⭐

The most important algorithmic property. Evaluating B(t0​) via de Casteljau also gives the control points of the two sub-curves:

  • Left sub-curve [0,t0​]: control points are the left edge of the triangle: P0(0)​,P0(1)​,P0(2)​,…,P0(n)​
  • Right sub-curve [t0​,1]: control points are the right edge: P0(n)​,P1(n−1)​,…,Pn(0)​

This is the basis of adaptive subdivision — the key algorithm in font rendering, path clipping, and intersection testing.

§7 — Derivatives and Curvature

7.1 Derivative Formula

The derivative of a degree-n Bézier is a degree-(n−1) Bézier:

B′(t)=n∑k=0n−1​Bk,n−1​(t),(Pk+1​−Pk​)

Proof sketch. Differentiate Bk,n​(t) and use the identity Bk,n′​(t)=n[Bk−1,n−1​(t)−Bk,n−1​(t)].

Let ΔPk​=Pk+1​−Pk​ (forward differences). Then:

B′(t)=n,BΔ​(t)

where BΔ​ is the Bézier curve with control points ΔP0​,…,ΔPn−1​.

7.2 Curvature at Endpoints

At t=0, for a cubic:

κ(0)=∣B′(0)∣3∣B′(0)×B′′(0)∣​

where × denotes the 2D “cross product” (scalar: u×v=ux​vy​−uy​vx​), and:

B′(0)=3(P1​−P0​),B′′(0)=6(P0​−2P1​+P2​)

§8 — Joining Bézier Curves: Continuity Conditions

A single cubic Bézier has limited shape vocabulary. Complex shapes (fonts, paths) are built from piecewise Bézier splines — sequences of cubic segments joined end-to-end.

Let segment 1 end at P3​ with last handle P2​, and segment 2 start at Q0​=P3​ with first handle Q1​.

Continuity ClassConditionGeometric Meaning
C0P3​=Q0​Curves share an endpoint — no gap
G1Q1​−P3​ parallel to P3​−P2​Same tangent direction — no kink
C1Q1​−P3​=P3​−P2​Same tangent direction and speed
C2C1 + second derivative matchesMatching curvature — the join is invisible

Design implication (Illustrator, Figma). When you drag a “smooth” handle in a vector editor, you’re enforcing G1. When you hold a modifier key to enforce equal handle lengths, you’re enforcing C1. The editor is managing these conditions for you.

G¹ join (smooth, different handle lengths):

    P2    P3=Q0    Q1
     o----●--------o
          ↑
      tangent direction continuous,
      speeds may differ (‖P3-P2‖ ≠ ‖Q1-Q0‖)

C¹ join (equal handle lengths):

    P2    P3=Q0    Q1
     o----●----o
          ↑
      P3 - P2 = Q1 - P3 (exact reflection)

§9 — Rational Bézier Curves and Conics

Standard Bézier curves cannot represent a circle arc exactly — they are polynomials, and circle arcs are algebraic of a different kind. The fix: rational Bézier curves.

Attach a weight wk​>0 to each control point:

B(t)=k=0∑n​wk​Bk,n​(t)k=0∑n​wk​Bk,n​(t),Pk​​

This is a projective generalization. For n=2 with weights (1,w,1):

  • w=1: parabola arc
  • w<1: ellipse arc
  • w=cos(θ/2) for arc subtending angle θ: exact circle arc
  • w>1: hyperbola arc

NURBS (Non-Uniform Rational B-Splines) — the industry standard in CAD (SolidWorks, CATIA, Rhino) — are piecewise rational Bézier curves with a knot vector controlling the join structure.

§11 — Bézier Curves in AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

11.1 Font Rendering and OCR

TrueType fonts store every glyph as piecewise quadratic Bézier curves; PostScript/PDF uses cubic Bézier curves. The entire Latin alphabet, CJK character sets, Arabic script — everything is Bézier control points.

OCR neural networks (Tesseract 4+, TrOCR) must decode these curves. Training data for OCR is generated by rendering Bézier-encoded fonts. Understanding what the network “sees” requires understanding how Bézier curves are rasterized.

11.2 Trajectory Planning in Reinforcement Learning

In robot learning and autonomous driving, smooth reference trajectories are represented as Bézier curves. A policy network predicts control points P0​,…,Pn​ rather than a sequence of waypoints, because:

  • The convex hull property guarantees the trajectory stays within a safe region
  • The tangent conditions enforce smooth acceleration/deceleration
  • Subdivision allows adaptive refinement at high-curvature regions
  • The entire trajectory is represented by 4(n+1) numbers (compact)

Example. In Waymo’s motion forecasting, predicted agent trajectories are parametrized as cubic Bézier curves. The loss function penalizes deviation between predicted and ground-truth control points.

11.3 Bézier Curves as Learnable Parameters

BézierFormer and related models directly output Bézier control points from attention-based decoders:

P^0​,…,P^n​=Decoder(image features)

The predicted curve is B^(t)=∑k​Bk,n​(t)P^k​. The loss is:

\mathcal{L} = \int_0^1 |\hat{\mathbf{B}}(t) - \mathbf{B}^_(t)|^2, dt \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N |\hat{\mathbf{B}}(t_j) - \mathbf{B}^_(t_j)|^2

This integral loss is differentiable with respect to the control points P^k​ because Bk,n​(t) is a smooth function of t, and the chain rule flows through:

∂P^k​∂L​=N2​∑j​Bk,n​(tj​),(B^(tj​)−B∗(tj​))

11.4 Text Detection: Scene Text in the Wild

ABCNet and TextBPN use Bézier curves to detect arbitrarily curved text in natural images (menus, storefronts, logos). The network predicts control points of a Bézier curve that traces the text baseline, enabling recognition of text that cannot be enclosed in a simple bounding box.

11.5 Neural Architecture: Bézier Activation Functions

Some recent work uses learnable Bézier activation functions:

σ(x)=∑k=0n​Bk,n​!(xmax​−xmin​x−xmin​​)wk​

where the weights wk​ are learned. This subsumes ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, and GELU as special cases, with the shape learned from data. The partition of unity property ensures σ is a well-normalized blending of the weights.

11.6 Latent Space Interpolation

In GANs and VAEs, interpolating between two latent codes z0​ and z1​ via a straight line often crosses low-density regions (producing blurry or out-of-distribution images). Bézier interpolation with control points placed in high-density regions:

z(t)=(1−t)2z0​+2t(1−t)zc​+t2z1​

where zc​ is chosen to stay near the data manifold, produces semantically smoother interpolations. The convex hull property keeps the path in the convex hull of z0​,zc​,z1​ — a region more likely to be in-distribution.

11.7 Attention Weight Smoothing and Positional Encodings

The Bernstein basis functions Bk,n​(t) are a smooth partition of unity — they sum to 1, are non-negative, and have compact support on [0,1]. This is exactly the property required of attention weights (softmax output). Some continuous attention models replace the softmax with a Bernstein mixture:

αk​=∑j​Bj,n​(τ)Bk,n​(τ)​

where τ is a learned “focus” parameter. This gives smooth, controllable attention as a function of position.

11.8 Diffusion Model Trajectory Parametrization

In diffusion models, the noising/denoising path x(t) for t∈[0,1] is typically linear (DDPM) or follows a learned schedule. Emerging work (2024) parametrizes the denoising trajectory as a Bézier curve in data space:

x(t)=∑k=0n​Bk,n​(t),Ck​

where C0​=xnoise​, Cn​=xdata​, and intermediate control points C1​,…,Cn−1​ are predicted by the network. The subdivision property allows adaptive step sizing; the convex hull property bounds the trajectory.

§12 — Rigorous Summary

Definition. The Bézier curve of degree n with control points P0​,…,Pn​∈Rd is: B:[0,1]→Rd,B(t)=∑k=0n​(kn​)(1−t)n−ktk,Pk​

Theorem (Properties). B satisfies:

  1. (Endpoint interpolation) B(0)=P0​, B(1)=Pn​
  2. (Convex hull) B(t)∈conv(P0​,…,Pn​) for all t∈[0,1]
  3. (Tangents) B′(0)=n(P1​−P0​), B′(1)=n(Pn​−Pn−1​)
  4. (Affine invariance) For any affine ϕ: ϕ(B(t))=Beˊzier(ϕ(P0​),…,ϕ(Pn​))(t)
  5. (Smoothness) B∈C∞([0,1]); in fact B is a polynomial of degree ≤n
  6. (Subdivision) de Casteljau at t0​ splits B into two Bézier sub-curves with explicitly computable control points
