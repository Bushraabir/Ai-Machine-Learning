The hyperbola is the ellipse’s shadow — same two foci, same defining distance condition, but subtracted instead of summed. That single sign change tears the curve open into two infinite branches.

1. The Geometric Definition

Definition. A hyperbola is the set of all points P in the plane such that the absolute difference of distances from P to two fixed points F1​ and F2​ (the foci) is constant.

Hyperbola=,P∈R2:​∣PF1​∣−∣PF2​∣​=2a,

where 2a>0 is the constant difference.

Compare with the ellipse:

The two branches correspond to the two cases: ∣PF1​∣−∣PF2​∣=2a (branch near F1​) and ∣PF2​∣−∣PF1​∣=2a (branch near F2​).

The fundamental relation (note the sign flip from the ellipse!):

c2=a2+b2​

Proof. Place P at a vertex (a,0). Then ∣PF1​∣=c−a and ∣PF2​∣=c+a, so the difference is 2a. ✓
Place P far out along an asymptote — the relation c2=a2+b2 is forced by requiring the asymptote slope to be b/a (derived in §5). □

2. Deriving the Equation

Place center at origin, foci at F1​=(−c,0) and F2​=(c,0). Take the branch with ∣PF2​∣−∣PF1​∣=2a:

(x+c)2+y2​−(x−c)2+y2​=2a

Move one radical and square (same algebra as the ellipse derivation):

(x+c)2+y2​=2a+(x−c)2+y2​

(x+c)2+y2=4a2+4a(x−c)2+y2​+(x−c)2+y2

4cx−4a2=4a(x−c)2+y2​

cx−a2=a(x−c)2+y2​

Square again (requires cx−a2≥0, i.e., x≥a2/c, satisfied on this branch):

c2x2−2a2cx+a4=a2(x2−2cx+c2+y2)

(c2−a2)x2−a2y2=a2(c2−a2)

Since b2=c2−a2>0:

b2x2−a2y2=a2b2

a2x2​−b2y2​=1​

Key observation: The hyperbola equation differs from the ellipse’s a2x2​+b2y2​=1 only in the sign of the y2 term. That minus sign is the entire geometric difference.

3. Standard Forms

Horizontal transverse axis (opening left/right)

a2x2​−b2y2​=1

  • Center: (0,0)
  • Vertices: (±a,0)
  • Foci: (±c,0), c=a2+b2​
  • Asymptotes: y=±ab​x
  • Transverse axis along x-axis

Vertical transverse axis (opening up/down)

a2y2​−b2x2​=1

  • Center: (0,0)
  • Vertices: (0,±a)
  • Foci: (0,±c), c=a2+b2​
  • Asymptotes: y=±ba​x
  • Transverse axis along y-axis

Identification rule: The positive term’s variable tells you the direction the hyperbola opens.

Shifted hyperbola (center at (h,k))

a2(x−h)2​−b2(y−k)2​=1(horizontal)

a2(y−k)2​−b2(x−h)2​=1(vertical)

4. Eccentricity

e=ac​>1​

c=ae,b2=a2(e2−1),b=ae2−1​

eShape
e→1+Very narrow, nearly parabolic branches
e=2​Rectangular hyperbola (a=b, asymptotes at 90°)
e→∞Branches open very wide, nearly flat

Contrast with other conics:

e<1 (ellipse),e=1 (parabola),e>1 (hyperbola)

Focus-directrix property: For each focus F and its corresponding directrix ℓ:

d(P,ℓ)∣PF∣​=e>1for every P on the hyperbola

Directrices: x=±ea​=±ca2​ (for horizontal hyperbola). Note these lie between the two vertices — inside the hyperbola.

5. The Asymptotes — The Defining Feature

The asymptotes are the lines the branches approach but never touch. For a2x2​−b2y2​=1:

y=±ab​x​

Derivation. Solve for y:

y=±ab​x2−a2​=±ab​x1−x2a2​​

As x→∞: y→±ab​x. The gap between the curve and the asymptote:

ab​x−ab​x2−a2​=ab​⋅x+x2−a2​a2​→0

The approach is asymptotic but never reaches zero for finite x. □

Compact form: The asymptotes are encoded in the equation

a2x2​−b2y2​=0⟹(ax​−by​)(ax​+by​)=0

i.e., the asymptotes are what the hyperbola “degenerates to” when the right-hand side is 0 instead of 1.

Angle between asymptotes:

tanθ=a2−b22ab​(when a=b)

For the rectangular hyperbola (a=b): asymptotes are perpendicular (θ=90°).

6. The Rectangular Hyperbola

When a=b, the asymptotes are perpendicular. Equation:

x2−y2=a2

Rotating 45° (substituting x=2​u+v​, y=2​u−v​):

uv=2a2​=k

The familiar form xy=k is a rectangular hyperbola with asymptotes along the coordinate axes. Its eccentricity is e=2​.

This is the curve behind:

  • Boyle’s law: PV=const
  • Supply-demand curves in economics
  • Harmonic sequences

7. Key Geometric Properties

7.1 Latus Rectum

The chord through a focus perpendicular to the transverse axis. At x=c in a2x2​−b2y2​=1:

a2c2​−b2y2​=1⟹y2=b2!(a2c2​−1)=b2⋅a2c2−a2​=a2b4​

Length of latus rectum=a2b2​

Identical in form to the ellipse — the sign difference in c2=a2±b2 is absorbed.

7.2 The Reflective Property

Theorem. A ray directed toward one focus reflects off the hyperbola and appears to come from the other focus.

Proof sketch. At point P on the hyperbola, the tangent line bisects the angle ∠F1​PF2​ internally (unlike the ellipse, where it bisects externally). By the reflection law, an incoming ray aimed at F2​ reflects as if it came from F1​. □

Application: Cassegrain telescopes use a parabolic primary mirror and a hyperbolic secondary mirror. Light focused by the parabola toward its focus is intercepted by the hyperbola, which redirects it to the other hyperbolic focus, passing through a hole in the primary mirror.

7.3 Tangent Line

At point (x0​,y0​) on a2x2​−b2y2​=1:

a2x,x0​​−b2y,y0​​=1

Slope of tangent:

dxdy​=a2y0​b2x0​​

Tangent with slope m:

y=mx±a2m2−b2​(requires ∣m∣>b/a)

(Condition: a2m2−b2>0, i.e., slope steeper than the asymptotes.)

8. Parametric Equations

Two equivalent parametrizations:

Hyperbolic functions (natural):

x=acosht,y=bsinht,t∈R

Right branch only. Verify: a2a2cosh2t​−b2b2sinh2t​=cosh2t−sinh2t=1. ✓

For left branch: x=−acosht, y=bsinht.

Trigonometric (both branches, sec parametrization):

x=asect,y=btant,t∈(−2π​,2π​)∪(2π​,23π​)

Verify: a2a2sec2t​−b2b2tan2t​=sec2t−tan2t=1. ✓

10. Area and Arc Length

Area between the hyperbola and its transverse axis (from x=a to x=x1​):

A=∫ax1​​ab​x2−a2​;dx=ab​[2xx2−a2​​−2a2​ln!​x+x2−a2​​]ax1​​

Arc length of one branch from vertex to (x1​,y1​):

L=∫ax1​​1+a2(x2−a2)b2x2​​,dx

(No closed form in general; expressed via elliptic integrals.)

Sector area (analogous to the 21​r2,dθ formula): in the (cosht,sinht) parametrization, the area of the sector swept from t=0 to t=T is 21​ab,T — the hyperbolic angle is literally the area.

11. Curvature

At parametric point t (hyperbolic parametrization):

κ(t)=(a2sinh2t+b2cosh2t)3/2ab​

At vertex (t=0, x=a):

κmax​=b2a​,ρmin​=ab2​

(Same form as the ellipse vertex formula — and same as the parabola vertex formula ρ=2p with p=b2/(2a).)

12. Worked Examples

Example 1: Find Everything from the Equation

Given 9x2​−16y2​=1.

  • a2=9⇒a=3 (positive term → opens left/right)
  • b2=16⇒b=4
  • c=9+16​=25​=5
  • e=c/a=5/3≈1.667
  • Vertices: (±3,0)
  • Foci: (±5,0)
  • Asymptotes: y=±34​x
  • Latus rectum: 32(16)​=332​

Example 2: Find the Equation

Foci at (0,±13), vertices at (0,±5).

  • Foci on y-axis → vertical transverse axis
  • a=5, c=13
  • b2=c2−a2=169−25=144, so b=12

25y2​−144x2​=1

  • Asymptotes: y=±125​x

Example 3: Shifted Hyperbola

9x2−4y2−36x+8y−4=0

Complete the square:

9(x2−4x)−4(y2−2y)=4

9(x−2)2−36−4(y−1)2+4=4

9(x−2)2−4(y−1)2=36

4(x−2)2​−9(y−1)2​=1

Center (2,1), a=2, b=3, c=13​, horizontal transverse axis.

Asymptotes: y−1=±23​(x−2).

14. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

14.1 Hyperbolic Space and Hyperbolic Neural Networks

Euclidean space has flat geometry; hyperbolic space has constant negative curvature. Poincaré disk and hyperboloid models of hyperbolic space are built on the equation:

−t2+x12​+x22​+⋯+xn2​=−1

This is a hyperboloid — the n-dimensional generalization of the hyperbola. Poincaré embeddings (Nickel & Kiela, 2017) embed hierarchical data (trees, knowledge graphs, taxonomies) into hyperbolic space because hyperbolic geometry grows exponentially with radius, perfectly matching the exponential branching structure of trees.

The core geometry: in 2D hyperbolic space, circles of radius r have circumference 2πsinhr≈πer — exponential growth vs the Euclidean 2πr. This is why hyperbolic embeddings can represent large hierarchies with low distortion in few dimensions.

14.2 The Hyperboloid Model and Lorentz Embeddings

The hyperboloid model of n-dimensional hyperbolic space is the upper sheet of:

−x02​+x12​+⋯+xn2​=−1,x0​>0

This is a direct generalization of the hyperbola to n+1 dimensions, with the Minkowski inner product ⟨u,v⟩L​=−u0​v0​+∑i=1n​ui​vi​.

Lorentz embeddings use this space to embed relational data and have shown state-of-the-art performance on link prediction in knowledge graphs. The geodesic distance in this space:

d(u,v)=arccosh(−⟨u,v⟩L​)

is the “hyperbolic distance” — directly encoding the hyperbola’s geometry into the similarity measure.

14.3 Hyperbolic Activation Functions

The hyperbolic tangent:

tanh(x)=ex+e−xex−e−x​=coshxsinhx​

is the most ubiquitous activation function in recurrent networks (LSTMs, GRUs). Its shape — bounded in (−1,1), odd, saturating — comes directly from the parametrization of the hyperbola x2−y2=1: the point (cosht,sinht) traces the right branch, and tanht=y/x is the slope to that point.

The sigmoid σ(x)=1+e−x1​ is a shifted, rescaled tanh: tanh(x)=2σ(2x)−1.

14.4 SVM Decision Boundaries and Hyperbolic Separation

In a two-class SVM, the margin boundary between the two support hyperplanes:

w⊤x+b=±1

When data is not linearly separable and a radial basis function (RBF) kernel is used, the decision boundary in input space can be a conic section — including a hyperbola in 2D. The hyperbolic boundary arises when the two classes’ centroids in feature space are separated in a way that produces a difference-of-distances condition, mirroring the hyperbola’s definition exactly.

14.5 Contrastive Learning and Hyperbolic Distance

Modern contrastive learning (SimCLR, MoCo, CLIP) learns representations by pulling positive pairs together and pushing negative pairs apart. When the representation space is taken to be hyperbolic rather than Euclidean, the contrastive loss becomes:

L=−log∑k​exp(−dH​(zi​,zk​)/τ)exp(−dH​(zi​,zj​)/τ)​

where dH​ is the hyperbolic distance. The level sets of dH​(z,z0​)=r are circles in the Poincaré disk model, but correspond to horocycles — curves asymptotically approaching the boundary, which are the hyperbolic analogs of the hyperbola’s branches approaching their asymptotes.

14.6 Asymptotic Behavior and Generalization Bounds

In learning theory, PAC-Bayes bounds and VC dimension bounds take the form:

Generalization error≤Training error+Cndlog(n/d)​​

The behavior of these bounds as n→∞ (fixed d) is asymptotically hyperbolic in the (n,error) plane: the bound curve approaches the x-axis asymptotically, never reaching zero — exactly like a hyperbola approaching its asymptote. The “price of complexity” d controls the eccentricity of this hyperbolic decay.
