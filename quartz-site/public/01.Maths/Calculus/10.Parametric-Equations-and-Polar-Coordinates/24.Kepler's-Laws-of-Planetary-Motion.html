These three laws are not empirical curiosities. They are theorems — consequences of Newton’s inverse-square law, conservation of angular momentum, and the polar conic machinery we already have.

Historical Context

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) extracted these three laws empirically from Tycho Brahe’s extraordinarily precise naked-eye observations of Mars. He did not know why they were true.

Isaac Newton (1687, Principia) proved all three as mathematical consequences of:

  1. F=ma (second law)
  2. F=−GMm/r2 (universal gravitation, inverse-square)
  3. Conservation of angular momentum

This is one of the great moments in the history of science: observation → law → derivation from deeper principle.

Note to self: When Newton derived Kepler’s First Law, he had to invent the calculus to do it. The orbit equation is a second-order nonlinear ODE — and its general solution is a conic section. This is not a coincidence; it is a theorem.

The Three Laws — Statement

Kepler’s First Law (The Law of Ellipses). The orbit of each planet is an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.

Kepler’s Second Law (The Law of Equal Areas). A line segment joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals.

Kepler’s Third Law (The Harmonic Law). The square of the orbital period T is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis a: T2=GM4π2​a3

These are connected. The second law is purely about angular momentum conservation — it requires only a central force (not necessarily inverse-square). The first and third laws require the specific 1/r2 form.

Law I: Orbits are Ellipses

Deriving the Orbit Equation from Newton’s Law

Setup. Use polar coordinates (r,θ) centered at the Sun (mass M), planet (mass m). Newton’s second law in polar coordinates:

Radial: m(r¨−rθ˙2)=−r2GMm​

Tangential: m(rθ¨+2r˙θ˙)=0

The tangential equation says: dtd​(r2θ˙)=0⟹r2θ˙=h=const

This constant h is the specific angular momentum (angular momentum per unit mass). This is already Law II in disguise.

Substitution. Let u=1/r. Then: r˙=−u21​u˙=−u2h​⋅dθdu​⋅1θ˙​=−hdθdu​

r¨=−hdθ2d2u​θ˙=−h2u2dθ2d2u​

Substituting into the radial equation: −h2u2dθ2d2u​−r⋅h2u4=−GMu2

Wait — let me be careful. With r=1/u and rθ˙2=(1/u)⋅(hu2)2=h2u3:

m(−h2u2dθ2d2u​−h2u3⋅u1​)⋅11​=−GMmu2

Simplifying the radial equation becomes the Binet equation:

dθ2d2u​+u=h2GM​​

This is a simple harmonic oscillator equation with a constant forcing term! Its general solution is:

u=h2GM​+Ccos(θ−θ0​)

Choosing θ0​=0 (orienting periapsis along polar axis) and setting ℓ=h2/(GM):

u=ℓ1​(1+ecosθ)⟹r=1+ecosθℓ​

where e=Cℓ≥0 is determined by initial conditions (total energy). This is exactly the polar conic equation with semi-latus rectum ℓ=h2/(GM). □

This is Kepler’s First Law as a theorem. The orbit is a conic section. It’s an ellipse when e<1, i.e., when the total energy is negative (bound orbit).

The Polar Conic Connection

Comparing with our Polar Equations in Conics note:

r=1+ecosθed​⟷r=1+ecosθℓ​

So ℓ=ed is the semi-latus rectum — the value of r at θ=π/2, i.e., the half-chord through the focus perpendicular to the major axis.

Law II: Equal Areas in Equal Times

Proof via Angular Momentum Conservation

Areal velocity. In time dt, the planet sweeps out a small triangle with the Sun. In polar coordinates, the area of this triangle is:

dA=21​r⋅(r,dθ)=21​r2,dθ

Therefore:

dtdA​=21​r2θ˙=2h​

Since h=r2θ˙ is conserved (for any central force, not just 1/r2), the areal velocity is constant.

dtdA​=2h​=const​

Equal times ⇒ equal ΔA. QED. □

This is the deepest of the three laws conceptually. It is equivalent to conservation of angular momentum, which follows from the fact that gravity is a central force (directed along the line joining Sun and planet). No specific r-dependence is needed. It would hold for 1/r3, 1/r5, anything central.

The Areal Velocity

The total area of an ellipse is A=πab. Over one period T, the full area is swept:

πab=2h​⋅T⟹T=h2πab​

This feeds directly into Law III.

Law III: The Harmonic Law

Proof for Circular Orbits (Warm-Up)

For a circular orbit of radius r, the gravitational force provides the centripetal acceleration:

r2GMm​=rmv2​=mrω2=mr(T2π​)2

Solving:

r2GM​=T24π2r​⟹T2=GM4π2​r3

For a circle, r=a, so T2∝a3. □

Proof for Elliptical Orbits (Full)

From the period formula derived in Law II:

T=h2πab​

For an ellipse:

  • Semi-latus rectum: ℓ=b2/a
  • So b2=aℓ=ah2/(GM)
  • Therefore b=a1/2⋅h/GM​

Substituting:

T=h2πa⋅(GM​a1/2h​)​=GM​2πa3/2​

T2=GM4π2​a3​

□

This is remarkable. The period depends only on a, the semi-major axis — not on the eccentricity. Two planets with the same semi-major axis but wildly different eccentricities have the same period. The shape doesn’t matter, only the “size” in one direction.

Unified Picture: Energy and Orbit Shape

The total mechanical energy per unit mass determines the orbit:

E=21​v2−rGM​=−2aGM​

The eccentricity is related to E and h by:

e=1+G2M22Eh2​​=1−a2b2​​

Energy EEccentricity eOrbita
E<00≤e<1Ellipse (bound)a=−GM/(2E)>0
E=0e=1Parabolaa→∞
E>0e>1Hyperbola (unbound)a=GM/(2E)>0*

*For hyperbola, a is defined as the distance from center to vertex.

Escape velocity. Setting E=0 at surface radius R: 21​vesc2​=RGM​⟹vesc​=R2GM​​

At Earth’s surface: vesc​≈11.2 km/s.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Earth’s Orbit

Earth: a=1 AU =1.496×1011 m, e≈0.0167.

Period check: T2=GM⊙​4π2​a3

With GM⊙​=1.327×1020 m³/s²:

T=2πGM⊙​a3​​=2π1.327×1020(1.496×1011)3​​≈3.156×107 s≈365.25 days✓

Perihelion and aphelion: rmin​=a(1−e)=0.9833 AU,rmax​=a(1+e)=1.0167 AU

About 3.3% variation in Earth-Sun distance. Not why we have seasons.

Example 2 — Halley’s Comet

T=75.3 years, e=0.967.

Semi-major axis: a=(4π2GM⊙​T2​)1/3

In astronomical units: a3=T2 (when T in years, a in AU) gives a=75.32/3≈17.8 AU.

Perihelion distance: rmin​=a(1−e)=17.8(0.033)≈0.587 AU (inside Venus’s orbit).

Aphelion distance: rmax​=a(1+e)=17.8(1.967)≈35 AU (beyond Neptune).

Polar equation (Sun at focus, periapsis at θ=0): ℓ=a(1−e2)=17.8(1−0.9672)≈1.15 AU r=1+0.967cosθ1.15​ AU

Example 3 — Geostationary Orbit

A satellite in geostationary orbit (T=24 h) around Earth. Find the orbital radius.

r3=4π2GM⊕​T2​

With GM⊕​=3.986×1014 m³/s² and T=86400 s:

r=(4π23.986×1014⋅(86400)2​)1/3≈4.22×107 m≈42,200 km

This is about 6.6 Earth radii from the center, or 35,800 km above the surface.

Example 4 — Interplanetary Transfer (Hohmann Transfer)

To travel from Earth (a1​=1 AU) to Mars (a2​=1.524 AU) on a Hohmann transfer ellipse:

  • Transfer orbit semi-major axis: at​=(a1​+a2​)/2=1.262 AU
  • Transfer time (half-period): t=Tt​/2=πat3​/GM⊙​​

t=π1(1.262)3​​≈π⋅1.418≈4.454 years/(2π)≈259 days

This is the minimum energy trajectory — the one that uses least fuel. It follows an ellipse with perihelion at Earth’s orbit and aphelion at Mars’s orbit.

Connection to AI / ML / Deep Learning

The connections here go deeper than might be expected.

1. Inverse Problems & Orbital Mechanics in ML

Given noisy observations of a planet’s position (ri​,θi​), recover the orbital parameters (e,ℓ) — this is a nonlinear least squares problem:

mine,ℓ​∑i​(ri​−1+ecosθi​ℓ​)2

Modern approaches use Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to solve orbital mechanics problems, where the Kepler equations are embedded as constraints or regularizers in the loss function. The network must learn solutions consistent with Kepler’s laws.

2. Hamiltonian Neural Networks — Learning Conservation Laws

Kepler’s Second Law is conservation of angular momentum: L=r2θ˙=h. Kepler’s Third Law encodes conservation of energy: E=−GM/(2a).

Hamiltonian Neural Networks (Greydanus et al., 2019) learn to respect these conservation laws by parameterizing dynamics through the Hamiltonian H(q,p):

q˙​=∂p∂H​,p˙​=−∂q∂H​

Trained on trajectory data, they provably conserve energy — unlike standard neural ODEs which drift. The orbital mechanics case (2-body problem) is the canonical benchmark.

The Hamiltonian for the Kepler problem is H=pr2​/2+pθ2​/(2r2)−GM/r. The pθ​=h=const is Law II; the value of H=E=const plus the orbit equation gives Law I; Law III follows from integrating Law II over a full period.

3. Neural ODEs and Symplectic Integrators

The equations of orbital motion form a Hamiltonian system. Numerically integrating them requires symplectic integrators (Störmer-Verlet, leapfrog) that preserve phase-space volume (Liouville’s theorem) and the symplectic structure.

Neural ODEs use neural networks as the vector field: dtdz​=fθ​(z,t)

When applied to physical systems like orbital mechanics, vanilla Neural ODEs violate conservation laws over long integration. Symplectic Neural ODEs and Lagrangian Neural Networks are architectures specifically designed to respect these geometric structures.

Kepler orbits are the most-studied test case — if your “physics-aware” architecture can’t reproduce elliptical orbits and the T2∝a3 relation, it’s wrong.

4. Symmetry and Equivariant Neural Networks

Kepler’s laws are consequences of symmetries:

  • Rotational symmetry of gravity → conservation of angular momentum (Law II) via Noether’s theorem
  • Time-translation symmetry → conservation of energy (Law I, orbit is fixed)
  • Hidden SO(4) symmetry (Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector) → closed elliptical orbits specifically (only for 1/r2!)

Equivariant neural networks (SE(3)-equivariant, E(n)-equivariant) are architectures that bake in these symmetries by design. They appear in:

  • AlphaFold 2 — SE(3)-equivariant attention for protein structure
  • NequIP, MACE — equivariant graph networks for molecular dynamics
  • Orbital mechanics simulators — SO(3)-equivariant networks for n-body problems

The lesson: knowing the symmetry group of your problem → better inductive bias → more data-efficient learning.

5. Areal Velocity = Cross Product = Attention Mechanism?

The areal velocity dA/dt=21​∣r×v∣ is a cross product in 2D — equivalently, the determinant of a 2×2 matrix:

h=​rx​​ry​ vx​​vy​​​

In the attention mechanism of transformers:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QKT​)V

The QKT matrix is a matrix of inner products between queries and keys. The softmax produces a probability distribution that “selects” values — it’s an angular weighting, related to projections. The connection is loose but real: both are fundamentally about inner-product geometry and selective integration over a space, with the dot product playing the role of the angular velocity in determining how much “area” (attention) is swept.

6. Orbital Mechanics in Satellite Scheduling — Combinatorial Optimization

Modern ML uses Kepler’s laws extensively in:

  • Satellite imagery scheduling (what can a satellite see and when, given its orbit)
  • Collision avoidance (predicting conjunction events)
  • Starlink/satellite constellation optimization — large-scale combinatorial problems where Keplerian propagation is the dynamics model and RL/evolutionary algorithms search for optimal configurations

The orbital propagator (numerical integration of Kepler’s equations) is literally called thousands of times per second in reinforcement learning training loops for these applications.
