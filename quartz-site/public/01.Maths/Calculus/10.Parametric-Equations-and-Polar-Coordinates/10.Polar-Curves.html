“The choice of coordinate system is not a matter of taste — it is a matter of what the geometry is trying to tell you.”

0. Why Bother? A Motivating Remark

Cartesian coordinates encode a point as a displacement from an origin: how far right, how far up. This is natural when geometry is built from rectangles and grids. But many curves — spirals, roses, cardioids, limaçons — are intrinsically radial: they are defined by how far you are from a fixed center, and in which direction. For such curves, the Cartesian description is grotesque while the polar description is one line.

In machine learning, the same philosophy recurs constantly: the right representation collapses complexity. A kernel function, an attention mechanism, a Fourier feature map — all are attempts to find coordinates in which the problem becomes simple.

1. The Polar Coordinate System

Definition. A point P in the plane is assigned polar coordinates (r,θ) where:

  • r = the radial distance from the pole (origin) O to P
  • θ = the polar angle measured counter-clockwise from the polar axis (positive x-axis)

The conversion identities are exact:

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ

r2=x2+y2,tanθ=xy​

Remark on uniqueness. Unlike Cartesian coordinates, polar representations are not unique:

(r,,θ)=(r,,θ+2πk)for any k∈Z

(r,,θ)=(−r,,θ+π)

This non-uniqueness is not a bug — it is the source of the richness we exploit when finding intersections of polar curves.

2. Polar Curves: Definition and Graphing

A polar curve is the set of points whose polar coordinates satisfy an equation r=f(θ), or more generally F(r,θ)=0.

3. Tangents to Polar Curves

3.1 Derivation

Given r=f(θ), the Cartesian parametrization is:

x(θ)=f(θ)cosθ,y(θ)=f(θ)sinθ

By the chain rule:

dθdy​=f′(θ)sinθ+f(θ)cosθ

dθdx​=f′(θ)cosθ−f(θ)sinθ

Therefore, the slope of the tangent at a point is:

dxdy​=f′(θ)cosθ−f(θ)sinθf′(θ)sinθ+f(θ)cosθ​​

3.2 Horizontal and Vertical Tangents

  • Horizontal tangent: dθdy​=0 and dθdx​=0
  • Vertical tangent: dθdx​=0 and dθdy​=0

Example. For the cardioid r=1+cosθ, find horizontal tangents.

f(θ)=1+cosθ, f′(θ)=−sinθ.

dθdy​=−sinθ⋅sinθ+(1+cosθ)cosθ=−sin2θ+cosθ+cos2θ

=cos(2θ)+cosθ=2cos2θ−1+cosθ

Setting equal to zero: 2cos2θ+cosθ−1=0, which factors as (2cosθ−1)(cosθ+1)=0.

So cosθ=1/2⇒θ=π/3,5π/3 and cosθ=−1⇒θ=π (the pole — needs separate analysis).

At θ=π/3: r=3/2, giving the Cartesian point (3/4,33​/4). ✓

4. Area Enclosed by Polar Curves

4.1 The Sector Formula

The key idea: a thin “wedge” of angle dθ and radius r has area ≈21​r2,dθ (the area of a circular sector with angle dθ and radius r).

Theorem (Area in Polar Coordinates). The area enclosed by r=f(θ) between θ=α and θ=β is:

A=21​∫αβ​r2,dθ=21​∫αβ​[f(θ)]2,dθ​

Proof sketch. Partition [α,β] into n subintervals. On the i-th subinterval [θi−1​,θi​], approximate the region by a circular sector of radius f(\theta_i^_) and angle Δθi​. Its area is \frac{1}{2}[f(\theta_i^_)]^2 \Delta\theta_i. Summing and taking n→∞ gives the integral. □

A=2⋅21​∫0π​(1+cosθ)2,dθ=∫0π​(1+2cosθ+cos2θ),dθ

=∫0π​(1+2cosθ+21​+2cos(2θ)​)dθ=∫0π​(23​+2cosθ+2cos(2θ)​)dθ

4.3 Area Between Two Polar Curves

If f(θ)≥g(θ)≥0 on [α,β], the area between r=f(θ) (outer) and r=g(θ) (inner) is:

A=21​∫αβ​([f(θ)]2−[g(θ)]2)dθ

Example. Area inside r=3sinθ and outside r=1+sinθ.

6. Intersection of Polar Curves

This is where most students get burned. The issue is non-uniqueness of representation.

To find all intersections of r=f(θ) and r=g(θ):

Step 1. Solve f(θ)=g(θ) simultaneously. These give “genuine” intersections.

Step 2. Check whether the pole lies on both curves: f(θ1​)=0 for some θ1​, and g(θ2​)=0 for some θ2​. If so, the pole is an intersection point (even if θ1​=θ2​).

Step 3. Check equivalent representations. If r=f(θ), also check r=f(θ+2πk) and −r=f(θ+π) against g.

Example. Find all intersections of r=cosθ and r=1−cosθ.

Step 1: cosθ=1−cosθ⇒2cosθ=1⇒θ=π/3,5π/3.

Points: r=1/2 at θ=π/3: Cartesian (1/4,3​/4) and at θ=5π/3: (1/4,−3​/4).

Step 2: r=cosθ=0 when θ=π/2. r=1−cos(π/2)=1=0. But r=1−cosθ=0 when θ=0, and r=cos(0)=1=0. The pole is NOT on r=1−cosθ… actually wait: 1−cosθ=0⇒θ=0, so the pole lies on r=1−cosθ (at θ=0). And r=cos(π/2)=0, so the pole lies on r=cosθ (at θ=π/2). Hence the pole is also an intersection.

Total: 3 intersection points.

8. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

Polar coordinates and polar curve theory connect to modern ML in several deep and concrete ways. This is not superficial analogy — these are genuine mathematical bridges.

8.1 Radial Basis Functions (RBFs) and Kernel Methods

A radial basis function ϕ(x)=ϕ(∣x∣) depends only on the distance from a center — it is constant on spheres, which are the “circles” of Euclidean space. This is exactly the polar symmetry r=const.

The Gaussian RBF kernel k(x,x′)=e−∣x−x′∣2/2σ2 is radially symmetric. In polar-like coordinates centered at x′, it becomes k=e−r2/2σ2, a function of r alone — identical in structure to polar curves like r=e−aθ2.

SVM with RBF kernels implicitly map data into an infinite-dimensional feature space where linearly inseparable data (a donut-shaped class, say) becomes separable. The donut has polar equation r1​≤r≤r2​ — it is a polar region.

8.2 Fourier Features and Periodic Representations

The rose curve r=cos(nθ) is built from cos(nθ) — a Fourier basis function. In Random Fourier Features (Rahimi & Recht, 2007), RBF kernels are approximated by random projections of the form z(x)=cos(ωTx+b) where ω is sampled from a specific distribution. These feature maps live naturally in polar/angular space.

Positional encodings in Transformers (e.g., sin(k/100002i/d), cos(k/100002i/d)) are essentially polar angle encodings applied to the token index k. The self-attention mechanism computes QKT, and the quality of this dot product depends critically on how angles are preserved — RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) makes this explicit by applying a rotation matrix in 2D blocks, i.e., a rotation in polar coordinates.

8.3 Loss Landscape Geometry and Angular Structure

The loss landscape L(θ) of a neural network is a high-dimensional scalar field. Understanding its critical points (saddles, minima) requires analyzing the directional behavior of L — in polar coordinates centered at a critical point, this means studying L(r,θ^) as r→0 from different directions θ^.

The sharpness of a minimum (related to the Hessian’s largest eigenvalue) corresponds to the maximum of ∂2L/∂r2 over all directions θ^. Flat minima (small sharpness) generalize better — this is the content of SAM (Sharpness-Aware Minimization).

8.4 Circular/Torus Data Manifolds

In data with periodic structure — time-of-day features, molecular bond angles, protein dihedral angles, directions in 2D space — the natural representation is angular: the data lives on a circle S1 or torus S1×S1, described by polar/toroidal coordinates.

von Mises distribution is the analogue of the Gaussian for circular data: p(θ)∝eκcos(θ−μ). Neural networks for protein structure prediction (AlphaFold) must handle such circular outputs — a naive MSE loss on angles is metrically wrong (the angle 359° is close to 1°, but their difference is 358°).

8.5 Equivariant Networks and Rotational Symmetry

A CNN that must be equivariant to rotation is most naturally designed in polar coordinates. Steerable CNNs and E(2)-equivariant networks decompose feature maps into irreducible representations of SO(2) — i.e., into Fourier modes einθ, the complex version of polar rose harmonics. The n-petal rose r=cos(nθ) is the real part of the n-th Fourier mode.

10. Exercises Worth Doing

  1. (Stewart §10.3) Sketch r=2sin(3θ) and determine the number of petals and the area of one petal.

  2. (MIT 18.02) Find the area of the region inside r=2 and inside r=2(1+cosθ). Hint: sketch first — both curves together.

  3. (Tangent lines) Find all tangent lines to r=1+sinθ that are horizontal or vertical.

  4. (Arc length) Compute the arc length of one petal of r=cos(2θ).

  5. (Intersections) Find all intersections of r=1 and r=2cosθ. Account for intersections arising from different parametrizations.

  6. (ML connection) Let ϕ:R2→R be defined by ϕ(r,θ)=e−r2. Show that ϕ is a radial function and compute ∫R2​ϕ,dA using polar area elements.
