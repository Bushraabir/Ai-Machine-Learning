0. Preliminary Remarks

The cycloid is a curve with an extraordinary history. In the 17th century it provoked simultaneous discovery, bitter priority disputes, and some of the deepest mathematics of the era — the brachistochrone problem alone launched the calculus of variations. Pascal, Descartes, Roberval, Torricelli, Huygens, Newton, Leibniz, and the Bernoullis all worked on it.

Mathematically, it is the archetype for what parametric equations can do that Cartesian equations cannot: there is no closed-form y=f(x) for the cycloid. It must be described parametrically. And yet every important quantity — arc length, area, curvature, period of oscillation — can be computed exactly and yields a beautiful answer.

This note is a complete treatment: derivation → exact computations → the tautochrone theorem (with proof) → the brachistochrone problem → connection to Fourier series and ML.

2. Derivation of the Parametric Equations

Setup

A circle of radius r rolls without slipping along the x-axis. A point P is fixed on the rim. At t=0, P is at the origin.

Step-by-Step Derivation

At parameter t (the angle rolled), the circle has turned through angle t radians. Since it rolls without slipping, the contact point has moved a distance rt along the x-axis.

         P
        *          ← point on rim
       ╱ ╲
      ╱  r╲t       ← angle turned = t
     ╱──────╲
────*────────C──────────────── x-axis
   0        rt
             ↑
          center C = (rt, r)

Center of the circle: C=(rt, r).

Position of P relative to C: The point P started at the bottom of the circle (angle −π/2 from center in standard position) and the circle has rotated clockwise by t radians. So P is at angle (−π/2−t) from C:

P−C=r(cos(−π/2−t) sin(−π/2−t)​)=r(−sint −cost​).

Absolute position: x=rt−rsint=r(t−sint),y=r−rcost=r(1−cost).​

Remark. At t=0: (x,y)=(0,0). At t=π: (x,y)=(rπ,2r) — the top of the arch. At t=2π: (x,y)=(2πr,0) — back on the ground, one full arch complete. ✓

3. Basic Properties

3.1 The Velocity Vector

x′(t)=r(1−cost),y′(t)=rsint.

The speed is: ∣γ˙​(t)∣=r(1−cost)2+sin2t​=r2−2cost​=2r​sin2t​​.

using the identity 1−cost=2sin2(t/2).

Cusps: ∣γ˙​∣=0 iff sin(t/2)=0 iff t=2kπ, i.e., exactly when the tracing point touches the ground. At these points the rolling circle has an instantaneous velocity of zero — the contact point is momentarily at rest. The curve has a cusp pointing downward.

3.2 Tangent Slope

dxdy​=x′(t)y′(t)​=r(1−cost)rsint​=1−costsint​.

Using the half-angle identities sint=2sin(t/2)cos(t/2) and 1−cost=2sin2(t/2):

dxdy​=2sin2(t/2)2sin(t/2)cos(t/2)​=cot2t​.

At t=π/2: slope =cot(π/4)=1. Tangent at 45° to horizontal.

At t=π: slope =cot(π/2)=0. Horizontal tangent at the top. ✓

At t→0+: slope =cot(0+)→+∞. Vertical tangent at the cusp. ✓

4. Arc Length of One Arch

Theorem 4.1. The arc length of one arch of a cycloid of radius r is L=8r.

Proof. L=∫02π​∣γ˙​(t)∣,dt=∫02π​2rsin2t​,dt (the integrand is non-negative on [0,2π], so the absolute value drops). Substitute u=t/2, du=dt/2: L=2r∫02π​sin2t​,dt=2r[−2cos2t​]02π​=2r(−2cosπ+2cos0)=2r(2+2)=8r.■

Remark. The circumference of the rolling circle is 2πr. The arch length 8r is 8r/(2πr)=4/π≈1.27 times the circumference — a non-obvious and irrational multiple of r, but a perfectly clean formula.

5. Area Under One Arch

Theorem 5.1. The area enclosed between one arch of the cycloid and the x-axis is A=3πr2.

Proof. Using the parametric area formula (Theorem 3.4 of Parametric Curves): A=∫02π​y(t),x′(t),dt=∫02π​r(1−cost)⋅r(1−cost),dt=r2∫02π​(1−cost)2,dt.

Expand: (1−cost)2=1−2cost+cos2t=1−2cost+21+cos2t​.

Integrate term by term over [0,2π]: ∫02π​1,dt=2π,∫02π​−2cost,dt=0,∫02π​21+cos2t​,dt=π.

Therefore: A=r2(2π+0+π)=3πr2.■

Remark. The area of the rolling circle is πr2. The arch area 3πr2 is exactly three times the area of the rolling circle. Roberval proved this result in 1634 using an ingenious geometric argument — before calculus existed.

6. Curvature

Theorem 6.1. The curvature of the cycloid at parameter t is: κ(t)=4rsin(t/2)1​. The radius of curvature is R(t)=4rsin(t/2).

Proof. Compute the second derivatives: x′=r(1−cost),x′′=rsint,y′=rsint,y′′=rcost.

Apply the curvature formula: κ=(x′2+y′2)3/2∣x′y′′−y′x′′∣​=(r2(1−cost)2+r2sin2t)3/2∣r(1−cost)⋅rcost−rsint⋅rsint∣​.

Numerator: r2∣(1−cost)cost−sin2t∣=r2∣cost−cos2t−sin2t∣=r2∣cost−1∣=r2(1−cost).

Denominator: using x′2+y′2=2r2(1−cost)=4r2sin2(t/2): (4r2sin2(t/2))3/2=8r3sin3(t/2).

Using 1−cost=2sin2(t/2) in the numerator: κ=8r3sin3(t/2)r2⋅2sin2(t/2)​=4rsin(t/2)1​.■

Observations:

  • At t=π (top of arch): R=4rsin(π/2)=4r — maximum radius of curvature, gentlest bending.
  • As t→0+ (cusp): R→0, κ→∞ — the cusp is infinitely sharp.
  • The curvature is symmetric: κ(2π−t)=κ(t), as expected by the arch’s symmetry.

Evolute of the cycloid. The center of the osculating circle at parameter t traces another curve — the evolute. For the cycloid, the evolute is itself a congruent cycloid, shifted by (πr,−r): xˉ=r(t+sint)−πr,yˉ​=−(1−cost)⋅r+r⋅2⋅..

More precisely: the evolute of x=r(t−sint), y=r(1−cost) is xˉ=r(t+sint), yˉ​=−r(1−cost) — a cycloid reflected across the x-axis and shifted. This self-reproducing property under the evolute operation is unique and central to Huygens’ clock design.

8. The Brachistochrone Problem

Problem (Johann Bernoulli, 1696). Among all smooth curves connecting two points A and B (with B below and to the right of A), find the curve along which a bead slides from A to B in the shortest time under gravity.

This is the brachistochrone problem (brachistos = shortest, chronos = time). It launched the calculus of variations.

The Time Functional

The descent time along a curve y=y(x) from (0,0) to (x1​,y1​) is: T[y]=∫0x1​​2gy​1+y′2​​,dx.

(Speed v=2gy​ from energy conservation; arc element ds=1+y′2​,dx.)

This is a functional — a function of a function. We minimize it over all smooth curves y(x) with y(0)=0, y(x1​)=y1​.

Euler–Lagrange Equation

The integrand is F(y,y′)=(1+y′2)/(2gy)​. Since F does not depend on x explicitly, the Beltrami identity gives a first integral: F−y′∂y′∂F​=C.

Computing ∂F/∂y′=y′/((1+y′2)​2gy​):

2gy​1+y′2​​−1+y′2​2gy​y′2​=2gy(1+y′2)​1​=C.

So 2gy(1+y′2)=1/C2=k (constant). This gives: y(1+y′2)=2gk​≡a(constant).

The Cycloid Solves the Brachistochrone

The cycloid x=r(t−sint), y=r(1−cost) satisfies:

1+y′2=1+cot2(t/2)=csc2(t/2)=1/sin2(t/2).

y(1+y′2)=r(1−cost)⋅sin2(t/2)1​=r⋅2sin2(t/2)⋅sin2(t/2)1​=2r=const.

So a=2r — the brachistochrone ODE is satisfied with r=a/2. ■

Theorem 8.1 (Bernoulli, 1696). The brachistochrone — the curve of fastest descent — is the cycloid.

Historical note. Bernoulli posed this as a public challenge in 1696. Leibniz, Newton, L’Hôpital, and Jacob Bernoulli all solved it. Newton submitted his solution anonymously; Bernoulli reportedly recognized it: “I recognize the lion by his paw.” The problem directly motivated Euler and Lagrange to develop the calculus of variations — the mathematics of optimizing functionals.

9. Fourier Series of the Cycloid

The y-coordinate of the cycloid has the exact Fourier series:

y(t)=r(1−cost).

This is already a finite Fourier series — just two terms. The x-coordinate, however, involves the linear term rt which is not periodic. For one arch, it’s the finite Fourier polynomial in t plus the linear growth.

More interesting: the curvature κ(t)=4rsin(t/2)1​ has a Fourier series: sin(t/2)1​=2∑n=0∞​sin!((2n+1)2t​)⋅(coefficients)…

The velocity squared: v2(t)=4r2g(1−cost)=8r2gsin2(t/2)=4r2g(1−cost) which is a finite Fourier polynomial. This simplicity — that the cycloid is a trigonometric curve — is why every integral computes exactly.

10. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

10.1 The Brachistochrone as Optimal Control

The brachistochrone is the prototype of an optimal control problem: miny(⋅)​T[y]=∫F(y,y′),dx,subject to boundary conditions.

Modern reinforcement learning (RL) solves a discrete analogue: find a policy π (the “curve”) minimizing cumulative cost (the “time”). The Euler–Lagrange equation is the continuous limit of the Bellman equation: V(s)=mina​[c(s,a)+γV(s′)]⟶F−y′∂y′∂F​=C.

The brachistochrone was solved in 1696. RL is solving its discrete, high-dimensional, stochastic generalization.

10.2 The Tautochrone as Isochronous Training

The tautochrone says: all starting points reach the optimum in the same time. This is an idealized version of what ML practitioners want from training: convergence rate independent of initialization.

In practice, training is not isochronous — convergence depends heavily on initialization (like a circle, not a cycloid). Research on loss landscape geometry (Goodfellow et al., Li et al.) studies whether there are “cycloid-like” loss landscapes where all paths reach the minimum equally fast. Presently, this is an open problem.

10.3 Cycloid-Shaped Learning Rate Schedules

The cycloid’s smooth variation between zero (at cusps) and maximum (at the top) — and specifically its exact parametric form — inspires cyclical learning rate schedules (Smith, 2017): η(t)=ηmin​+(ηmax​−ηmin​)⋅f(t), where f(t) is a periodic function, typically triangular or cosine. The cosine annealing schedule: η(t)=ηmin​+2ηmax​−ηmin​​(1−cos!(Tπt​)), is precisely the y-coordinate of the cycloid (with r=(ηmax​−ηmin​)/2) as a function of the parameter t! This is not coincidence — both are designed to smoothly vary between 0 and a maximum with zero derivative at the extremes.

10.4 Calculus of Variations → Variational Autoencoders

The brachistochrone problem minimizes a functional (integral over a path). The VAE objective is: L(θ,ϕ)=Eqϕ​(z∣x)​[logpθ​(x∣z)]−DKL​(qϕ​(z∣x)∣p(z)),

which is also a functional — an expectation (integral) over the latent space path. The Euler–Lagrange equation is the analog of the VAE optimality condition. Variational inference is the calculus of variations applied to probability distributions.

  1. Derive the parametric equations x=r(t−sint), y=r(1−cost) from scratch, tracking the geometry of the rolling circle carefully (§2).
  2. Verify the curvature formula κ=1/(4rsin(t/2)) (Theorem 6.1) by filling in all the algebra from the curvature formula κ=∣x′y′′−y′x′′∣/(x′2+y′2)3/2.

  4. Prove the area result A=3πr2 (Theorem 5.1) by expanding (1−cost)2 and integrating term by term.
