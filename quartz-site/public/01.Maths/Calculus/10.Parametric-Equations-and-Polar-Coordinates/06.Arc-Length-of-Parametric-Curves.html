“Distance is not a gift the curve gives you — it must be earned, infinitesimal by infinitesimal.”

§0 — Motivation

In Cartesian calculus, arc length of y=F(x) from x=a to x=b is:

L=∫ab​1+(dxdy​)2​,dx

But this formula secretly assumes x increases monotonically along the curve. The moment the curve doubles back, spirals, or loops — as virtually every interesting curve in science and engineering does — this formula breaks down entirely.

The parametric formulation fixes this. Instead of describing the curve as a graph, we describe it as a trajectory: a particle moving through the plane, its position (x(t),y(t)) varying with time t. Arc length becomes the total distance traveled — and that is a concept that makes sense regardless of how the curve bends, reverses, or loops.

§1 — Derivation from First Principles

1.1 Polygonal Approximation

Partition [α,β] into n subintervals by α=t0​<t1​<⋯<tn​=β. The curve is approximated by the inscribed polygon with vertices at (f(ti​),g(ti​)).

The length of the i-th chord is:

ℓi​=(f(ti​)−f(ti−1​))2+(g(ti​)−g(ti−1​))2​

By the Mean Value Theorem, applied to f and g separately on [ti−1​,ti​]: there exist ti∗​,ti∗∗​∈(ti−1​,ti​) such that

f(ti​)−f(ti−1​)=f′(ti∗​),Δti​,g(ti​)−g(ti−1​)=g′(ti∗∗​),Δti​

So:

ℓi​=f′(ti∗​)2+g′(ti∗∗​)2​,Δti​

Summing:

L≈∑i=1n​f′(ti∗​)2+g′(ti∗∗​)2​,Δti​

Subtlety. ti∗​ and ti∗∗​ are generally different sample points — so this is not quite a Riemann sum for f′2+g′2​. The rigorous argument uses the fact that if f′ and g′ are continuous, then as ∣Δ∣→0, both sample points converge to the same limiting behavior, and the sum converges to the integral below. This is the content of the arc length theorem in real analysis.

Taking n→∞ (and ∣Δ∣→0):

L=∫αβ​(dtdx​)2+(dtdy​)2​,dt=∫αβ​f′(t)2+g′(t)2​,dt​

1.2 The Infinitesimal Arc Element

Define the arc length element ds:

ds=(dtdx​)2+(dtdy​)2​,dt=∣r′(t)∣,dt

This is the speed of the particle times the time increment: ds=∣v(t)∣,dt. Arc length is just the integral of speed over time — total distance traveled.

L=∫αβ​∣r′(t)∣,dt=∫αβ​∣v(t)∣,dt

This is the fundamental formula of kinematics, reinterpreted as geometry.

§2 — The Speed Vector Perspective

Let r(t)=⟨f(t),g(t)⟩. Then:

  • Velocity vector: v(t)=r′(t)=⟨f′(t),g′(t)⟩
  • Speed (scalar): ∣v(t)∣=f′(t)2+g′(t)2​
  • Arc length: L=∫αβ​∣v(t)∣,dt

The speed ∣v(t)∣ is independent of how the curve is parametrized — it depends on both the curve’s shape and the rate of traversal. This is why two different parametrizations of the same geometric curve can give the same L but different integrands.

2.1 Natural (Arc Length) Parametrization

Define the arc length function from a base point t=α:

s(t)=∫αt​f′(u)2+g′(u)2​,du

Then dtds​=f′(t)2+g′(t)2​=∣r′(t)∣.

If we reparametrize the curve by s (arc length), we get r(s) with the remarkable property:

​dsdr​​=1for all s

The unit tangent vector is then simply T^=dsdr​. This is the natural parametrization — the curve traversed at unit speed — and it is the foundation of differential geometry (curvature, Frenet–Serret frames, etc.).

Remark. In practice, s(t) is rarely expressible in closed form, so reparametrizing explicitly is often impossible. But the existence of arc length parametrization is conceptually essential.

§3 — Relation to the Cartesian Formula

Suppose y=F(x), i.e., x=t, y=F(t). Then f′(t)=1, g′(t)=F′(t), and:

L=∫ab​1+F′(t)2​,dt=∫ab​1+(dxdy​)2​,dx

The Cartesian formula is a special case of the parametric formula — with the trivial parametrization x=t.

§4 — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Circumference of a Circle

x=rcost,y=rsint,t∈[0,2π]

f′(t)=−rsint,g′(t)=rcost

L=∫02π​r2sin2t+r2cos2t​,dt=∫02π​r,dt=2πr✓

A sanity check: the parametric formula recovers the most ancient result in geometry.

Example 2 — Arc Length of One Arch of the Cycloid

x=r(t−sint),y=r(1−cost),t∈[0,2π]

f′(t)=r(1−cost),g′(t)=rsint

f′(t)2+g′(t)2=r2[(1−cost)2+sin2t]=r2[1−2cost+cos2t+sin2t]=r2[2−2cost]

Using the half-angle identity 1−cost=2sin2(t/2):

=2r2(1−cost)=4r2sin2!(2t​)

Therefore f′2+g′2​=2r∣sin(t/2)∣=2rsin(t/2) (since sin(t/2)≥0 for t∈[0,2π]).

L=∫02π​2rsin!(2t​)dt=2r[−2cos!(2t​)]02π​=2r[−2cosπ+2cos0]=2r(2+2)=8r

L=8r​

The arc length of one cycloid arch is exactly 8 radii of the rolling circle — a beautiful, clean answer from what looked like a messy integral.

Example 3 — The Astroid

x=acos3t,y=asin3t,t∈[0,2π]

f′(t)=−3acos2tsint,g′(t)=3asin2tcost

f′2+g′2=9a2cos4tsin2t+9a2sin4tcos2t=9a2sin2tcos2t(cos2t+sin2t)=9a2sin2tcos2t

f′2+g′2​=3a∣sintcost∣=23a​∣sin2t∣

By symmetry across the four quadrants:

L=4∫0π/2​23a​sin2t,dt=6a[−2cos2t​]0π/2​=6a[21​+21​]=6a

L=6a​

The astroid’s perimeter is 6a — exactly π6​ times the circumference of its circumscribed circle. Remarkably clean.

Example 4 — Logarithmic Spiral

x=etcost,y=etsint,t∈[0,T]

f′(t)=et(cost−sint),g′(t)=et(sint+cost)

f′2+g′2=e2t[(cost−sint)2+(sint+cost)2]=e2t[2cos2t+2sin2t]=2e2t

L=∫0T​2​,et,dt=2​(eT−1)

The logarithmic spiral has finite arc length to any finite T, and it grows exponentially with T.

§6 — Surface Area of Revolution (Extension)

When the parametric curve is revolved around the x-axis, the surface area is:

S=∫αβ​2π,∣g(t)∣,f′(t)2+g′(t)2​,dt=∫αβ​2π,∣y∣,ds

Around the y-axis:

S=∫αβ​2π,∣f(t)∣,f′(t)2+g′(t)2​,dt=∫αβ​2π,∣x∣,ds

The integrand 2π∣y∣,ds has a clear geometric meaning: each infinitesimal arc element ds sweeps out a thin band of circumference 2π∣y∣ — a “ring” of area 2π∣y∣,ds.

Example. Sphere from semicircle x=rcost,,y=rsint,,t∈[0,π], revolved around x-axis:

S=∫0π​2πrsint⋅r,dt=2πr2∫0π​sint,dt=2πr2⋅2=4πr2✓

§7 — Why Most Arc Length Integrals Are Non-Elementary

The integrand f′(t)2+g′(t)2​ involves a square root of a sum of squares. For most natural choices of f and g, this does not integrate in closed form. Specifically:

  • Ellipse x=acost,,y=bsint: the integrand becomes a2sin2t+b2cos2t​, which integrates to an elliptic integral — a transcendental function not expressible in elementary terms. This is why the perimeter of an ellipse has no simple formula.
  • Parabola, sine curve, most polynomials: similar non-elementary integrands.

The fact that the circle (a=b=r), the cycloid, and the astroid do yield clean answers is remarkable — and is why these curves appeared repeatedly in 17th–19th century mathematics.

This is not a failure of technique. Liouville’s theorem in differential algebra proves that such integrals are genuinely non-elementary for most curves.

§8 — Rigorous Summary

Theorem (Parametric Arc Length). Let r(t)=(f(t),g(t)) where f′ and g′ are continuous on [α,β]. Then the arc length of the curve from t=α to t=β is: L=∫αβ​f′(t)2+g′(t)2​,dt

Theorem (Parametrization Independence). If r(t) and R(u) are two smooth parametrizations of the same oriented curve, related by a smooth bijection t=ϕ(u) with ϕ′(u)>0, then both give the same arc length L.

Proof sketch. Substituting t=ϕ(u), dt=ϕ′(u),du, and f′(t)=dudf​⋅ϕ′(u)1​ — the ϕ′(u) factors cancel, leaving the same integrand in u. □

Corollary. Arc length is an intrinsic geometric property of the curve, independent of parametrization.

§9 — Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

9.1 Gradient Descent Trajectory Length

Training dynamics in neural networks define a parametric curve θ(t) in weight space Rn. The total path length of the optimization trajectory is:

L=∫0T​∣θ′(t)∣,dt=∫0T​∣∇θ​L(θ(t))∣⋅η,dt

(in continuous gradient flow). This is the arc length of the loss descent curve. Research has shown that flatter loss landscapes (small ∣∇L∣, i.e., low speed along the trajectory) correlate with better generalization — and the total trajectory length is a measure of how much the optimizer “traveled” to reach a solution.

9.2 Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Path Length

In continual learning, EWC penalizes large moves in weight space between tasks. The penalty is related to the squared ℓ2​ distance traveled:

LEWC​=LB​(θ)+2λ​∑i​Fi​(θi​−θA,i∗​)2

where Fi​ approximates the local metric (Fisher information). This is a discretization of arc length in a Riemannian metric on weight space — the Fisher metric. The arc length integral ∫∣r′(t)∣,dt generalizes to ∫r′(t)TF(θ(t)),r′(t)​,dt in the Fisher geometry.

9.3 Natural Parametrization and Positional Encodings

In Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) — used in LLaMA, Mistral, and most modern LLMs — the position encoding rotates token embeddings by an angle proportional to position:

qm​=RΘ,m​,qm​

where RΘ,m​ is a block-diagonal rotation matrix. The positions m are the arc length parameter of a curve on the unit circle in each frequency subspace. The choice of unit-speed parametrization (equal angular increments per position) is precisely the natural (arc length) parametrization of the circular trajectory — ensuring uniform spacing in the embedding space.

9.4 Curve Length as a Regularizer in Shape Models

In active contour models (Snakes, used in medical image segmentation), the energy functional to minimize includes an arc length regularization term:

E[r]=∫01​[α∣r′(s)∣2+β∣r′′(s)∣2]ds+Eimage​[r]

The first term α∫∣r′∣2,ds penalizes long contours (arc length proxy). The second penalizes high curvature (bending). This is a calculus of variations problem over parametric curves — and modern deep learning versions (DeepSnake, E2EC) learn to minimize similar energies via neural networks.

9.5 Riemannian Geometry and Geodesics in Latent Space

In VAEs and generative models, the latent space carries a non-Euclidean geometry induced by the decoder. The geodesic (shortest path) between two latent points z0​ and z1​ is the curve γ(t) minimizing:

L[γ]=∫01​γ′(t)TG(γ(t)),γ′(t)​,dt

where G(z) is the pullback metric (Riemannian metric induced by the decoder Jacobian). This is the parametric arc length formula in a curved space — the direct generalization of ∫f′2+g′2​,dt to a Riemannian manifold. Interpolating in latent space along geodesics (instead of straight lines) produces semantically smoother interpolations between generated images.

9.6 Sequence Length and Attention Complexity

In transformers, the sequence length n is treated as a discrete arc length — the number of tokens is the number of steps along the “curve” of a document. The quadratic O(n2) complexity of self-attention comes from computing all pairwise interactions along this sequence. Efficient transformers (Longformer, Big Bird) approximate this by attending only to nearby positions — mimicking the local nature of the arc length element ds, which only sees infinitesimal neighborhoods.

§11 — Common Pitfalls

  1. Forgetting the square root is of a sum, not a sum of square roots. f′2+g′2​=∣f′∣+∣g′∣.

  2. Assuming the formula gives displacement. Arc length is total distance traveled, not ∣r(β)−r(α)∣. A curve that loops back still accumulates length on the return trip.

  3. Missing absolute values in ∣sin(t/2)∣. In the cycloid derivation, sin(t/2)≥0 on [0,2π], so it’s fine — but on extended intervals, check the sign.

  4. Confusing arc length with area. Area: ∫g(t),f′(t),dt. Arc length: ∫f′2+g′2​,dt. The geometric content is completely different.

  5. Parametrization independence. Two different parametrizations of the same curve give the same L. The integrand changes, but the integral does not.
