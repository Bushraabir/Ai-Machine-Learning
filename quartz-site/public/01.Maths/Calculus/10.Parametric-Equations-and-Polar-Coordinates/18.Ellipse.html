18.Ellipse

The ellipse is a circle that has been honestly stretched. Everything about it — its foci, its reflective property, its area — flows from one number: how much it has been stretched.

1. The Geometric Definition

Definition. An ellipse is the set of all points P in the plane such that the sum of distances from P to two fixed points F1​ and F2​ (the foci) is constant.

Ellipse=,P∈R2:∣PF1​∣+∣PF2​∣=2a,

where 2a is the constant sum, a>0.

Why 2a? When P is at a vertex (end of the major axis), one distance is a−c and the other is a+c, summing to 2a. This is the calibration.

        P
       /|\
      / | \
     /  |  \
    /   |   \
F₁ *---+---* F₂        |F₁F₂| = 2c
    \   |   /
     \  |  /
      \ | /
       \|/

  |PF₁| + |PF₂| = 2a  (constant, for every P on the ellipse)

Three fundamental constants:

SymbolMeaningConstraint
asemi-major axis (half the long diameter)a>0
bsemi-minor axis (half the short diameter)0<b≤a
cfocal distance (center to focus)c≥0

The fundamental relation:

a2=b2+c2​

Proof. Place P at the end of the minor axis: (0,b) with foci at (±c,0). Then ∣PF1​∣=∣PF2​∣=a (by symmetry), and by Pythagoras: a2=b2+c2. □

2. Deriving the Equation

Place center at origin, foci at F1​=(−c,0) and F2​=(c,0).

Let P=(x,y). The definition gives:

(x+c)2+y2​+(x−c)2+y2​=2a

Move one radical: (x+c)2+y2​=2a−(x−c)2+y2​

Square: (x+c)2+y2=4a2−4a(x−c)2+y2​+(x−c)2+y2

Simplify the left minus right: 4cx−4a2=−4a(x−c)2+y2​

a2−cx=a(x−c)2+y2​

Square again: a4−2a2cx+c2x2=a2(x2−2cx+c2+y2)

a4−c2x2−a2y2=a4−a2c2⟹(a2−c2)x2+a2y2=a2(a2−c2)

Since b2=a2−c2:

b2x2+a2y2=a2b2

a2x2​+b2y2​=1​

3. Standard Forms

Horizontal major axis (a>b>0, foci on x-axis)

a2x2​+b2y2​=1

  • Center: (0,0)
  • Vertices: (±a,0)
  • Co-vertices: (0,±b)
  • Foci: (±c,0), where c=a2−b2​

Vertical major axis (a>b>0, foci on y-axis)

b2x2​+a2y2​=1

  • Center: (0,0)
  • Vertices: (0,±a)
  • Co-vertices: (±b,0)
  • Foci: (0,±c), where c=a2−b2​

Rule: The larger denominator is always a2, and the foci lie on the axis of the larger denominator.

Shifted ellipse (center at (h,k))

a2(x−h)2​+b2(y−k)2​=1

4. Eccentricity

e=ac​,0≤e<1​

eShape
e=0Perfect circle (c=0, a=b)
e→0Nearly circular
e→1Very elongated (nearly degenerate)

Eccentricity measures how far the foci are from center relative to the semi-major axis. It is the single number encoding the “shape” of the ellipse — all ellipses with the same e are similar.

Alternative definition via focus-directrix:

Each focus has a corresponding directrix. For focus F1​=(−c,0), the directrix is x=−ea​=−ca2​. The ellipse satisfies:

d(P,ℓ1​)∣PF1​∣​=efor every P on the ellipse

5. Key Geometric Features

5.1 Latus Rectum

The chord through a focus perpendicular to the major axis. Its half-length:

At x=c in a2x2​+b2y2​=1:

a2c2​+b2y2​=1⟹y2=b2(1−a2c2​)=a2b4​

Length of latus rectum=a2b2​

5.2 The Reflective (Optical) Property

Theorem. A ray from one focus reflects off the ellipse and passes through the other focus.

Proof sketch. At point P on the ellipse, the tangent line makes equal angles with PF1​​ and PF2​​. This follows because the tangent is the external angle bisector of ∠F1​PF2​ — a consequence of the fact that the ellipse is the locus where ∣PF1​∣+∣PF2​∣ is constant (Fermat’s principle / equal-angle reflection). □

Applications: Whispering galleries (St. Paul’s Cathedral, Capitol building), lithotripsy (focusing ultrasound to shatter kidney stones), ellipsoidal mirrors in optics.

       F₁ ----→ reflects → F₂
       
  ___________________________
 /                           \
|    F₁ *         * F₂       |
 \___________________________/

  Every ray from F₁ bounces to F₂.

5.3 Tangent Line

At point (x0​,y0​) on a2x2​+b2y2​=1:

a2x,x0​​+b2y,y0​​=1​

This is the contact equation — symmetric and elegant. Compare with the tangent to a circle x2+y2=r2 at (x0​,y0​): xx0​+yy0​=r2. The ellipse tangent is the same with denominators a2,b2.

Tangent with slope m:

y=mx±a2m2+b2​

6. Area and Perimeter

Area

A=πab​

Derivation. The ellipse a2x2​+b2y2​=1 is a circle x2+y2=1 scaled by x↦ax, y↦by. The Jacobian of this map is ab. Since a unit disk has area π:

A=π⋅ab

When a=b=r: A=πr2. ✓

Perimeter (No Closed Form!)

The perimeter of an ellipse cannot be expressed in elementary functions. It is:

P=4a∫0π/2​1−e2sin2θ​,dθ=4aE(e)

where E(e) is the complete elliptic integral of the second kind — a transcendental function.

Ramanujan’s approximation (remarkably accurate):

P≈π[3(a+b)−(3a+b)(a+3b)​]

Simple bounds:

2πb≤P≤2πa(from circumscribed/inscribed circles)

P≈2π2a2+b2​​(crude approximation)

7. Parametric Form

x=acost,y=bsint,t∈[0,2π)

Verify: a2a2cos2t​+b2b2sin2t​=cos2t+sin2t=1. ✓

  • t=0: right vertex (a,0)
  • t=π/2: top co-vertex (0,b)
  • t=π: left vertex (−a,0)

Velocity: r˙=(−asint,,bcost)

Speed: ∣r˙∣=a2sin2t+b2cos2t​

The arc length integral ∫02π​∣r˙∣,dt is exactly the elliptic integral 4aE(e) above.

8. Polar Form (Focus at Pole)

With one focus at the origin:

r=1−ecosθa(1−e2)​=1−ecosθb2/a​=1−ecosθℓ​

where ℓ=ab2​ is the semi-latus rectum.

θr
0 (periapsis)rmin​=a(1−e)=a−c
π (apoapsis)rmax​=a(1+e)=a+c
π/2r=ℓ=b2/a

Note: rmin​+rmax​=2a, consistent with ∣PF1​∣+∣PF2​∣=2a at the vertices.

9. Conjugate Diameters and the General Equation

General Second-Degree Form

Ax2+Bxy+Cy2+Dx+Ey+F=0

is an ellipse when B2−4AC<0 (and the conic is non-degenerate).

Reduce by completing the square → shifted standard form.

Matrix Form

x⊤Mx=1,M=(1/a2​0 0​1/b2​)

For a rotated ellipse: M is a positive definite symmetric matrix. Its eigenvalues are 1/a2 and 1/b2; its eigenvectors are the axes of the ellipse.

10. Worked Examples

Example 1: Find Everything from the Equation

Given 25x2​+9y2​=1.

  • a2=25⇒a=5 (major axis horizontal, larger denominator)
  • b2=9⇒b=3
  • c=25−9​=16​=4
  • e=c/a=4/5=0.8
  • Foci: (±4,0)
  • Vertices: (±5,0)
  • Co-vertices: (0,±3)
  • Latus rectum: 2b2/a=18/5=3.6
  • Area: π⋅5⋅3=15π

Example 2: Find the Equation

Foci at (0,±3), sum of distances =10.

  • Foci on y-axis → vertical major axis
  • 2a=10⇒a=5, so a2=25
  • c=3, so b2=a2−c2=25−9=16

16x2​+25y2​=1

Example 3: Shifted Ellipse

4x2+9y2−16x+18y−11=0

Complete the square:

4(x2−4x)+9(y2+2y)=11

4(x−2)2−16+9(y+1)2−9=11

4(x−2)2+9(y+1)2=36

9(x−2)2​+4(y+1)2​=1

Center (2,−1), a=3, b=2, c=5​, horizontal major axis.

12. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

12.1 Ellipsoidal Level Sets of the Gaussian Distribution

The multivariate Gaussian:

p(x)=(2π)d/2∣Σ∣1/21​exp!(−21​(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ))

has ellipsoidal level sets. The equation (x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ)=k2 is the d-dimensional ellipsoid (ellipse in 2D) with axes aligned to the eigenvectors of Σ, with semi-axis lengths kλi​​ where λi​ are eigenvalues of Σ.

Every time you compute a Gaussian distribution — in a VAE, a Kalman filter, a Gaussian process, naive Bayes — you are computing with ellipses.

12.2 Covariance Ellipse and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA finds the axes of the covariance ellipse of the data:

Σ^=n1​∑i=1n​(xi​−xˉ)(xi​−xˉ)⊤

The principal components are the eigenvectors of Σ^, and the eigenvalues λ1​≥λ2​≥⋯ are the squared semi-axes of the data’s covariance ellipse. The eccentricity of this ellipse measures how much the data varies in one direction vs another — a nearly circular covariance ellipse means all directions are equally informative; a thin, elongated ellipse means the data lies nearly on a line.

Dimensionality reduction = projecting onto the longest axes of the ellipse.

12.3 Trust Region Methods in Optimization

Trust region optimization (used in second-order methods like TRPO in reinforcement learning) constrains the update step Δw to lie inside an ellipsoidal region:

Δw⊤H,Δw≤δ

where H is the Hessian (or Fisher information matrix). This is an ellipsoid in parameter space — its shape is determined by the local curvature of the loss. Directions of high curvature get short semi-axes (small allowed steps); directions of low curvature get long semi-axes. The optimal step is the eigenvector of H corresponding to the steepest descent direction, constrained to this ellipsoid.

TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization) uses a KL-divergence ellipsoid to constrain policy updates — the geometry is fundamentally ellipsoidal.

12.4 The Fisher Information Ellipse and Natural Gradient

The Fisher information matrix F defines a Riemannian metric on the parameter space of a statistical model. Its level sets are ellipses. The natural gradient:

∇~L=F−1∇L

rescales the gradient to account for the ellipsoidal geometry of the parameter space. Standard gradient descent treats parameter space as isotropic (circular level sets); natural gradient descent uses the true ellipsoidal geometry. This is why natural gradient / K-FAC methods converge faster — they respect the intrinsic ellipsoidal structure.

12.5 Elliptic Embeddings and Knowledge Graphs

Recent work (Vilnis et al., Box Embeddings; Peng et al., Hyperbolic Embeddings) embeds entities in ellipsoidal regions rather than points. An entity is represented as a Gaussian (equivalently, an ellipse in 2D), and entailment/subsumption is modeled as containment of ellipses:

"A is-a B"⟺ellipseA​⊂ellipseB​

The volume of the ellipse (πab) encodes the generality of the concept: large ellipses = general concepts, small ellipses = specific ones.

12.6 Elliptical Attention and Anisotropic Kernels

Standard dot-product attention computes softmax(q⊤k/d​) — a spherical similarity. Elliptical attention generalizes this to:

score(q,k)=exp!(−(q−k)⊤M(q−k))

where M is a learned positive definite matrix. The similarity decays along ellipsoidal contours, allowing the model to learn anisotropic feature relevance — some feature dimensions matter more than others for a given query.
