12.Other Models of Population Growth

12.Other Models of Population Growth

Apr 19, 202613 min read

“The logistic model is elegant but rigid — it assumes a fixed carrying capacity, symmetric growth, and no memory. The real world is messier. Each modification of the logistic equation is a lesson in how a single extra parameter can shatter simplicity and birth entirely new phenomena.”

1. The Zoo of Growth Models — Overview

All population models specify the per-capita growth rate P1​dtdP​. The models differ in how this rate depends on P:

ModelPer-capita rate P1​P˙ODE
Malthus (exponential)r (constant)P˙=rP
Logistic (Verhulst)r(1−P/K) (linear decay)P˙=rP(1−P/K)
Gompertzrln(K/P) (log decay)P˙=rPln(K/P)
Allee effectr(1−P/K)(P/A−1)P˙=rP(1−P/K)(P/A−1)
Theta-logisticr(1−(P/K)θ)P˙=rP(1−(P/K)θ)
DoomsdayrP1+ϵP˙=rP1+ϵ
Harvestingr(1−P/K)−H/PP˙=rP(1−P/K)−H

Each model is a separable first-order ODE (or reducible to one). The solution techniques are the same; the qualitative behavior is radically different.

2. The Gompertz Model

2.1 Setup

Proposed by Benjamin Gompertz (1825), originally for mortality curves. The per-capita growth rate decays logarithmically rather than linearly:

\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\ln\frac{K}{P} \tag{1}

Note: ln(K/P)=lnK−lnP>0 when P<K, and <0 when P>K. So the qualitative behavior is similar to the logistic, but the approach to K is asymmetric — slower from below, faster from above.

2.2 Solving the Gompertz ODE

Rewrite: ln(K/P)=lnK−lnP.

Separate:

Pln(K/P)dP​=r,dt

Substitution: Let u=ln(K/P). Then:

dPdu​=−P1​⟹du=−PdP​

So Pln(K/P)dP​=u−du​. The ODE becomes:

−udu​=r,dt⟹udu​=−r,dt

ln∣u∣=−rt+C1​⟹u=Ae−rt

Back-substitute u=ln(K/P):

lnPK​=Ae−rt

PK​=eAe−rt

\boxed{P(t) = K, e^{-Ae^{-rt}}} \tag{2}

Apply P(0)=P0​:

P0​=Ke−A⟹A=lnP0​K​

P(t) = K\exp!\left(-\ln!\tfrac{K}{P_0}\cdot e^{-rt}\right) = K\left(\frac{P_0}{K}\right)^{e^{-rt}} \tag{3}

2.3 Key Properties

  • Equilibria: P∗=0 (unstable), P∗=K (stable) — same as logistic.
  • Inflection point: Set d2P/dt2=0.

From P˙=rPln(K/P):

P¨=rP˙lnPK​+rP⋅P−P˙​=rP˙(lnPK​−1)

Setting P¨=0 (with P˙=0):

lnPK​=1⟹P∗=eK​≈0.368K

The inflection is at P=K/e — lower than the logistic’s K/2. The Gompertz curve is skewed: it rises steeply early and tails off slowly into K.

 K  ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ────────────────────  ← K
                    ___/ ← Gompertz (slow tail)
K/2 ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─/─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─
K/e ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ── ←inflection (Gompertz)
               __/ ← logistic inflection at K/2
              /
─────────────/──────────────────────────────
     0                                   → t

2.4 Applications

  • Tumor growth: Gompertz fits the growth of solid tumors better than logistic — they slow down more gradually as they approach their maximum size.
  • Mortality tables: Gompertz’s original use — human mortality rate increases exponentially with age.
  • Technology adoption: Product adoption curves often fit Gompertz better than logistic (asymmetric S-curve).

3. The Allee Effect

3.1 Motivation

The logistic model assumes: more individuals → slower growth (crowding). The Allee effect (Warder Clyde Allee, 1931) adds: too few individuals → slower growth too (difficulty finding mates, reduced cooperative defense, inbreeding).

This creates a minimum viable population A below which the population declines to extinction.

3.2 The ODE

\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right)\left(\frac{P}{A} - 1\right), \quad 0 < A < K \tag{4}

Equilibria: P∗=0, P∗=A, P∗=K.

Stability analysis — let f(P)=rP(1−P/K)(P/A−1):

  • f′(0): near P=0, f(P)≈rP(1)(0/A−1)=−rP<0 → _P^_ = 0 is stable* (extinction attractor).
  • P∗=A: unstable (the tipping point).
  • P∗=K: stable (the survival attractor).

3.3 Phase Line

f(P) = dP/dt

      ↑
      │
   ───┼──────────────────────────────── P
      0    A              K
      │    ↑              ↑
   ←──│──→   ←───────────   ←──────────
  (-)│(+)       (-)             (+)... wait:

Sign of f(P) on each interval:
  P ∈ (0, A):   f < 0  →  P decreases to 0  (extinction)
  P ∈ (A, K):   f > 0  →  P increases to K  (survival)
  P ∈ (K, ∞):   f < 0  →  P decreases to K

Phase line:

  0 ←───── A ─────→ K ←─────
 (stable) (unstable) (stable)

The Allee threshold A is a separatrix: populations above A survive; below A they go extinct. This is called the strong Allee effect.

3.4 Solving the Allee ODE

The ODE (4) is separable. Partial fractions on P(1−P/K)(P/A−1)1​ are messy but tractable. The solution is implicitly defined, and the key qualitative feature is the existence of the threshold A — the exact formula is less important than the phase line.

3.5 Applications

  • Conservation biology: Minimum viable population estimates for endangered species.
  • Epidemiology: Below a critical infectious population, an epidemic dies out (Allee-like threshold = herd immunity complement).
  • Invasive species: A newly introduced population must exceed A to establish.

4. The Theta-Logistic (Generalized Logistic)

4.1 The ODE

\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left[1 - \left(\frac{P}{K}\right)^\theta\right], \quad \theta > 0 \tag{5}

The parameter θ controls the shape of density dependence:

  • θ=1: standard logistic.
  • θ<1: density dependence kicks in early (when P is still small). Concave per-capita rate.
  • θ>1: density dependence kicks in late (population grows almost freely until near K). Convex per-capita rate.

4.2 Inflection Point

Setting P¨=0 (similar calculation as before):

P∗=K(1+θ1​)1/θ

  • θ=1: P∗=K/2 ✓ (logistic)
  • θ→0+: P∗→K/e (approaches Gompertz inflection)
  • θ→∞: P∗→K (inflection pushed to the top — nearly J-shaped)

The theta-logistic is a one-parameter family interpolating between all major growth curves.

4.3 Solving

The ODE is separable:

P[1−(P/K)θ]dP​=r,dt

Substitute u=(P/K)θ:

du=θ(P/K)θ−1⋅K1​,dP=Pθ​(P/K)θ,dP=Pθu​,dP

So dP/P=du/(θu):

∫1−udu/(θu)​=rt+C

θ1​∫u(1−u)du​=rt+C

θ1​[lnu−ln(1−u)]=rt+C

θ1​ln1−(P/K)θ(P/K)θ​=rt+C

This defines P(t) implicitly. For θ=1 it reduces exactly to the logistic solution.

5. The Doomsday Model

5.1 The ODE

\frac{dP}{dt} = kP^{1+\epsilon}, \quad k > 0,, \epsilon > 0 \tag{6}

The per-capita rate kPϵ increases with P — no carrying capacity, the population accelerates itself.

5.2 Solving (Separable)

P−(1+ϵ),dP=k,dt

∫P−(1+ϵ),dP=kt+C

−ϵP−ϵ​=kt+C

P−ϵ=−ϵkt+C0​

Apply P(0)=P0​: C0​=P0−ϵ​:

P(t) = \left(P_0^{-\epsilon} - \epsilon k t\right)^{-1/\epsilon} \tag{7}

5.3 Finite-Time Blow-Up

The solution P(t)→∞ in finite time t∗=ϵkP0−ϵ​​=ϵkP0ϵ​1​.

This is called a doomsday singularity — the population grows so fast it becomes infinite in finite time. Obviously unphysical past some point, but the model fits certain superexponential growth phases (e.g., global human population from 1 AD to 1970, which von Foerster et al. famously fit with ϵ≈1 and t∗≈2026… an eerie coincidence with the present date).

     0                       t*    → t

6. The Harvesting Model

6.1 Constant Harvesting

\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right) - H, \quad H > 0 \tag{8}

Equilibria: Set P˙=0:

rP(1−KP​)=H⟹Kr​P2−rP+H=0

P∗=2rrK±r2K2−4rKH​​=2K​(1±1−rK4H​​)

Three regimes:

ConditionEquilibriaBehavior
H<4rK​Two real equilibria P−∗​<P+∗​P_+^_ stable, P_-^_ unstable
H=4rK​One equilibrium P∗=K/2Saddle-node bifurcation
H>4rK​No real equilibriaP˙<0 always — extinction

Maximum sustainable yield (MSY): H∗=rK/4, achieved at P=K/2.

dP/dt
  ↑         logistic curve rP(1-P/K)
  │        ╭──────╮
rK/4 ─ ─ ─│─ ─ ─╮│─ ─ ─ ─ ─  ← H = rK/4 (tangent: MSY)
  │       /    H₂ \\
  H₁ ─ ─/─ ─ ─ ─ ─\─ ─ ─ ─ ─  ← H₁ < rK/4 (two crossings: two equil.)
  │     /            \
──┼────/──────────────\──────── P
  0   P*₋             P*₊   K

6.2 Proportional Harvesting (Threshold Policy)

dtdP​=rP(1−KP​)−EP,E>0

=P[r(1−KP​)−E]=P[(r−E)−Kr​P]

This is still a logistic equation with modified growth rate r~=r−E and modified carrying capacity K~=K(1−E/r) (for E<r). Proportional harvesting preserves the logistic structure; constant harvesting does not.

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

8.1 Gompertz Activation and Loss Curves

The Gompertz curve appears in ML as a model for training loss dynamics. Loss curves are almost never symmetric S-curves — they drop rapidly early, then tail off extremely slowly. The Gompertz ODE’s inflection at K/e≈0.37K (lower than logistic’s K/2) means the fastest learning happens early, matching observed neural network training.

The Gompertz activation function:

G(x)=e−e−x

has been proposed as an alternative to sigmoid. It is the Gompertz solution evaluated at t=x with K=1, A=1. Unlike sigmoid, it is asymmetric: slower to saturate at 1 than at 0, which can be beneficial for modeling asymmetric distributions.

8.2 Allee Effect and Rare Event Learning

The Allee threshold has a direct analogue in few-shot and zero-shot learning: a model trained on fewer than A examples of a class fails to learn it (collapses to “extinction” — random guessing); above A examples it generalizes successfully.

More precisely, in memorization vs. generalization (grokking): the model first memorizes (population at P0​≪A), then — after enough weight norm regularization acts like environment pressure — crosses the Allee threshold and “generalizes” (escapes to the K-attractor). The Allee model is a minimal ODE that exhibits this threshold-crossing behavior.

8.3 Doomsday and Superexponential Scaling Laws

The neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) show that language model performance scales as a power law with compute:

L(C)∼C−α,α≈0.05

Equivalently, capability P∼Cα grows as a power law in compute C. If compute itself grows superexponentially (as it has historically), C(t)∼eβtγ, then P(t) grows superexponentially too — a doomsday-type trajectory in capability space.

The finite-time blow-up in the doomsday model is a mathematical caricature of the intelligence explosion hypothesis: if capability growth rate ∼Pϵ (smarter systems build better systems faster), the system reaches infinite capability in finite time t∗.

8.4 Theta-Logistic and Learning Rate Schedules

The theta-logistic’s parameter θ controls when density dependence (saturation) kicks in. In deep learning:

  • θ<1 regime (early saturation): analogous to aggressive learning rate decay — the model slows down early, converging cautiously but potentially to a sharper minimum.
  • θ>1 regime (late saturation): analogous to warmup schedules — the model grows nearly freely until close to convergence, then slows.

The cosine annealing schedule (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) has a theta-logistic-like shape with θ>1: it keeps the learning rate high for most of training, then decays sharply at the end.

8.5 Harvesting and Dropout / Regularization

Dropout removes a fraction H of activations at each forward pass. The effect on a single layer’s output is analogous to constant harvesting:

effective output=f(x)−H

For small H (low dropout rate), two stable regimes exist: high activation (analogous to P+∗​, the learned representation) and zero activation (analogous to P=0, dead neurons). For H above the MSY threshold, all activations collapse to zero — neuron death, the analogue of population collapse past the harvesting threshold.

This geometric analogy explains why too-high dropout rates cause training failure: they push the system past the bifurcation point where no stable “alive” equilibrium exists.

8.6 Compartmental Population Models and Graph Neural Networks

Multi-species population models (Lotka-Volterra predator-prey, SIR epidemics) are compartmental systems of ODEs:

dtdP​=F(P),P∈Rn

These are the continuous-time substrate of message-passing in Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). The GNN update:

hv(k+1)​=ϕ(hv(k)​, ⨁u∈N(v)​ψ(hu(k)​))

is an Euler step of a compartmental ODE on the graph, where each node v is a “species” exchanging “population” (features) with neighbors. The stability theory of population ODEs — equilibria, Jacobian eigenvalues, limit cycles — directly informs the over-smoothing problem in deep GNNs (features converging to a uniform fixed point, like all populations reaching the same carrying capacity).

9. Summary — The Hierarchy of Models

Malthus: dP/dt = rP
    ↓  (add ceiling K)
Logistic: dP/dt = rP(1 - P/K)
    ↓  (change linear → log saturation)     ↓  (add shape parameter θ)
Gompertz: rP ln(K/P)              Theta-logistic: rP[1-(P/K)^θ]
    ↓  (add minimum viable population A)
Allee: rP(1-P/K)(P/A - 1)
    ↓  (add external removal)
Harvesting: rP(1-P/K) - H
    ↑  (remove ceiling, add acceleration)
Doomsday: kP^{1+ε}  →  blow-up in finite time t*

10. Common Pitfalls

  1. Treating all models as separable without checking. All models in this note are separable (or reducible to separable via substitution). But multi-species models (Lotka-Volterra) are generally not separable — they require phase plane or linearization methods.
  2. Confusing Allee effect stability. In the strong Allee effect, P∗=0 is stable (extinction is an attractor). Students often expect 0 to be unstable as in the logistic. It is not — small populations go extinct.
  3. Forgetting θ‘s effect on units. In the theta-logistic, (P/K)θ is dimensionless for any θ, so r always has units 1/time regardless of θ.
  4. Missing the MSY bifurcation. In the harvesting model, the transition at H=rK/4 is a saddle-node bifurcation — two equilibria collide and annihilate. This is not a smooth transition; it is a sudden collapse. Fishing industries that ignore this threshold have historically caused stock collapses.
  5. Applying the Doomsday model globally. The doomsday ODE fits data over a finite interval. Extrapolation to t→t∗ is physically meaningless — the model breaks down long before blow-up (as Malthus himself understood about food limits).
