0. Orientation

A differential equation is a constraint on a function expressed through its own rates of change. Nature tends to know rates — not states. It knows how fast a population grows, how fast heat diffuses, how fast a voltage changes. The differential equation is the mathematical language in which nature speaks; our job is to decode it into explicit functions.

This note builds the full architecture: classification → existence/uniqueness → first-order methods → second-order theory → systems → qualitative analysis → the ML thread running through all of it.

1. What Is a Differential Equation?

A differential equation (DE) is an equation involving an unknown function and one or more of its derivatives.

General implicit form:

F!(x,,y,,y′,,y′′,,…,,y(n))=0

where y=y(x) is the unknown, x is the independent variable.

A solution is any function y=ϕ(x) defined on some interval I such that substituting ϕ and its derivatives into the equation produces a true identity for all x∈I.

2. Classification

Every DE should be classified along four axes before attempting a solution. Classification determines which tools apply.

2.1 Ordinary vs. Partial

2.2 Order

The order is the highest derivative present.

y′′+y=0(order 2),dxdy​=y2(order 1),y′′′=ex(order 3)

2.3 Linearity

An ODE of order n is linear if it has the form:

an​(x),y(n)+an−1​(x),y(n−1)+⋯+a1​(x),y′+a0​(x),y=g(x)

Key requirements:

  • The unknown y and each derivative y(k) appear to the first power.
  • No products like y⋅y′ or functions like sin(y), ey.

If these fail, the ODE is nonlinear.

2.4 Homogeneity (for Linear ODEs)

A linear ODE is:

  • Homogeneous if g(x)=0: an​y(n)+⋯+a0​y=0
  • Non-homogeneous (inhomogeneous) if g(x)=0

Warning: “Homogeneous” has a different (unrelated) meaning for first-order nonlinear ODEs — see §4.4.

3. Existence and Uniqueness

Before solving, we ask: does a solution exist, and is it unique? These are not pedantic questions — they tell you whether your model is well-posed.

3.1 First-Order IVP (Picard–Lindelöf Theorem)

Theorem (Picard–Lindelöf). Consider the initial value problem:

dxdy​=f(x,y),y(x0​)=y0​

If f is continuous in a rectangle R=∣x−x0​∣≤a,;∣y−y0​∣≤b and ∂f/∂y is continuous (equivalently, f is Lipschitz in y) on R, then:

  1. A solution exists on some interval ∣x−x0​∣<h.
  2. The solution is unique on that interval.

Lipschitz condition (precise): ∃,L>0 such that ∣f(x,y1​)−f(x,y2​)∣≤L∣y1​−y2​∣ for all (x,y1​),(x,y2​)∈R.

The Lipschitz constant L controls how fast nearby solutions can diverge — it is the local “sensitivity” of the ODE.

3.2 Failure of Uniqueness: A Classic Example

dxdy​=y1/3,y(0)=0

Here ∂f/∂y=31​y−2/3 blows up at y=0 — Lipschitz fails. Two distinct solutions pass through (0,0):

y1​(x)=0andy2​(x)=(32x​)3/2

Both satisfy the ODE and initial condition. Uniqueness fails exactly where Lipschitz fails. ■

3.3 Second-Order IVP

Theorem. For y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=G(x) with P,Q,G continuous on interval I and (x0​,y0​,y0′​)∈I×R2, there exists a unique solution on all of I.

Note: the solution interval is the entire interval of continuity of the coefficients — much stronger than the first-order result.

4. First-Order ODEs: Solution Methods

4.1 Separable Equations

Form: dxdy​=g(x),h(y)

Method: Separate and integrate.

h(y)dy​=g(x),dx⟹∫h(y)dy​=∫g(x),dx

Example: dxdy​=xy

ydy​=x,dx⟹ln∣y∣=2x2​+C⟹y=Aex2/2

This is the Gaussian! It appears as the fundamental solution to the heat equation and as the normal distribution in probability — the solution to the “most entropic” separable ODE.

4.2 Linear First-Order: Integrating Factor

Form: dxdy​+P(x)y=Q(x)

Integrating factor: μ(x)=e∫P(x),dx

Multiply both sides by μ:

dxd​[μ(x),y]=μ(x),Q(x)

Integrate:

y=μ(x)1​[∫μ(x),Q(x),dx+C]​

Why does this work? We are manufacturing a perfect derivative on the left. The product rule gives dxd​[μy]=μy′+μ′y. For this to equal μ(y′+Py), we need μ′=μP, i.e., μ=e∫P,dx. ■

Example: y′+x2​y=x2, x>0

μ=e∫2/x,dx=e2lnx=x2

dxd​[x2y]=x4⟹x2y=5x5​+C⟹y=5x3​+x2C​

4.3 Exact Equations

Form: M(x,y),dx+N(x,y),dy=0

Exactness condition: ∂y∂M​=∂x∂N​

If exact, there exists F(x,y) with Fx​=M, Fy​=N, and the solution is F(x,y)=C (a family of level curves).

Finding F:

F=∫M,dx+g(y)then solve Fy​=N for g′(y)

Example: (2xy+3),dx+(x2−4y3),dy=0

Check: My​=2x, Nx​=2x. ✓ Exact.

F=∫(2xy+3),dx=x2y+3x+g(y) Fy​=x2+g′(y)=x2−4y3⟹g′(y)=−4y3⟹g(y)=−y4

Solution: x2y+3x−y4=C

4.4 Homogeneous First-Order (Substitution v=y/x)

Form: dxdy​=F!(xy​)

Substitution: v=y/x, so y=vx, y′=v+xv′:

v+xv′=F(v)⟹F(v)−vdv​=xdx​

Now separable. Solve for v(x), then y=vx.

4.5 Bernoulli Equation

Form: y′+P(x)y=Q(x)yn, n=0,1

Substitution: w=y1−n, which linearizes the equation:

w′+(1−n)P(x)w=(1−n)Q(x)

Now a linear first-order ODE — solve by integrating factor.

5. Second-Order Linear ODEs

5.1 Structure Theorem

The complete general solution of the non-homogeneous linear ODE:

y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=G(x)

is:

y=yh​+yp​​

where:

  • yh​=C1​y1​+C2​y2​ is the general homogeneous solution (y1​,y2​ linearly independent solutions of the homogeneous equation),
  • yp​ is any particular solution of the full non-homogeneous equation.

Proof sketch: If y is any solution, then y−yp​ satisfies the homogeneous equation (by linearity), so y−yp​∈spany1​,y2​. ■

5.2 Constant Coefficients: The Characteristic Equation

For ay′′+by′+cy=0, try y=erx:

ar2+br+c=0⟹r=2a−b±b2−4ac​​

The discriminant Δ=b2−4ac governs the three cases:

Case 1: Δ>0 (two distinct real roots r1​=r2​)

yh​=C1​er1​x+C2​er2​x

Case 2: Δ=0 (repeated root r1​=r2​=r)

yh​=(C1​+C2​x),erx

Why xerx? We need two linearly independent solutions. Since erx is already one, we use reduction of order: assume y2​=v(x)erx, substitute, and solve for v(x)=x. ■

Case 3: Δ<0 (complex conjugate roots r=α±iβ)

yh​=eαx(C1​cosβx+C2​sinβx)

Euler’s formula eiθ=cosθ+isinθ converts complex exponentials to real sinusoids.

5.3 The Wronskian and Linear Independence

For two solutions y1​,y2​, the Wronskian is:

W(y1​,y2​)(x)=​y1​​y2​ y1′​​y2′​​​=y1​y2′​−y2​y1′​

Theorem (Abel): W(x)=W(x0​),exp!(−∫x0​x​P(t),dt)

Consequence: W is either identically zero (dependent) or never zero (independent) on I. There is no middle ground.

y1​,y2​ form a fundamental set of solutions ⟺ W(y1​,y2​)=0 on I.

5.4 Finding yp​: Method of Undetermined Coefficients

Applies when coefficients are constant and G(x) is of a “nice” type. Guess yp​ based on the form of G(x):

G(x)Trial yp​
Pn​(x) (polynomial degree n)xs(An​xn+⋯+A0​)
eαxxsAeαx
cosβx or sinβxxs(Acosβx+Bsinβx)
eαxcosβxxseαx(Acosβx+Bsinβx)
Sum of aboveSum of corresponding trials

The modification rule: s=0 normally; s=1 if the trial solution duplicates a term in yh​; s=2 if the duplication is with a repeated root.

Example: y′′−3y′+2y=ex

Homogeneous: r2−3r+2=0⇒r=1,2, so yh​=C1​ex+C2​e2x.

G=ex and ex already appears in yh​ (with r=1), so use yp​=Axex (s=1):

yp′​=Aex+Axex,yp′′​=2Aex+Axex

Substitute: (2Aex+Axex)−3(Aex+Axex)+2Axex=−Aex=ex

So A=−1, giving yp​=−xex.

5.5 Finding yp​: Variation of Parameters

Works for any G(x) (including non-elementary). Given yh​=C1​y1​+C2​y2​, assume:

yp​=u1​(x),y1​(x)+u2​(x),y2​(x)

Impose the auxiliary condition u1′​y1​+u2′​y2​=0 (suppresses second derivatives of u). Then substituting into the ODE gives:

u1′​y1′​+u2′​y2′​=G(x)

Solving this 2×2 linear system via Cramer’s rule:

u1′​=−Wy2​,G​,u2′​=Wy1​,G​​

Integrate to get u1​,u2​.

Why this works: We have two unknowns u1′​,u2′​ and two equations (the auxiliary condition + the ODE). The Wronskian W=0 guarantees the system is non-singular. ■

6. Systems of ODEs and Phase Plane Analysis

6.1 Converting to First-Order Systems

Any n-th order ODE can be converted to a system of n first-order ODEs. For y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x), let y1​=y, y2​=y′:

y˙​1​=y2​,y˙​2​=g−py2​−qy1​

In vector form: y˙​=Ay+b (if linear), or y˙​=f(y) (general).

This is the standard form for numerical solvers (Euler, Runge–Kutta) and for Neural ODEs.

6.2 Linear Autonomous Systems

x˙=Ax,A∈R2×2

The solution is x(t)=eAtx0​, where eAt=∑n=0∞​n!(At)n​ (matrix exponential).

The qualitative behavior depends entirely on the eigenvalues λ1​,λ2​ of A:

6.5 Stability: The Lyapunov Framework

Definition. The fixed point x∗ is:

  • Stable (Lyapunov): ∀ε>0, ∃δ>0 s.t. |\mathbf{x}(0) - \mathbf{x}^_| < \delta \implies |\mathbf{x}(t) - \mathbf{x}^_| < \varepsilon for all t>0.
  • Asymptotically stable: Stable and x(t)→x∗ as t→∞.
  • Unstable: Not stable.

Lyapunov’s Direct Method. If there exists V:Rn→R with:

  1. V(\mathbf{x}^_) = 0 and V(x)>0 for \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{x}^_,
  2. V˙(x)=∇V⋅f(x)≤0,

then x∗ is stable. If V˙<0 (strictly), it is asymptotically stable.

V is a “generalized energy” that the system dissipates. Finding V is an art — for quadratic systems, V=x⊤Px with P the solution to the Lyapunov matrix equation A⊤P+PA=−Q.

7. Numerical Methods: The Bridge to Computation

Analytic solutions are the exception. Numerical methods are the rule.

7.1 Euler’s Method

For y′=f(x,y), y(x0​)=y0​, with step h:

yn+1​=yn​+h,f(xn​,yn​)

This is a first-order Taylor approximation: y(x+h)≈y(x)+hy′(x).

Local truncation error: O(h2). Global error: O(h).

7.2 Improved Euler (Heun’s Method)

A predictor-corrector scheme:

y~​n+1​=yn​+h,f(xn​,yn​)(predict) yn+1​=yn​+2h​[f(xn​,yn​)+f(xn+1​,y~​n+1​)](correct)

Global error: O(h2).

7.3 Runge–Kutta (RK4)

The workhorse of scientific computing. Four slope evaluations per step:

k1​=f(xn​,,yn​) k2​=f!(xn​+2h​,,yn​+2h​k1​) k3​=f!(xn​+2h​,,yn​+2h​k2​) k4​=f!(xn​+h,,yn​+h,k3​) yn+1​=yn​+6h​(k1​+2k2​+2k3​+k4​)​

Global error: O(h4). The weighted average 61​(k1​+2k2​+2k3​+k4​) is a Simpson’s rule approximation to the integral ∫0h​f,dt.

8. A Brief Look at PDEs

The most important PDEs in science all arise from ODE-like local balance laws.

8.1 The Big Three

Heat (diffusion) equation: ∂t∂u​=α∂x2∂2u​ Models heat conduction, diffusion of particles, probability density evolution (Fokker–Planck).

Wave equation: ∂t2∂2u​=c2∂x2∂2u​ Models sound, light, vibrating strings. Second-order in both t and x — the spring ODE extended to a continuum.

Laplace/Poisson equation: ∇2u=0(Laplace),∇2u=f(Poisson) Models steady-state heat, electrostatics, fluid potential. Solutions are harmonic functions.

8.2 Separation of Variables for PDEs

The standard technique: assume u(x,t)=X(x)T(t). For the heat equation:

TT′​=αXX′′​=−λ(separation constant)

This yields two ODEs: T′=−λT⟹T(t)=e−λt X′′=−αλ​X⟹X(x)=Acos(μx)+Bsin(μx),μ=λ/α​

Boundary conditions on X force λ to take discrete values — eigenvalues — and the solution is a Fourier series. The PDE reduces to infinitely many decoupled ODEs.

9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The ODE is not a detour from ML — it is ML’s hidden foundation.

9.1 Every Gradient Flow Is an ODE

Training a neural network by gradient descent on loss L(θ) is, in the continuous limit, solving:

dtdθ​=−∇θ​L(θ)

This is a first-order autonomous ODE — a gradient flow. The minima of L are exactly the stable fixed points of this ODE. Saddle points are unstable fixed points. The entire theory of §6 (stability, phase portraits, Lyapunov functions) applies to understanding optimization.

9.2 Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018)

A ResNet layer: ht+1​=ht​+fθ​(ht​) is Euler’s method with h=1 applied to h˙=fθ​(h).

The Neural ODE takes the continuous limit: define the hidden state by the IVP:

dtdh​=fθ​(h(t),t),h(0)=x

The network “depth” becomes a continuous time variable. Output is h(T), computed by any ODE solver (e.g., RK45). Backpropagation through the solver uses the adjoint method — a classical ODE technique for sensitivity analysis.

The adjoint a(t)=∂L/∂h(t) satisfies a backward ODE:

dtda​=−a⊤∂h∂fθ​​

This is variation of parameters / the transpose of the linearized forward equation.

9.3 Stability ↔ Generalization

The damping theory of §5 (Spring note) and the stability theory of §6 both say: negative real parts of eigenvalues ⇒ convergence to a fixed point.

In neural networks:

  • The Jacobian J=∂fθ​/∂h at a fixed point plays the role of the matrix A.
  • If all eigenvalues of J have negative real parts: the network’s representation is stable — small input perturbations don’t explode. This is related to robustness and adversarial stability.
  • If eigenvalues have positive real parts: exploding activations or sensitivity to inputs.

9.4 Diffusion Models (Score-Based Generative Models)

The forward process of a diffusion model (DDPM, score matching) is the stochastic differential equation:

dx=−21​β(t),x,dt+β(t)​,dWt​

This is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process — a damped linear ODE with noise. Specifically:

  • The deterministic part −21​β(t)x is an exponentially decaying ODE (overdamped spring with time-varying k).
  • The noise dWt​ is Brownian motion.

The reverse process (generation = denoising) solves a backward SDE derived from the score function ∇x​logpt​(x). Learning this score is exactly learning the drift of an ODE.

Without understanding the forward/backward ODE, diffusion models are a black box. With it, they become a natural extension of the framework we’ve built.

9.5 Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs solve PDEs by embedding the equation as a loss term. For PDE N[u]=0:

L(θ)=data lossN1​i∑​∣uθ​(xi​,ti​)−udata​(xi​,ti​)∣2​​+physics lossM1​j∑​∣N[uθ​](xj​,tj​)∣2​​

The neural network uθ​ is trained to satisfy both data and the differential equation simultaneously. The network is the approximate solution to the PDE, and the ODE/PDE theory determines whether the solution is physically reasonable.

9.6 Numerical Solvers Inside ML

Modern ML frameworks (JAX, PyTorch) include differentiable ODE solvers (torchdiffeq, diffrax). These compute ∂θ∂​∫0T​fθ​(h,t),dt using the adjoint method. Every concept from §7 (Euler, RK4, step size, stability) is directly relevant to training speed and numerical accuracy.

12. Exercises (Self-Check)

  1. Classification. Classify each as order/linear/homogeneous: (a) y′′+(sinx)y=ex, (b) yy′=x, (c) y′′′−y=0, (d) y′+y2=1.

  2. Non-uniqueness. Verify that both y=0 and y=(x/2)2 solve y′=y​, y(0)=0. Where does Picard–Lindelöf fail?

  3. Integrating factor. Solve y′+(cotx)y=cosx. (Hint: ∫cotx,dx=ln∣sinx∣.)

  4. Exact equation. Show (exsiny+2x)dx+(excosy+2y)dy=0 is exact and find the general solution.

  5. Wronskian. For y′′−y=0 with solutions y1​=ex, y2​=e−x: compute W, verify Abel’s theorem, and confirm the discriminant of the characteristic equation matches.

  6. Variation of parameters. Solve y′′+y=secx using variation of parameters. (The method of undetermined coefficients fails here — why?)

  7. Phase portrait. For x˙=(−1​2 0​−3​)x: find eigenvalues, classify the fixed point, sketch the phase portrait.

  8. Neural ODE. Write the spring equation x¨+2γx˙+ω02​x=0 as a first-order system. Apply Euler’s method with step h. For what values of h does the discrete system remain stable? (Compare ∣λh∣≤2 for Euler stability.)

  9. Gradient flow. For L(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta^_)^2, solve the gradient flow ODE θ˙=−L′(θ) explicitly. Show it is an integrating-factor problem. At what rate does \theta(t) \to \theta^_?

  10. Diffusion intuition. The forward diffusion SDE has deterministic part x˙=−2β​x. Solve this ODE. What does the solution say about the long-term fate of any initial data point? Why is this desirable for a generative model?
