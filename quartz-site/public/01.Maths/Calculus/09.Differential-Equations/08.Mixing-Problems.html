08.Mixing Problems

“A tank, a pipe in, a pipe out — and hidden inside this mundane setup is the entire theory of first-order linear ODEs, compartmental models, and the mathematics of how information decays and concentrates in neural networks.”

1. The Physical Setup

A tank contains a solution (fluid + dissolved substance). Two processes happen simultaneously:

  • Inflow: Solution enters at rate rin​ (volume/time) with concentration cin​ (substance/volume).
  • Outflow: Well-mixed solution leaves at rate rout​ (volume/time).

We want to track Q(t) = amount of substance in the tank at time t.

The governing principle is a balance law (conservation):

dtdQ​=Rate in−Rate out

dtdQ​=rin​⋅cin​−rout​⋅V(t)Q(t)​​

where V(t) is the volume of solution in the tank at time t.

The term V(t)Q(t)​ is the concentration in the tank at time t — the “well-mixed” assumption means the outflow carries whatever concentration is currently in the tank.

2. The Volume Function V(t)

V(t)=V0​+(rin​−rout​),t

where V0​=V(0) is the initial volume.

Three cases:

ConditionEffectODE type
rin​=rout​V(t)=V0​ (constant)Linear, constant coefficients
rin​>rout​Tank fillsLinear, variable coefficients
rin​<rout​Tank drainsLinear, variable coefficients (finite time blow-up possible)

3. The ODE — First-Order Linear

Substituting V(t)=V0​+(rin​−rout​)t:

\frac{dQ}{dt} + \underbrace{\frac{r_{out}}{V_0 + (r_{in} - r_{out})t}}_{P(t)} Q = \underbrace{r_{in} \cdot c_{in}}_{f(t)} \tag{1}

This is a first-order linear ODE in standard form Q′+P(t)Q=f(t).

Integrating Factor Method

Multiply both sides by the integrating factor

μ(t)=e∫P(t),dt,

so that the left side becomes an exact derivative:

dtd​[μ(t),Q(t)]=μ(t),f(t).

Integrate both sides:

μ(t),Q(t)=∫μ(t),f(t),dt+C.

Solve for Q(t) and apply the initial condition Q(0)=Q0​.

4. Rigorous Derivation of the Integrating Factor

Claim: If μ(t)=e∫P(t),dt, then μ(t)(Q′+P(t)Q)=dtd​(μQ).

Proof:

dtd​(μQ)=μ′Q+μQ′.

Since μ=e∫P,dt, we have μ′=P(t),μ. Therefore:

dtd​(μQ)=PμQ+μQ′=μ(Q′+PQ).■

So multiplying the ODE by μ converts the left side to a perfect derivative, which we can integrate directly.

5. Examples

Example 1 — Constant Volume (Equal Flow Rates)

Setup: A 500 L tank initially contains 10 kg of salt dissolved in water. Brine containing 0.05 kg/L flows in at 10 L/min. Well-mixed solution drains at 10 L/min.

Parameters:

  • V0​=500 L, rin​=rout​=10 L/min (so V(t)=500)
  • cin​=0.05 kg/L, Q0​=10 kg

ODE:

dtdQ​=10⋅0.05−10⋅500Q​=0.5−50Q​

\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{1}{50}Q = 0.5 \tag{2}

Integrating factor: μ=et/50.

dtd​(et/50Q)=0.5,et/50

Integrate:

et/50Q=0.5⋅50,et/50+C=25,et/50+C

Q(t)=25+Ce−t/50

Apply Q(0)=10: 10=25+C⟹C=−15.

Q(t)=25−15e−t/50 kg​

Interpretation:

  • As t→∞: Q(t)→25 kg. The equilibrium concentration is cin​=0.05 kg/L applied to 500 L, giving 25 kg. ✓
  • The system forgets its initial condition exponentially, with time constant τ=50 min.
  • At t=50 min (one time constant): Q(50)=25−15e−1≈19.48 kg.

Example 2 — Draining Tank (Variable Volume)

Setup: A 200 L tank initially contains 50 kg of salt. Pure water (cin​=0) flows in at 4 L/min. Mixed solution drains at 6 L/min.

Parameters:

  • V(t)=200+(4−6)t=200−2t (tank empties at t=100 min)
  • Q0​=50 kg, cin​=0

ODE:

dtdQ​=0−6⋅200−2tQ​=−200−2t6Q​=−100−t3Q​

\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{3}{100 - t} Q = 0 \tag{3}

This is actually separable (it’s homogeneous linear):

QdQ​=−100−t3,dt​

ln∣Q∣=3ln∣100−t∣+C1​

Q(t)=K(100−t)3

Apply Q(0)=50: 50=K⋅1003⟹K=50/106=5×10−5.

Q(t)=50(100100−t​)3=50(1−100t​)3 kg​

Check: Q(100)=0 — tank is empty and contains no salt. ✓

The concentration in the tank:

c(t)=V(t)Q(t)​=200−2t50(1−t/100)3​=200(1−t/100)50(1−t/100)3​=20050​(1−t/100)2=41​(1−100t​)2

At t=0: c(0)=1/4 kg/L. At t=100: c(100)=0 kg/L. The tank dilutes to zero as it drains.

Example 3 — Two-Tank Cascade (System of ODEs)

Setup: Tank 1 (100 L) and Tank 2 (200 L) are connected. Brine at 0.1 kg/L flows into Tank 1 at 5 L/min. The outflow of Tank 1 (5 L/min) feeds into Tank 2. Tank 2 drains at 5 L/min. Initially both tanks contain pure water.

Let Q1​(t), Q2​(t) = salt in tanks 1 and 2.

System:

dtdQ1​​=5⋅0.1−5⋅100Q1​​=0.5−20Q1​​

dtdQ2​​=5⋅100Q1​​−5⋅200Q2​​=20Q1​​−40Q2​​

Solve Tank 1 first (same structure as Example 1):

Q1​(t)=10(1−e−t/20)

Substitute into Tank 2’s ODE:

dtdQ2​​+40Q2​​=20Q1​​=2010(1−e−t/20)​=21​(1−e−t/20)

Integrating factor μ=et/40:

dtd​(et/40Q2​)=21​et/40(1−e−t/20)=21​et/40−21​e−t/40

Integrate:

et/40Q2​=21​⋅40,et/40−21​⋅(−40),e−t/40+C=20et/40+20e−t/40+C

Q2​(t)=20+20e−t/20+Ce−t/40

Apply Q2​(0)=0: 0=20+20+C⟹C=−40.

Q2​(t)=20−40e−t/40+20e−t/20​

As t→∞: Q2​→20 kg = 0.1 kg/L × 200 L. ✓

Key structure: The two-tank cascade is a compartmental model — the output of one ODE drives the next. This is the architecture of recurrent and feedforward systems in both biology and machine learning.

7. The Equilibrium and Time Constant

For the constant-volume case Q′+Vr​Q=r⋅cin​, the solution always takes the form:

Q(t)=Qeq​+(Q0​−Qeq​),e−t/τ

where:

  • Qeq​=cin​⋅V — the equilibrium amount (what the tank converges to)
  • τ=V/r — the time constant (how fast it gets there)

At t=τ: the gap ∣Q(t)−Qeq​∣ has decayed to e−1≈36.8 of its initial value.

At t=5τ: gap is e−5≈0.67 — effectively at equilibrium.

The time constant τ has a clean physical meaning: it is the time it takes to flush one full tank volume through the system (τ=V/r).

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

The mixing problem is not merely a physics exercise. Its mathematical structure — a first-order linear ODE with a source term and a decay term — is the fundamental equation of memory, forgetting, and information blending in machine learning.

8.1 Exponential Moving Average — The Discrete Mixing Problem

In optimization and statistics, the exponential moving average (EMA) of a quantity xt​ is:

St​=βSt−1​+(1−β)xt​,β∈(0,1).

This is the discrete-time version of the mixing ODE. To see why, write it as:

St​−St−1​=−(1−β)(St−1​−xt​)

ΔS=−(1−β)(S−xt​)

In continuous time with step Δt→0, setting β=1−rΔt/V:

dtdS​=−Vr​(S−cin​)=Vr​cin​−Vr​S

This is exactly the constant-volume mixing ODE. The EMA is the mixing problem in disguise, with:

  • β controlling the time constant (memory length)
  • xt​ playing the role of inflow concentration
  • St​ being the amount in the tank

8.2 Momentum in Gradient Descent (Heavy Ball / Adam)

The momentum update in SGD is:

vt​=βvt−1​+(1−β)∇L(θt​)

θt+1​=θt​−ηvt​

The velocity vt​ obeys a mixing ODE: it is a running average of past gradients, with old gradients “rinsing out” exponentially. The time constant is τ=1/(1−β) steps.

  • β=0.9: memory of τ=10 steps.
  • β=0.99: memory of τ=100 steps.

Adam uses two mixing ODEs simultaneously:

mt​=β1​mt−1​+(1−β1​)∇L (first moment — gradient EMA) vt​=β2​vt−1​+(1−β2​)(∇L)2 (second moment — squared gradient EMA)

Both are mixing problems running in parallel, one tracking the “mean tank” and one tracking the “variance tank.”

8.3 Batch Normalization — Mixing Running Statistics

During inference, batch normalization uses running estimates of mean μ and variance σ2:

μrun​←(1−α),μrun​+α,μbatch​

This is a mixing ODE with time constant τ=1/α. The running mean is the “amount of substance” in a tank being continuously flushed with the batch statistics.

Choosing α too large = the tank is too reactive (noisy estimates). Too small = too sluggish (doesn’t track distribution shift). This is exactly the tradeoff between τ being too short or too long in Example 1.

8.4 Leaky Integrator Neurons — The Neural Mixing Tank

The standard model of a biological neuron’s membrane potential V(t) is:

τm​dtdV​=−V(t)+Rm​I(t)

dtdV​+τm​V​=τm​Rm​​I(t)

This is precisely the mixing ODE with:

  • Q↔V (membrane potential ↔ amount of substance)
  • r/V↔1/τm​ (leak rate)
  • r⋅cin​↔Rm​I(t)/τm​ (input current ↔ inflow)

The neuron integrates its input and leaks — it is a mixing tank. The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron used in spiking neural networks is built directly on this ODE.

In deep learning, Liquid State Machines and Echo State Networks (reservoir computing) generalize this to high-dimensional networks of leaky integrators — tanks coupled into a cascade, exactly like Example 3.

8.5 LSTM Forget Gate — Controlled Mixing

The LSTM cell state update:

Ct​=ft​⊙Ct−1​+it​⊙C~t​

where ft​∈(0,1) is the forget gate and it​ is the input gate.

Compare with the mixing ODE solution:

Q(t+Δt)=e−Δt/τQ(t)+(1−e−Δt/τ)Qeq​

The forget gate ft​ plays the role of e−Δt/τ — the “fraction remaining in the tank.” The input gate it​⊙C~t​ is the new inflow. The LSTM learns to set its own time constant adaptively, solving a parameterized family of mixing problems at each timestep.

The entire architecture of LSTMs is a learnable, input-dependent version of the mixing ODE. Grokking one gives you the geometric intuition for the other.

8.6 Compartmental Models in Epidemiology and Federated Learning

The two-tank cascade (Example 3) generalizes to compartmental models: systems of ODEs where substance flows between compartments. In ML:

  • SIR/SEIR epidemic models (used in federated learning to model data infection/spread across nodes) are compartmental mixing systems.
  • Graph Neural Networks can be understood as compartmental mixing problems on graphs, where “substance” (feature information) diffuses between nodes according to the graph Laplacian — a high-dimensional mixing ODE.

10. Common Pitfalls

  1. Using Q/V0​ instead of Q/V(t) in variable-volume problems. The concentration uses the current volume — a common and fatal error.
  2. Forgetting the units. Rate in is (L/min) × (kg/L) = kg/min. Rate out is (L/min) × (kg/L) = kg/min. dQ/dt must have units kg/min.
  3. Not checking the domain. If rout​>rin​, V(t) reaches zero in finite time t∗=V0​/(rout​−rin​). The solution is only valid on [0,t∗).
  4. Confusing Q(t) with c(t). Q is amount (kg); c=Q/V is concentration (kg/L). The problem statement specifies which one is given and which is sought.
  5. Wrong equilibrium. The equilibrium is Qeq​=cin​⋅V (concentration × volume), not cin​ alone.
