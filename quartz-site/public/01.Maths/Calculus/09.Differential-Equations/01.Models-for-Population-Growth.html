0. Why This Matters Before We Begin

Population models are the pedagogical spine of first-order ODEs. They are also, quietly, the conceptual skeleton of a surprising slice of modern machine learning — activation functions, learning-rate schedules, neural population codes, epidemic-spread on graphs. The mathematics here is not a warm-up act; it is the act.

1. The Exponential (Malthusian) Model

1.1 Derivation from First Principles

Let P(t) denote population at time t. The Malthusian hypothesis is that the instantaneous rate of change is proportional to current population:

dtdP​=kP,P(0)=P0​

where k∈R is the intrinsic growth rate (births minus deaths per individual per unit time).

Solving by separation of variables:

PdP​=k,dt⟹∫P0​P​udu​=∫0t​k,ds⟹ln!(P0​P​)=kt

P(t)=P0​,ekt​

  • k>0: unbounded exponential growth.
  • k<0: exponential decay (radioactive decay, drug clearance — same equation).
  • k=0: P(t)=P0​, a fixed point (trivially stable).

1.2 The Fixed Point and Stability

The ODE P˙=kP has a single equilibrium: P∗=0.

The linearisation around P∗ is trivial — the equation is linear. Stability:

  • k<0: P∗=0 is stable (all trajectories decay to it).
  • k>0: P∗=0 is unstable (trajectories flee from it).

1.3 Phase Line

k > 0:          k < 0:

P                P
↑    →  →  →    ↑   ←  ←  ←
|                |
0                0

All trajectories on the real line either all flow away from (or toward) the origin. No complexity here — that comes next.

1.4 Concrete Example

World population (crude estimate): In 1800, P0​≈109. Suppose k=0.014 yr−1 (roughly 1.4% annual growth). Predict population in 1900.

P(100)=109⋅e0.014×100=109⋅e1.4≈109×4.055≈4.05×109

Historical reality: ~1.6 billion in 1900. The model overshoots drastically — because k isn’t constant, and resources are finite. This failure motivates §2.

2. The Logistic Model

2.1 The Key Insight: Carrying Capacity

Verhulst (1838) proposed that k is not constant but decreases linearly with population — as P grows, competition for resources reduces the effective per-capita growth rate:

effective rate=r(1−KP​)

where:

  • r>0: intrinsic (maximum) growth rate,
  • K>0: carrying capacity (environmental ceiling).

This yields the logistic equation:

dtdP​=rP(1−KP​)​

2.2 Qualitative Analysis Before Solving

Fixed points: Set P˙=0: P∗=0(trivial, unstable)andP∗=K(non-trivial)

Phase line analysis:

RegionSign of P˙Direction
P<0negative (biologically meaningless)—
0<P<KpositiveP increases
P=Kzeroequilibrium
P>KnegativeP decreases

So K is a stable equilibrium (globally attracting for P>0). P∗=0 is unstable.

Phase line:

←  (0)  →  →  →  →  [K]  ←  ←  ←

2.3 Exact Solution

Separate variables:

P(1−P/K)dP​=r,dt

Use partial fractions on the left:

P(1−P/K)1​=P1​+1−P/K1/K​

Integrate both sides:

lnP−ln!(1−KP​)=rt+C

ln!(K−PP​)=rt+C

Apply initial condition P(0)=P0​, so C=ln!(K−P0​P0​​). Exponentiate and solve for P:

P(t)=P0​+(K−P0​),e−rtKP0​​​

This is the logistic function — an S-shaped (sigmoidal) curve.

2.4 Asymptotic Behavior

limt→∞​P(t)=K(regardless of P0​∈(0,K])

limt→−∞​P(t)=0

The inflection point (where growth is fastest, P¨=0) occurs at P=K/2.

Proof of inflection: Differentiate P˙=rP(1−P/K) with respect to t:

P¨=rP˙(1−K2P​)=r2P!(1−KP​)!(1−K2P​)

Setting P¨=0 (with P>0, P=K) gives P=K/2. ■

2.6 Concrete Example

U.S. population: Stewart uses P0​=3.9×106 (1790), K≈197×106, and fits r to historical data.
At t=0 (1790): rapid growth. By 1900, growth slows — logistic fits better than exponential.

3. The Harvesting Extension (Brief)

A natural perturbation: suppose we remove h individuals per unit time (fishing, hunting):

dtdP​=rP!(1−KP​)−h

The equilibria satisfy a quadratic. For h<rK/4 (below the maximum sustainable yield), two equilibria exist; above it, population collapses. MSY is achieved at P=K/2 — the same inflection point, not a coincidence.

hmax​=4rK​

5. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is the part most textbooks skip. Don’t.

5.1 The Logistic Function Is the Sigmoid

Rewrite the logistic solution. Let P0​=K/2 (starting at inflection), u=rt:

P(t)=1+e−rtK​=K⋅σ(rt)

where σ(x)=1+e−x1​ is the sigmoid (logistic) activation function in neural networks.

This is not a notational coincidence. The sigmoid activation was originally motivated by biological neuron firing rates — neurons saturate at maximum firing (analogous to K) and have a threshold below which they barely fire. The logistic ODE describes exactly this saturation dynamic.

In practice: every time a neural network applies σ(w⊤x+b), it is evaluating the logistic function — the solution to a population ODE.

5.2 Logistic Regression

Binary classification via logistic regression models the probability that output y=1:

P(y=1∣x)=σ(w⊤x+b)=1+e−(w⊤x+b)1​

The cross-entropy loss is the negative log-likelihood of this model. Training via gradient descent is literally fitting the parameters of a logistic (population-growth) curve to data.

5.3 Neural ODE and Continuous-Depth Networks

In the 2018 Neural ODE framework (Chen et al.), the hidden state h(t) of a network satisfies:

dtdh​=fθ​(h(t),t)

where fθ​ is a neural network. This is a learned ODE. The logistic equation is the simplest non-trivial case of such a system with a single hidden unit.

Euler discretization of h˙=fθ​(h) with step Δt=1 gives exactly a ResNet layer:

ht+1​=ht​+fθ​(ht​)

Understanding population ODEs qualitatively — fixed points, stability, phase lines — is the conceptual prerequisite for reasoning about the dynamics of deep networks.

5.4 Epidemic/Information Diffusion (SIR Model)

The spread of information, memes, or viruses in a network follows the SIR model:

dtdS​=−βSI,dtdI​=βSI−γI,dtdR​=γI

This is a direct generalization of population ODEs to a 3-compartment system. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) trained on epidemiological or social data are learning to approximate the solutions to ODE systems like this one.

5.5 Learning Rate Schedules

Many modern optimizers use exponential decay of the learning rate:

η(t)=η0​,e−λt

This is the solution to η˙​=−λη — a population decay ODE applied to an optimization hyperparameter. “Cosine annealing” is a smooth perturbation of this.

6. Proofs and Technicalities Worth Knowing

6.1 Existence and Uniqueness (Picard–Lindelöf)

The right-hand side f(t,P)=rP(1−P/K) is a polynomial in P, hence continuously differentiable (Lipschitz in P on any compact set). By the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, the IVP has a unique solution on some interval around t0​.

For the logistic equation, the explicit formula shows the solution exists for all t∈R.

6.2 Global Stability of K (Lyapunov Argument)

Define the Lyapunov function V(P)=(P−K)2 for P>0.

V˙=2(P−K)P˙=2(P−K)⋅rP(1−KP​)

Note 1−P/K=−(P−K)/K, so:

V˙=2(P−K)⋅rP⋅(−KP−K​)=−K2rP​(P−K)2≤0

with equality only when P=K or P=0. Since P>0, V˙<0 whenever P=K. So K is Lyapunov stable and the trajectory converges to it. ■

8. Exercises (Self-Check)

  1. Doubling time. Show that for exponential growth, the doubling time is T2​=kln2​, independent of P0​.

  2. Logistic — large K. Show that if P0​≪K, the logistic solution approximates exponential growth for small t.
    (Hint: expand e−rt or take K→∞.)

  3. Harvesting. For P˙=rP(1−P/K)−h with h<rK/4, find both equilibria and determine their stability via linearisation.

  4. Sigmoid. Starting from dxdσ​=σ(1−σ), derive this directly from the logistic ODE. What is the biological interpretation?

  5. Neural ODE. Euler-discretize h˙=tanh(Wh) with step 1. Compare to a one-layer RNN update rule.
