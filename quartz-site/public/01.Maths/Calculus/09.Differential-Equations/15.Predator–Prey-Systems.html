“Nature does not solve differential equations — she simply satisfies them. Our job is to listen carefully enough to write them down.” — Personal margin note, Stewart §9.6

See also: Linear Equations · Application to Electric Circuits

0. Preliminary Remarks

The predator–prey system is one of the great examples of applied mathematics: simple equations, profound behavior, no closed-form solution — yet completely tractable by qualitative analysis. It sits at the intersection of differential equations, dynamical systems, linear algebra (linearization), and nonlinear analysis.

This note builds the theory from scratch: model derivation → equilibria → linearization → phase plane → full qualitative picture → extensions → connections to ML.

The mathematics here lives in ODE theory and dynamical systems, but the tools are exactly those of Linear Equations — applied locally near equilibria via the Jacobian.

1. Model Derivation — From Biology to Equations

1.1 The Two Species

Let:

  • x(t)≥0 = population of prey (e.g., rabbits) at time t
  • y(t)≥0 = population of predators (e.g., foxes) at time t

1.2 Modelling Assumptions

AssumptionMathematical consequence
Prey grow exponentially without predatorsx˙=ax for some a>0
Each predator-prey encounter reduces prey at rate proportional to both populations−bxy term in x˙
Predators die exponentially without prey−cy term in y˙​
Each encounter increases predator population+dxy term in y˙​

The term xy is the law of mass action: encounters occur at rate proportional to the product of both populations (like molecules colliding in a gas).

1.3 The Lotka–Volterra Equations

Definition 1.1 (Lotka–Volterra System). The predator–prey system with parameters a,b,c,d>0 is: x˙=ax−bxy,y˙​=−cy+dxy.​ Equivalently, with u=(x,y)T: u˙=F(u)=(ax−bxy −cy+dxy​).

Historical note. Vito Volterra derived these equations in 1926 to explain oscillations in Adriatic fish populations following World War I. Alfred Lotka independently derived them in 1910 in a chemical context. The mathematics predates the biology.

Factored form (useful for finding equilibria): x˙=x(a−by),y˙​=y(−c+dx).

2. Equilibria

Definition 2.1. A point \mathbf{u}^* = (x^_, y^_) is an equilibrium (fixed point) of the system if F(u∗)=0, i.e., the population does not change.

Setting x˙=0 and y˙​=0:

x(a−by)=0andy(−c+dx)=0.

Case 1: x=0 and y=0. Equilibrium at the origin E0​=(0,0).

Case 2: x=0 and y=0. Then a−by=0⇒y∗=a/b, and −c+dx=0⇒x∗=c/d.

Equilibrium at the coexistence point: E∗=(dc​, ba​).​

Remark. There are exactly two equilibria in the first quadrant (the biologically relevant region x≥0,y≥0). The entire qualitative theory will determine what happens near each.

3. Linearization — The Jacobian

The key tool for understanding behavior near equilibria is linearization: approximate the nonlinear system by its best linear approximation at the equilibrium point. This is exactly the multivariable Taylor theorem.

Definition 3.1 (Jacobian). The Jacobian matrix of F=(F1​,F2​) at a point (x0​,y0​) is: J(x0​,y0​)=(∂F1​/∂x​∂F1​/∂y ∂F2​/∂x​∂F2​/∂y​).

For the Lotka–Volterra system: J(x,y)=(a−by​−bx dy​−c+dx​).

Theorem 3.2 (Hartman–Grobman). Near a hyperbolic equilibrium \mathbf{u}^_ (one where J(\mathbf{u}^_) has no eigenvalues with zero real part), the nonlinear system is topologically conjugate to its linearization ξ˙​=J(u∗)ξ.

In plain language: the eigenvalues of J at an equilibrium determine the local qualitative behavior of the full nonlinear system.

3.1 Linearization at E0​=(0,0)

J(0,0)=(a​0 0​−c​).

Eigenvalues: λ1​=a>0, λ2​=−c<0.

Since eigenvalues have opposite signs, E0​ is a saddle point — unstable. Solutions starting near the origin move away (except along the y-axis, which is the stable manifold).

Biological meaning: If both populations are near zero, prey grow (positive eigenvalue in x-direction) and predators die (negative eigenvalue in y-direction). The extinction state is unstable: any small prey population recovers.

3.2 Linearization at E∗=(c/d,a/b)

J!(dc​,ba​)=(a−b⋅ba​​−b⋅dc​ d⋅ba​​−c+d⋅dc​​)=(0​−dbc​ bad​​0​).

Characteristic equation: det(J−λI)=λ2+dbc​⋅bad​=λ2+ac=0.

Eigenvalues: λ=±iac​.

Pure imaginary eigenvalues! The linearization gives a center.

Warning. Here Hartman–Grobman does NOT apply — E∗ is not hyperbolic (eigenvalues have zero real part). The linearization says “center,” but the nonlinear system could be a center, a stable spiral, or an unstable spiral. We need a different tool: a conserved quantity.

4. The Conserved Quantity — Proof of Periodic Orbits

Theorem 4.1. The function H(x,y)=d,x−clnx+b,y−alny is a first integral of the Lotka–Volterra system: dtd​H(x(t),y(t))=0 along every solution with x,y>0.

Proof. Compute H˙ along solutions: dtdH​=∂x∂H​x˙+∂y∂H​y˙​=(d−xc​)(ax−bxy)+(b−ya​)(−cy+dxy).

Expand each term: (d−xc​)(ax−bxy)=adx−bdxy−ac+bcy, (b−ya​)(−cy+dxy)=−bcy+bdxy+ac−adx.

Sum: H˙=(adx−bdxy−ac+bcy)+(−bcy+bdxy+ac−adx)=0.■

Corollary 4.2. Every solution with x(0),y(0)>0 is a closed orbit (periodic solution). The period and amplitude depend on the initial condition.

Proof. H is constant along each trajectory. The level sets H(x,y)=k are closed curves in the first quadrant (one verifies this by analyzing H‘s critical points — its unique minimum in the first quadrant is at E∗). Since trajectories follow level sets, they are closed. ■

5. Phase Plane Analysis

5.1 Nullclines

Definition 5.1. The x-nullcline is (x,y):x˙=0. The y-nullcline is (x,y):y˙​=0.

For Lotka–Volterra:

  • x-nullclines: x=0 (the y-axis) and y=a/b (horizontal line)
  • y-nullclines: y=0 (the x-axis) and x=c/d (vertical line)

The equilibria are exactly the intersections of x- and y-nullclines.

5.2 Direction Field — Four Quadrant Analysis

The nullclines divide the first quadrant into four regions. In each, determine signs of x˙ and y˙​:

         y
         │
    a/b ─┼────────────────────────────────
         │  II           │   I
         │  ẋ < 0        │   ẋ > 0
         │  ẏ > 0        │   ẏ > 0
         │  (prey ↓,     │   (prey ↑,
         │  pred ↑)      │   pred ↑)
         │               │
         │               E* = (c/d, a/b)
         │               │
         │  III          │   IV
         │  ẋ < 0        │   ẋ > 0
         │  ẏ < 0        │   ẏ < 0

         │  pred ↓)      │   pred ↓)
         │               │
    ─────┼───────────────┼──────────────── x
         0              c/d

Reading the orbit direction counterclockwise: I → II → III → IV → I.

Mnemonic. When prey are plentiful and predators few (Quadrant IV), predators thrive and prey decline → Quadrant I. When predators are many and prey scarce (Quadrant II), predators starve → Quadrant III. The system perpetually chases its tail.

5.3 Time Series — Oscillations with Phase Lag

The populations oscillate periodically. The predator population lags the prey population by approximately a quarter period:

Population
    │
    │    ╭─── Prey x(t)
    │   ╱ ╲              ╭──
    │  ╱   ╲            ╱
    │ ╱     ╲    ╭─── Predator y(t)
    │╱       ╲  ╱  ╲

    │               ╲   ╭──
    │                ╲ ╱

         T/4   T/2  3T/4   T

Phase lag: y peaks after x peaks.

Why the lag? Prey grow → more food for predators → predators grow → prey decline → predators decline → prey recover. Each step takes time. The quarter-period lag is a signature of the ±iac​ eigenvalues (phase angle = π/2).

6. The Conservation Law — Deeper Analysis

6.1 Shape of Level Sets of H

At the coexistence equilibrium E∗=(c/d,a/b), compute H: H∗=H(E∗)=d⋅dc​−clndc​+b⋅ba​−alnba​=c+a−clndc​−alnba​.

For any other initial condition (x0​,y0​), H(x_0, y_0) > H^_ (since E^_ is the global minimum of H in the first quadrant — proven below), and the orbit is a closed curve enclosing E∗.

Lemma 6.1. E∗ is the unique global minimum of H in R>02​.

Proof. ∂H/∂x=d−c/x=0⇒x=c/d, ∂H/∂y=b−a/y=0⇒y=a/b. So E^_ is the unique critical point. The Hessian: \nabla^2 H = \begin{pmatrix} c/x^2 & 0 \ 0 & a/y^2 \end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{E^_} = \begin{pmatrix} d^2/c & 0 \ 0 & b^2/a \end{pmatrix}, which is positive definite (diagonal with positive entries). So E∗ is a strict local minimum. Since H→+∞ as x→0+, x→+∞, y→0+, or y→+∞, it is the global minimum. ■

6.2 Average Populations Over One Period

Theorem 6.2. The time-average of x(t) over one period T is xˉ=c/d. The time-average of y(t) over one period is yˉ​=a/b.

Proof. Integrate x˙/x=a−by over one period: ∫0T​xx˙​,dt=∫0T​(a−by),dt=aT−b∫0T​y,dt.

The left side is [lnx]0T​=0 (periodic). So b∫0T​y,dt=aT, giving yˉ​=a/b. Similarly for xˉ=c/d. ■

Corollary (Volterra’s Principle). The time-averaged populations equal the equilibrium values regardless of amplitude. Uniform harvesting of prey shifts the equilibrium and thus the average predator population — a counterintuitive consequence.

7. Stability Analysis — Summary

EquilibriumJacobian eigenvaluesTypeStability
E0​=(0,0)λ=a>0, λ=−c<0SaddleUnstable
E∗=(c/d,a/b)λ=±iac​Center (nonlinear)Neutrally stable (Lyapunov stable, not asymptotically stable)

Definition 7.1. An equilibrium is Lyapunov stable if solutions starting close remain close for all time, but do not necessarily converge to it. It is asymptotically stable if solutions also converge to it as t→∞.

E∗ is Lyapunov stable (orbits stay on their level curve of H) but not asymptotically stable (no convergence — the orbit is periodic).

8. Extensions and Modifications

8.1 Logistic Prey (More Realistic)

Replace exponential prey growth with logistic growth: x˙=ax(1−Kx​)−bxy,y˙​=−cy+dxy.

The carrying capacity K limits prey growth. The coexistence equilibrium becomes: x∗=dc​,y∗=ba​(1−dKc​).

(Exists only if c/d<K, i.e., predators can survive before prey hit carrying capacity.)

The Jacobian at E∗ now has eigenvalues with negative real part — the equilibrium becomes asymptotically stable. Orbits spiral inward: populations don’t oscillate forever, they settle to coexistence. This is the Poincaré–Bendixson picture.

8.2 Holling Type II Functional Response

Real predators don’t hunt indefinitely faster as prey increase — they saturate (handling time). Replace bxy with: 1+bh,xbxy​, where h is handling time per prey. This is Holling Type II and introduces a new equilibrium branch and can generate limit cycles (Hopf bifurcation).

8.3 The General Form

x˙=x,f(x,y),y˙​=y,g(x,y).

Both f and g are general functions. Lotka–Volterra is the case f=a−by, g=−c+dx — both linear in (x,y). The entire rich theory of predator–prey models generalizes from this observation.

9. Numerical Example — Concrete Computation

Parameters: a=1, b=0.1, c=1.5, d=0.075.

Equilibrium: E∗=(0.0751.5​, 0.11​)=(20, 10).

Jacobian at E∗: J(20,10)=(0​−0.1⋅20 0.075⋅10​0​)=(0​−2 0.75​0​).

Eigenvalues: λ2+2⋅0.75=0⟹λ2=−1.5⟹λ=±i1.5​≈±1.225i.

Period of linearized oscillation: T≈ac​2π​=1⋅1.5​2π​≈5.13 time units.

Initial condition: (x0​,y0​)=(25,10). Prey start above equilibrium, predators at equilibrium.

Conservation value: H(25,10)=0.075⋅25−1.5ln25+0.1⋅10−1ln10≈1.875−4.832+1−2.303=−4.26.

Compare with H(E∗)=0.075⋅20−1.5ln20+0.1⋅10−1ln10≈1.5−4.497+1−2.303=−4.30.

Since H(x0​,y0​)>H(E∗) (less negative), the orbit is outside the equilibrium — a closed curve slightly larger.

11. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

11.1 Lotka–Volterra as a Dynamical System — Neural ODE

The Lotka–Volterra equations are an autonomous ODE: u˙=F(u). This is exactly the structure of a Neural ODE (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2018):

Definition. A Neural ODE parameterizes the vector field F by a neural network fθ​: h˙(t)=fθ​(h(t),t),h(t1​)=ODESolve(fθ​,h(t0​),t0​,t1​).

One trains θ to fit observed trajectories. If the true dynamics are Lotka–Volterra, a Neural ODE should discover the vector field F(x,y)=(x(a−by), y(−c+dx)).

The conservation law H is a first integral — a Neural ODE with a Hamiltonian structure (Hamiltonian Neural Network, Greydanus et al., 2019) can be constrained to discover and preserve H exactly.

11.2 Predator–Prey Dynamics in GANs

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) pit a generator G against a discriminator D in a minimax game: minG​maxD​ E[logD(x)]+E[log(1−D(G(z)))].

The training dynamics of GANs are notoriously oscillatory and unstable — reminiscent of predator–prey orbits around the Nash equilibrium (G^_, D^_). This is not a coincidence:

Theorem (informal). Near a Nash equilibrium, simultaneous gradient descent on a two-player zero-sum game has eigenvalues ±iω — exactly the pure imaginary eigenvalues of the Lotka–Volterra linearization. Training oscillates; it does not converge.

This is why GAN training is hard and why techniques like gradient penalty, spectral normalization, and unrolled optimization are needed — they add damping (negative real part to eigenvalues) converting the center to a stable spiral.

11.3 Competitive Dynamics in Resource Allocation (RL)

In multi-agent reinforcement learning, agents compete for resources. Their populations evolve under replicator dynamics: x˙i​=xi​(fi​(x)−fˉ​(x)),

where fi​ is agent i‘s fitness and fˉ​ is the population average. This is a generalization of Lotka–Volterra to n competing strategies. The game-theoretic equilibria correspond to Nash equilibria; their stability is analyzed via the Jacobian — exactly as in §3.

11.4 Ecological Modeling with ML

Modern approaches use ML to learn predator–prey dynamics from data:

  1. SINDy (Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics): Given time series (x(t),y(t)), compute x˙ and y˙​ numerically, then regress u˙ against a library of candidate terms x,y,xy,x2,… using sparse linear regression (L1 regularization). If the true model is Lotka–Volterra, SINDy recovers x˙=ax−bxy exactly.

  2. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs): Train a neural network x^(t),y^​(t) to satisfy the Lotka–Volterra ODEs as a penalty in the loss: L=data fit∣x^(ti​)−xi​∣2​​+λODE residual∣x^˙−x^(a−by^​)∣2​​.

  3. Conservation-aware learning: Enforce H(x(t),y(t))=const as a hard constraint. This dramatically reduces the hypothesis space and improves generalization.

13. Things to Prove Yourself

  1. Verify by direct computation that H˙=0 along solutions of the Lotka–Volterra system (fill in all algebra from Theorem 4.1).
  2. Prove Volterra’s Principle (Theorem 6.2) for xˉ=c/d by integrating y˙​/y over one period.
  3. For the logistic-prey system (x˙=ax(1−x/K)−bxy, y˙​=−cy+dxy): find the coexistence equilibrium and compute its Jacobian. Show the eigenvalues have negative real part when c/d<K, confirming asymptotic stability.
  4. Show that the linearization \dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}} = J(E^_)\boldsymbol{\xi} with J(E^_) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -bc/d \ ad/b & 0\end{pmatrix} gives solutions of the form ξ(t)=eiac​,tv and write out v explicitly.
  5. Prove that the function g(s)=s−1−lns satisfies g(s)≥0 for all s>0 with equality iff s=1. Use this to show H(x,y) \geq H(E^_) for all x,y>0. (This gives a clean proof that E^_ is the global minimum.)
  6. SINDy exercise. If you were given noisy data (x(ti​),y(ti​)) and had to recover the LV equations using sparse regression, what library of basis functions would you include? What is the minimum number of data points needed in principle?
