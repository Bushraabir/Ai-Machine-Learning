“Every circuit is a linear system in disguise. Kirchhoff didn’t invent new mathematics — he read the physics and wrote down the equations that were already there.” — Personal margin note, Stewart §7.3 / MIT 18.02

See also: Linear Equations (foundations, trichotomy theorem, matrix form)

0. Preliminary Remarks

Electric circuits are one of the most natural and historically important applications of linear systems. The key insight — due to Kirchhoff in 1845 — is that the physical laws governing current and voltage are linear constraints. Every node and every loop contributes one linear equation. The circuit becomes a matrix. Solving it is Gaussian elimination.

This note builds from physical laws → linear equations → full matrix system → worked examples → connection to ML (circuit simulation in neural networks and analog computing).

1. The Physical Laws — Stated Precisely

1.1 Ohm’s Law

Law 1.1 (Ohm). For a resistor with resistance R>0 (in ohms, Ω), if current I (amperes) flows through it, the voltage drop across it is: V=IR.

This is the fundamental linear relation between voltage and current. It is a single linear equation in two unknowns — one constraint.

Remark. Ohm’s Law is an empirical approximation valid for resistors at constant temperature. It fails for diodes, transistors, and nonlinear elements — which is precisely where circuit analysis becomes nonlinear and requires numerical methods.

1.2 Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL)

Law 1.2 (KCL). At every node (junction) of a circuit, the algebraic sum of currents is zero: ∑k​Ik​=0, where currents entering the node are positive and currents leaving are negative (or vice versa — pick a convention and be consistent).

Physical meaning: Charge is conserved. No charge accumulates at a node.

Mathematical character: KCL gives one linear equation per node in the unknown currents.

1.3 Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)

Law 1.3 (KVL). Around every closed loop in a circuit, the algebraic sum of voltage drops is zero: ∑k​Vk​=0. Voltage rises (across sources) are counted positive; voltage drops (across resistors) are counted negative.

Physical meaning: The electric potential is a well-defined function on the circuit graph. Its line integral around any closed path vanishes — this is the statement that the electric field is conservative (∇×E=0 in the static case).

Mathematical character: KVL gives one linear equation per independent loop.

Key observation. Substituting Ohm’s Law (Vk​=Ik​Rk​) into KVL converts voltage equations into current equations. Every equation is linear in the unknown currents. The entire circuit is a linear system.

3. Simple Series Circuit — One Loop

Circuit Description

A voltage source E in series with two resistors R1​ and R2​. One loop, one unknown current I.

     ┌────R₁────┬────R₂────┐
     │          │          │
    [ε]         │          │
     │          │          │
     └──────────┴──────────┘
          (ground)

Applying KVL

Traverse the loop clockwise. Voltage rises across the source, drops across resistors: E−IR1​−IR2​=0.

Rearranging: I(R1​+R2​)=E.

This is a linear equation in one unknown I.

Solution: I=R1​+R2​E​.

Existence and uniqueness follow from Definition 1.1 of Linear Equations, since R1​+R2​>0.

Concrete example. E=12,V, R1​=2,Ω, R2​=4,Ω: I=2+412​=2,A,VR1​​=2⋅2=4,V,VR2​​=2⋅4=8,V.

Check KVL: 12−4−8=0. ✓

4. Two-Loop Circuit — A 2×2 System

Circuit Description

        R₁          R₂
   ┌────/\/\/────┬────/\/\/────┐
   │             │             │
  [ε₁]          R₃           [ε₂]
   │             │             │
   └─────────────┴─────────────┘
         (ground node)

Two meshes. Assign mesh currents I1​ (left loop, clockwise) and I2​ (right loop, clockwise).

Applying KVL to Each Mesh

Mesh 1 (left loop):

Voltage rise across E1​, drop across R1​ (carries I1​), drop across R3​ (carries I1​−I2​, since I2​ flows opposite through R3​): E1​−I1​R1​−(I1​−I2​)R3​=0 \mathcal{E}_1 = I_1(R_1 + R_3) - I_2 R_3. \tag{1}

Mesh 2 (right loop):

Voltage rise across E2​, drop across R2​ (carries I2​), drop across R3​ (carries I2​−I1​): E2​−I2​R2​−(I2​−I1​)R3​=0 \mathcal{E}_2 = -I_1 R_3 + I_2(R_2 + R_3). \tag{2}

Matrix Form

A(R1​+R3​​−R3​ −R3​​R2​+R3​​)​​(I1​ I2​​)=(E1​ E2​​)

Observation. The matrix A is symmetric and has a special structure: diagonal entries are sums of resistances in each mesh; off-diagonal entries are the negative of shared resistances. This is the mesh resistance matrix — it arises from the topology of the circuit, not from the specific values.

Solvability

det(A)=(R1​+R3​)(R2​+R3​)−R32​=R1​R2​+R1​R3​+R2​R3​>0 (since all Ri​>0).

Therefore A is invertible and the solution is unique: (I1​ I2​​)=A−1(E1​ E2​​).

Theorem 4.1. For any circuit of n independent meshes with positive resistances, the mesh resistance matrix A is symmetric positive definite (SPD), hence always invertible. The linear system AI=E always has a unique solution.

Proof sketch. A is symmetric by construction (shared resistances appear symmetrically). For any nonzero x: xTAx=∑branches​Rb​(∑meshes∋b​±xm​)2>0, since each term is a squared current times a positive resistance, and at least one term is nonzero. Hence A is SPD ⇒ det(A)>0 ⇒ unique solution. ■

Concrete Numerical Example

R1​=1,Ω, R2​=2,Ω, R3​=3,Ω, E1​=10,V, E2​=5,V:

A=(4​−3 −3​5​),b=(10 5​).

det(A)=20−9=11.

By Cramer’s rule: I1​=11det(10​−3\5​5​)​=1150+15​=1165​≈5.91,A. I2​=11det(4​10\-3​5​)​=1120+30​=1150​≈4.55,A.

Current through R3​: I1​−I2​=15/11≈1.36,A.

5. General Circuit — The Node Voltage Method

For a circuit with n+1 nodes (label one as ground, V0​=0), the n remaining node voltages V1​,…,Vn​ are the unknowns.

KCL at Node i

By KCL, the sum of currents leaving node i equals zero. The current from node i to node j through resistance Rij​ is: Iij​=Rij​Vi​−Vj​​.

Summing over all neighbors j of node i: ∑j∼i​Rij​Vi​−Vj​​=Iext,i​,

where Iext,i​ is external current injected at node i by current sources.

Rearranging: (∑j∼i​Rij​1​)Vi​−∑j∼i​Rij​1​Vj​=Iext,i​.

The Conductance Matrix (Laplacian)

Define conductance Gij​=1/Rij​. The system in matrix form is:

n×nL​​V=Iext​,

where L is the graph Laplacian of the circuit: Lii​=∑j∼i​Gij​,Lij​=−Gij​ (i=j).

Theorem 5.1 (Laplacian Structure). The conductance matrix L satisfies:

  1. L is symmetric: Lij​=Lji​.
  2. All diagonal entries are positive.
  3. All off-diagonal entries are ≤0.
  4. Each row sums to zero: ∑j​Lij​=0.
  5. L is positive semidefinite. After fixing ground (V0​=0), the reduced matrix L~ is SPD, hence invertible.

Proof of row-sum zero: ∑j​Lij​=Lii​+∑j=i​Lij​=∑j∼i​Gij​−∑j∼i​Gij​=0. ■

This means L itself has a null space spanned by (1,1,…,1)T (constant potentials correspond to zero current — you can shift all potentials by the same constant). Grounding one node removes this degeneracy and makes the system uniquely solvable.

7. Superposition — The Linearity Principle

Theorem 7.1 (Superposition). In a linear circuit, the response (current or voltage anywhere) due to multiple independent sources equals the sum of responses due to each source acting alone (all others replaced by their internal resistances: voltage sources → short circuit, current sources → open circuit).

Proof. The node voltage system is LV=Iext​. If Iext​=I(1)+I(2) (two sources), then: LV=I(1)+I(2)⟹V=L−1I(1)+L−1I(2)=V(1)+V(2).

This is exactly linearity of the map Iext​↦V=L−1Iext​. ■

Remark. Superposition fails for power (since P=V2/R is quadratic, not linear). This is a critical practical note — never superpose power directly.

8. Thévenin’s Theorem — Existence of a Canonical Form

Theorem 8.1 (Thévenin). Any linear circuit with two output terminals can be replaced by a single voltage source Vth​ in series with a single resistance Rth​.

Proof sketch. The terminal voltage V and current I satisfy a relation of the form V=Vth​−IRth​ (a linear equation!) — this follows from the fact that the open-circuit voltage is Vth​=V∣I=0​ and the short-circuit current is Isc​=Vth​/Rth​. The linear relationship between V and I is guaranteed by the linearity of the underlying system. ■

How to find Vth​ and Rth​:

  1. Vth​ = open-circuit voltage at terminals (solve the linear system with I=0).
  2. Rth​ = resistance seen from terminals with all independent sources killed.

Example. Circuit: E=10,V, R1​=6,Ω in series, R2​=3,Ω in parallel with terminals.

Vth​=E⋅R1​+R2​R2​​=10⋅93​=310​,V. Rth​=R1​∥R2​=6+36⋅3​=2,Ω.

This two-parameter description encodes the entire linear circuit for any load.

10. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

10.1 Circuit Simulation as Linear Algebra

SPICE (the industry-standard circuit simulator) solves the node voltage system LV=I at its core. The matrix L is sparse (most entries zero — each node connects to few others). Efficient sparse linear solvers (LU factorization with fill-reducing orderings) are the computational heart of every circuit simulator.

The same sparse linear algebra appears in:

  • Graph neural networks (GNNs): message passing on a graph is exactly the Laplacian operator L applied to node features.
  • Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs): enforce circuit equations as loss terms.

10.2 Resistor Networks as Neural Networks

A resistor network is mathematically equivalent to a linear neural network:

  • Node voltages ↔ neuron activations
  • Conductances Gij​ ↔ weights wij​
  • KCL ↔ the linear combination at each neuron: Vi​=∑j​Gij​∑j​Gij​Vj​​

This is not a metaphor. Hopfield (1984) showed that analog circuits with resistors and capacitors perform associative memory — a precursor to modern attention mechanisms.

10.3 Equilibrium Propagation (Modern ML)

The Equilibrium Propagation algorithm (Scellier & Bengio, 2017) trains neural networks by nudging the circuit toward a target state and measuring how the equilibrium LV=I changes. The gradient of the loss is encoded in the difference between two linear system solutions. This is a biologically plausible alternative to backpropagation.

10.4 Power Systems and Grid ML

Power grids are large-scale circuit systems. The DC power flow equations are a linear approximation: Bθ=P,

where B is the susceptance matrix (a Laplacian), θ are bus voltage angles, and P are power injections. Machine learning models for grid optimization (e.g., optimal power flow) are trained on solutions to this linear system.

12. Things to Prove Yourself

  1. Prove that the mesh resistance matrix A for any planar resistive circuit with n meshes is symmetric positive definite. (Hint: express xTAx as a sum over branches.)
  2. Derive the π-to-Y (delta-to-star) transformation for three resistors. Show it reduces to solving a 3×3 linear system.
  3. Prove Superposition rigorously from the linearity of L−1.
  4. Show that if all resistances in a circuit are scaled by a constant λ>0, then all currents scale by 1/λ and all voltages remain unchanged (when driven by voltage sources). What does this say about the condition number of L?
  5. Formulate a 4-node circuit of your choice as a node-voltage system, solve it by Gaussian elimination, and verify each KCL equation in the solution.
  6. Relate the graph Laplacian L of a resistor network to the adjacency matrix A of the underlying graph. When is L=D−A exactly (where D is the degree matrix)?
