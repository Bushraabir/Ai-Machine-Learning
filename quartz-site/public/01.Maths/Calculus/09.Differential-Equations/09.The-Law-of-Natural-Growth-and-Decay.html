09.The Law of Natural Growth and Decay

“The simplest ODE in existence. One equation. And yet it is the heartbeat of compound interest, radioactive decay, population dynamics, and the gradient flow of every neural network ever trained.”

1. The Governing Principle

The Law of Natural Growth (or Decay) asserts:

The rate of change of a quantity is proportional to the quantity itself.

Mathematically:

\frac{dP}{dt} = kP, \quad k \in \mathbb{R} \tag{1}

where:

  • P(t) is the quantity at time t
  • k is the proportionality constant (the growth/decay rate)
  • k>0: growth; k<0: decay

This is the simplest separable ODE, and it has a unique, explicit, beautiful solution.

2. Derivation — Full Rigorous Solution

2.1 Separation of Variables

Assuming P(t)=0, separate:

PdP​=k,dt

Integrate both sides:

∫PdP​=∫k,dt

ln∣P∣=kt+C1​,C1​∈R

Exponentiate:

∣P(t)∣=eC1​ekt

P(t)=±eC1​ekt=Aekt,A∈R∖0.

Check P=0: Substituting into (1): 0=k⋅0 ✓. Recovered by A=0.

General solution:

\boxed{P(t) = A e^{kt}, \quad A \in \mathbb{R}} \tag{2}

2.2 Applying the Initial Condition

Given P(0)=P0​:

P0​=Ae0=A⟹A=P0​

\boxed{P(t) = P_0, e^{kt}} \tag{3}

2.3 Uniqueness

By Picard–Lindelöf: f(t,P)=kP is Lipschitz in P (with constant ∣k∣) on any rectangle, so there exists a unique solution through any initial condition (t0​,P0​). The family (2) is therefore the complete solution — no singular solutions exist.

3. The Constant k — What It Means

Rewrite (3) as:

P0​P(t)​=ekt

Taking logarithm:

k=t1​lnP0​P(t)​

So k is the instantaneous fractional growth rate per unit time. Concretely:

  • In time Δt, the quantity changes by the factor ekΔt.
  • For small kΔt: ekΔt≈1+kΔt — a growth of approximately k⋅100 per unit time.

4. Half-Life and Doubling Time

Doubling Time (k>0)

The time T2​ for P to double:

2P0​=P0​ekT2​⟹T2​=kln2​≈k0.693​

The famous Rule of 70: for a growth rate of k×100 per year, doubling time ≈70/(percent rate) years.

Half-Life (k<0, write k=−λ, λ>0)

The time t1/2​ for P to halve:

2P0​​=P0​e−λt1/2​⟹t1/2​=λln2​

Key fact: Half-life is independent of P0​. No matter how much substance you start with, it always takes the same time to halve.

5. Examples

Example 1 — Radioactive Decay: Carbon-14

Carbon-14 decays with half-life t1/2​=5730 years.

Find k:

k=−5730ln2​≈−1.2097×10−4 yr−1

Model: P(t)=P0​,e−1.2097×10−4,t

Radiocarbon dating: A bone fragment has 72 of original C-14. How old is it?

0.72P0​=P0​,ekt⟹t=kln0.72​=−1.2097×10−4ln0.72​≈2694 years

Example 2 — Population Growth: Malthusian Model

A bacterial population of 500 doubles every 3 hours.

Find k:

T2​=3⟹k=3ln2​≈0.2310 hr−1

Model: P(t)=500,e0.2310,t

Question: How many bacteria after 24 hours?

P(24)=500,e0.2310×24=500,e5.545≈500×256=128,000

(Exactly 500×28 — 8 doublings in 24 hours. ✓)

Question: When does the population reach 1 million?

Example 3 — Newton’s Law of Cooling (Shifted Natural Decay)

Newton’s Law of Cooling states that the rate of change of temperature T(t) of an object is proportional to the difference between T and the ambient temperature Ta​:

dtdT​=k(T−Ta​),k<0

Substitution: Let u(t)=T(t)−Ta​. Then u′=T′, so:

dtdu​=ku

This is the natural decay equation! Solution: u(t)=u0​,ekt, i.e.:

T(t)=Ta​+(T0​−Ta​),ekt​

Example: Coffee at 90°C in a 20°C room. After 10 min, coffee is 70°C. Find T(30).

Find k:

70=20+(90−20)e10k⟹50=70e10k⟹k=101​ln75​≈−0.03365 min−1

At t=30:

T(30)=20+70,e−0.03365×30=20+70,e−1.0094≈20+70(0.3644)≈45.5°C

Example 4 — Compound Interest

A bank offers continuous compounding at annual rate r. Principal P0​ dollars.

dtdP​=rP⟹P(t)=P0​,ert

Comparison with discrete compounding n times/year:

Pn​(t)=P0​(1+nr​)nt

As n→∞:

limn→∞​P0​(1+nr​)nt=P0​,ert

This is one of the most important limit definitions of e — the natural growth ODE is the continuous limit of compound interest.

Example 5 — Two Measurements, No Initial Condition Given

Setup: A radioactive substance measures 8 g at t=0 and 5 g at t=1 year. When will 1 g remain?

Find k (no need for half-life):

5=8,ek⋅1⟹k=ln85​≈−0.4700 yr−1

Model: P(t)=8,e−0.4700,t

When is P=1?

1=8,e−0.4700,t⟹t=−0.4700ln(1/8)​=0.47003ln2​≈4.42 years

The only equilibrium is P∗=0. Its stability depends entirely on the sign of k.

7. The Exponential Function IS the Solution

The natural growth ODE y′=ky can be taken as the defining property of the exponential function:

ekt is the unique function equal to k times its own derivative, with value 1 at t=0.

This is why e appears everywhere in calculus — it is the base that makes the derivative of ex exactly ex (i.e., k=1). Every other base ax=exlna satisfies dxd​ax=(lna),ax — scaling the rate by lna.

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

The natural growth/decay law is not a background fact in ML — it is the structural skeleton of training dynamics, weight behavior, and information propagation.

8.1 Gradient Flow — Parameters Decay to Minimum

For a quadratic loss L(θ)=2λ​θ2 (L2-regularized), gradient flow gives:

dtdθ​=−∇θ​L=−λθ

This is natural decay with rate k=−λ:

θ(t)=θ0​,e−λt

The parameter decays exponentially toward zero at rate λ — the regularization strength is literally the decay constant. This is why L2 regularization is also called weight decay.

More generally, near a local minimum \theta^_, the Hessian H = \nabla^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta^_) governs the linearized dynamics. Each eigenmode vi​ of H (with eigenvalue λi​>0) decays independently as e−λi​t. Directions with large curvature (λi​ large) decay fast; flat directions (λi​ small) decay slowly.

8.2 Vanishing and Exploding Gradients — Natural Growth in Backpropagation

In a deep network with L layers, the gradient of the loss with respect to layer ℓ involves a product of Jacobians:

∂hℓ​∂L​=∂hL​∂L​⋅∏i=ℓL−1​∂hi​∂hi+1​​

If each Jacobian has spectral norm ρ (dominant eigenvalue), this product behaves as ρL−ℓ:

​∂hℓ​∂L​​≈​∂hL​∂L​​⋅ρL−ℓ

This is discrete natural growth/decay in depth L−ℓ (playing the role of time):

  • ρ<1: vanishing gradients — exponential decay in depth.
  • ρ>1: exploding gradients — exponential growth in depth.
  • ρ=1: neutral propagation — why residual connections (ResNets) and careful weight initialization (Xavier, He) aim for ρ≈1.

The law of natural growth is precisely why deep networks are hard to train without architectural care.

8.3 Learning Rate Decay Schedules

The most common learning rate schedule is exponential decay:

η(t)=η0​,e−λt

This solves dtdη​=−λη — natural decay applied to the learning rate itself. The intuition:

  • Early training (t small): large η for fast progress.
  • Late training (t large): small η for fine-grained convergence.

The decay constant λ controls the tradeoff. In practice, the discrete version is:

ηt​=η0​⋅γt/T,γ∈(0,1)

which is e−(t/T)ln(1/γ) — exactly P0​ekt with k=−Tln(1/γ)​.

8.4 Weight Decay IS Natural Decay

The L2-regularized SGD update:

θt+1​=θt​−η∇L(θt​)−ηλθt​=(1−ηλ)θt​−η∇L(θt​)

The factor (1−ηλ) multiplied at each step is the discrete natural decay: after T steps with no gradient signal,

θT​=(1−ηλ)Tθ0​≈e−ηλTθ0​

(using (1−x)n≈e−nx for small x). Weight decay is natural decay in discrete time — parameter values shrink exponentially toward zero unless gradients push back.

8.5 Dropout as Stochastic Decay

At each forward pass, dropout retains each neuron with probability p and zeroes it with probability 1−p. Over T training steps, the expected magnitude of a weight that receives no useful gradient signal decays as:

E[effective weight]∼pT⋅w0​=eTlnp⋅w0​

Since lnp<0 for p∈(0,1), this is natural decay in T — dropout induces an effective exponential shrinkage of “useless” weights.

8.6 Attention Score Softmax — Natural Growth Normalizing

The softmax function in attention:

softmax(zi​)=∑j​ezj​ezi​​

The numerator ezi​ is the natural growth function evaluated at the attention logit. The softmax is not merely a convenient normalization — it is the Boltzmann distribution arising from entropy maximization, and the exponential is there because the entropy-maximizing distribution over energies zi​ takes the form ezi​/T (here temperature T=1). The law of natural growth is the generating mechanism behind every attention score in every transformer.

8.7 Information-Theoretic Interpretation — KL Divergence and Exponential Families

The natural growth solution P(t)=P0​ekt belongs to the exponential family of distributions — the most important parametric family in statistics and machine learning:

p(x;η)=h(x)exp(η⋅T(x)−A(η))

Every distribution used in deep learning (Gaussian, Bernoulli, Categorical, Poisson) is an exponential family member. Their canonical form is precisely the natural growth function in the sufficient statistic T(x) — which is why the cross-entropy loss (used to train virtually every classifier) takes logarithmic form: logp=η⋅T(x)−A(η)+logh(x), recovering kt from ekt.

10. Common Pitfalls

  1. Confusing k and t1/2​ or T2​. The growth rate is k=ln2/T2​=−ln2/t1/2​. Always derive k explicitly before writing the solution.
  2. Using base 2 or base 10 instead of e. The ODE P′=kP forces base e. If the problem states doubling time T2​, write P(t)=P0​⋅2t/T2​=P0​e(t/T2​)ln2 — both are equivalent, but the ODE derivation always produces the e-form.
  3. Forgetting to check P=0. The separation step assumes P=0; verify that P=0 is also a solution (it is, trivially).
  4. Newton’s cooling — not applying the shift. Set u=T−Ta​ first; then it becomes natural decay. Working directly on T′=k(T−Ta​) without substitution is error-prone.
  5. Assuming k is always negative for “decay.” k is negative for decay, positive for growth. The sign is determined by the physics/problem statement, not by convention.
