Definition & Notation

Definition. A sequence is a function a:N→R (or C, or a metric space). We write its values as a1​,a2​,a3​,… and denote the sequence an​n=1∞​, or simply an​.

The domain is the natural numbers — the sequence is ordered and countably infinite by definition. This is the essential difference from a set.

Examples of sequences:

NameFormulaFirst few terms
Reciprocalsan​=1/n1,1/2,1/3,1/4,…
Powers of ran​=rnr,r2,r3,…
Alternatingan​=(−1)n/n−1,1/2,−1/3,1/4,…
Factorial growthan​=n!1,2,6,24,120,…
FibonacciF1​=1,F2​=1,Fn​=Fn−1​+Fn−2​1,1,2,3,5,8,13,…
Recursiona1​=2, an+1​=21​!(an​+an​2​)2,1.5,1.4167,…→2​

Note. The last example is Newton’s method for 2​. We will return to this — it is the prototype of iterative algorithms in optimization, which is the heartbeat of modern ML.

Limit of a Sequence

Definition (Informal). We say limn→∞​an​=L if an​ gets arbitrarily close to L as n gets large.

This is fine for intuition. But “arbitrarily close” and “gets large” need precision.

Rigorous Definition: The ε-N Criterion

Definition (Formal). We say limn→∞​an​=L if:

∀ε>0, ∃N∈N such that n>N⟹∣an​−L∣<ε

Unpacking the logic:

  • You give me any tolerance ε>0 (no matter how small).
  • I must produce a threshold N (depending on ε).
  • For all n beyond N, the sequence is within ε of L.

The order of quantifiers is everything: ∀ε ∃N, not ∃N ∀ε.

Personal note. This definition is the foundation of all of analysis. Once you truly internalize it — not just “know” it — limits of functions, derivatives, integrals, and infinite series all fall into place. Every subsequent ε-δ argument is this definition in costume.

Terminology. If such L exists, we say an​ converges to L. Otherwise it diverges.

Proof Template: an​=1/n→0

Claim. limn→∞​n1​=0.

Proof. Let ε>0 be given. Choose N∈N with N>1/ε (such N exists by the Archimedean property of R). Then for all n>N:

​n1​−0​=n1​<N1​<ε

□

Note. The key move: we need 1/n<ε, i.e., n>1/ε. So set N=⌈1/ε⌉. The work in every ε-N proof is: rearrange the target inequality to find what N must be.

Proof Template: an​=rn→0 for ∣r∣<1

Claim. If ∣r∣<1, then limn→∞​rn=0.

Proof. Let ε>0. We need ∣rn−0∣=∣r∣n<ε. Taking logarithms: nln∣r∣<lnε⟹n>ln∣r∣lnε​

(Note: ln∣r∣<0 since ∣r∣<1, so the inequality flips.) Choose N>ln∣r∣lnε​. Then for all n>N: ∣r∣n<ε. □

Proof Template: Geometric Sequence Limit

Claim. limn→∞​n+1n​=1.

Proof. ​n+1n​−1​=n+11​<n1​. Given ε>0, choose N>1/ε. Then n>N⇒n+11​<n1​<ε. □

Limit Laws for Sequences

Theorem (Limit Laws). Suppose liman​=L and limbn​=M. Then:

  1. lim(an​±bn​)=L±M
  2. lim(an​⋅bn​)=LM
  3. lim(an​/bn​)=L/M provided M=0
  4. limc⋅an​=cL for constant c
  5. limanp​=Lp for p>0 and L>0

These are proved by ε-N arguments using the triangle inequality.

Key tool in proofs:

∣an​bn​−LM∣=∣an​bn​−an​M+an​M−LM∣≤∣an​∣∣bn​−M∣+∣M∣∣an​−L∣

Since an​ converges it is bounded (∣an​∣≤B for all n), giving:

≤B∣bn​−M∣+∣M∣∣an​−L∣<B⋅2Bε​+∣M∣⋅2∣M∣ε​=ε

with the right choice of N.

Important Theorems

Squeeze Theorem

Theorem (Squeeze / Sandwich). Suppose an​≤bn​≤cn​ for all n≥N0​, and liman​=limcn​=L. Then limbn​=L.

Proof. Let ε>0. Since an​→L, ∃N1​ s.t. n>N1​⇒∣an​−L∣<ε⇒L−ε<an​. Since cn​→L, ∃N2​ s.t. n>N2​⇒cn​<L+ε. For n>max(N0​,N1​,N2​):

L−ε<an​≤bn​≤cn​<L+ε⟹∣bn​−L∣<ε□

Classic application. limn→∞​ncosn​=0, since −n1​≤ncosn​≤n1​→0.

Monotone Convergence Theorem

Theorem (MCT). Every bounded monotone sequence converges.

  • If an​ is increasing (an​≤an+1​) and bounded above, it converges to supn​an​.
  • If an​ is decreasing (an​≥an+1​) and bounded below, it converges to infn​an​.

Proof (increasing case). Let L=supan​:n∈N (exists since the sequence is bounded above; this uses the Completeness Axiom of R). By definition of supremum, for any ε>0, there exists some aN​>L−ε. Since the sequence is increasing, for all n>N:

L−ε<aN​≤an​≤L<L+ε

Hence ∣an​−L∣<ε. □

This theorem is deep. It is equivalent to the Completeness Axiom of R — you cannot prove it in Q. The sequence an​=⌊n2​⌋/n is increasing and bounded above by 2​, but 2​∈/Q.

Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem

Theorem (Bolzano–Weierstrass). Every bounded sequence in R has a convergent subsequence.

Proof sketch. Let an​⊂[a,b]. Bisect [a,b]: at least one half contains infinitely many terms; call it I1​. Bisect I1​: at least one half has infinitely many terms; call it I2​. Continue. By the Nested Interval Property, ⋂Ik​=L for some L. The subsequence of selected terms converges to L. □

Significance. This theorem is used to prove the existence of extreme values (Extreme Value Theorem), convergence of optimization algorithms, and is the beating heart of compactness arguments in analysis.

Subsequences

Definition. A subsequence of an​ is ank​​ where n1​<n2​<n3​<⋯.

Theorem. If limn→∞​an​=L, then every subsequence also converges to L.

Theorem (Contrapositive). If two subsequences converge to different limits, the original sequence diverges.

Classic example. an​=(−1)n. The subsequence a2k​=1→1 and a2k−1​=−1→−1. Since the limits differ, (−1)n diverges.

Special Sequences & Their Limits

These should be memorized. They recur constantly.

limn→∞​np1​=0(p>0)

limn→∞​rn=0(∣r∣<1),=1(r=1),diverges(∣r∣>1)

limn→∞​n1/n=1

limn→∞​(1+nx​)n=ex

limn→∞​n!xn​=0for any fixed x

limn→∞​nlnn​=0

limn→∞​annk​=0(a>1,k fixed)[exponential beats polynomial]

limn→∞​n!an​=0[factorial beats exponential]

Hierarchy of growth rates (slowest to fastest): lnn≪np≪an≪n!≪nn

Proof: limn1/n=1

Let an​=n1/n−1≥0. By Bernoulli’s inequality, for integer n≥1:

n=(1+an​)n≥1+nan​+2n(n−1)​an2​≥2n(n−1)​an2​

So an2​≤n−12​, giving 0≤an​≤n−12​​→0. By Squeeze, an​→0, hence n1/n→1. □

Proof: lim(1+x/n)n=ex

Let an​=nln(1+x/n). As n→∞, set h=x/n→0:

an​=hln(1+h)​⋅x→1⋅x=x

using limh→0​ln(1+h)/h=1 (L’Hôpital or Taylor). Since exp is continuous:

(1+nx​)n=ean​→ex□

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Limit Laws

limn→∞​5n2+n−43n2−2n+1​

Divide numerator and denominator by n2 (the leading power):

=limn→∞​5+1/n−4/n23−2/n+1/n2​=5+0−03−0+0​=53​

Example 2 — Exponential vs Polynomial

limn→∞​n1002n​

Use the fact that limxn/n!=0 to show limrn/nk=∞ for r>1:

By AM-GM or by writing 2n=enln2 and comparing growth rates: nln2≫100lnn. The limit is +∞.

Example 3 — Recursive Sequence via MCT

Define a1​=1, an+1​=2+an​​. Does it converge?

Step 1 — Bounded above. Claim an​≤2 for all n.

  • Base: a1​=1≤2. ✓
  • Inductive step: an+1​=2+an​​≤2+2​=2. ✓

Step 2 — Increasing. a2​=3​>1=a1​. Suppose an​>an−1​. Then an+1​=2+an​​>2+an−1​​=an​.

Step 3 — Conclude. By MCT, L=liman​ exists. Taking limits on both sides: L=2+L​⟹L2=2+L⟹L2−L−2=0⟹(L−2)(L+1)=0

Since an​>0 for all n, L=2.

Example 4 — Classic ε-N Proof

Claim. limn→∞​n+12n+3​=2.

Scratch work. ​n+12n+3​−2​=​n+12n+3−2n−2​​=n+11​<n1​. We need this <ε: take N>1/ε.

Proof. Let ε>0. Choose N∈N with N>1/ε. For all n>N: ​n+12n+3​−2​=n+11​<n1​<N1​<ε□

Example 5 — The Number e

The sequence an​=(1+n1​)n is:

  • Increasing (can be shown via AM-GM)
  • Bounded above by 3 (by Binomial Theorem: an​<∑k=0n​k!1​<∑k=0∞​k!1​<3)

By MCT, it converges. Its limit is defined as e≈2.71828…

This is one of the most important sequences in mathematics.

Connection to Series

Every infinite series ∑k=1∞​ak​ is defined through sequences — specifically, the sequence of partial sums:

Sn​=∑k=1n​ak​

We say the series converges iff Sn​ converges:

∑k=1∞​ak​=limn→∞​Sn​

So the entire theory of infinite series — geometric series, Taylor series, Fourier series — is secretly the theory of sequences. See: Series.

Key necessary condition. If ∑ak​ converges, then ak​→0. Proof: an​=Sn​−Sn−1​→L−L=0. The converse fails: ∑1/n diverges despite 1/n→0.

Connection to AI / ML / Deep Learning

This is where the abstraction pays off. Nearly everything in modern ML is a sequence — and understanding convergence is understanding whether and why algorithms work.

1. Gradient Descent IS a Sequence

Define the parameter sequence:

θn+1​=θn​−η∇L(θn​)

This is a recursively defined sequence in Rd (or millions of dimensions). The central question of optimization theory is:

Does θn​ converge, and to what?

The tools are exactly the ones in this note:

  • MCT analog: If the loss is bounded below and decreasing along the sequence, we need a monotone convergence argument (in Rd, this requires convexity).
  • Cauchy criterion: Convergence without knowing the limit — useful when the true minimum is unknown.
  • Subsequences: Stochastic gradient descent produces a random sequence; convergence is shown for subsequences with probability 1 (almost sure convergence).

For strongly convex loss with Lipschitz gradient and step size η<2/L:

|\theta_n - \theta^_| \leq \left(1 - \eta\mu\right)^n |\theta_0 - \theta^_|

This is geometric convergence — the sequence rn with r=1−ημ<1.

2. Learning Rate Schedules — Convergence Conditions

The Robbins-Monro conditions for SGD convergence require the learning rate sequence ηn​ to satisfy:

∑n=1∞​ηn​=∞and∑n=1∞​ηn2​<∞

These are series convergence conditions on sequences. A common choice: ηn​=c/n, for which ∑1/n=∞ (harmonic series diverges) and ∑1/n2<∞ (p-series, p=2>1). Both conditions satisfied.

The first condition ensures the algorithm can “travel far enough” to reach the minimum; the second ensures the noise eventually dies out. This is rigorous sequence analysis applied directly to algorithm design.

3. Recurrent Neural Networks — Sequences as Input

RNNs process sequences x1​,x2​,…,xT​ by maintaining a hidden state:

ht​=f(Wh​ht−1​+Wx​xt​+b)

This is a time-varying recursion — a sequence of hidden states. The question of whether information at time t=1 affects hT​ as T→∞ is a question about the convergence/divergence of this recursion.

Vanishing gradients correspond to the sequence ∣∂hT​/∂h1​∣ converging to 0 (like rn→0 for ∣r∣<1). Exploding gradients correspond to divergence (like rn→∞ for ∣r∣>1). LSTMs and GRUs are architectural fixes for this sequence convergence problem.

4. Attention & Positional Encoding

The transformer’s positional encoding is defined as a sequence of vectors:

PE(n,2i)=sin!(100002i/dn​),PE(n,2i+1)=cos!(100002i/dn​)

This is a family of sequences in n (position), parameterized by frequency 1/100002i/d. The design choice ensures that relative positions can be expressed as linear transformations — a property that depends on the identities sin(n+k)=sinncosk+cosnsink, i.e., the algebraic properties of these sequences.

5. Convergence of Neural Network Training — Loss Sequences

The loss sequence L(θn​) is a sequence of non-negative real numbers. We want it to converge. Tools from sequence analysis:

  • Bounded below (loss ≥0) + decreasing (with correct step size) → by MCT, L(θn​) converges to some L∗.
  • But does θn​ converge? Not necessarily — the loss can flatten while parameters wander (flat minima, manifold of solutions).
  • Cauchy sequences in parameter space: If θn​ is Cauchy, then θn​→θ∗. This is what “converging weights” actually means.

The theory of convergence for Adam, RMSProp, etc. is active research, and it is precisely: proving that the parameter sequence θn​ is Cauchy under appropriate conditions.

6. Fixed-Point Iterations — Banach Contraction

Many algorithms are fixed-point iterations: xn+1​=f(xn​), seeking x∗=f(x∗).

Banach Fixed-Point Theorem. If f:X→X is a contraction (∣f(x)−f(y)∣≤c∣x−y∣, c<1) on a complete metric space, then the sequence xn+1​=f(xn​) converges to the unique fixed point x^_, and: |x_n - x^_| \leq \frac{c^n}{1-c}|x_1 - x_0|

This is a geometric sequence bound ∝cn. Applications in ML:

  • Value iteration in reinforcement learning (Bellman operator is a γ-contraction)
  • Newton’s method for training (quadratic convergence near solution)
  • Normalizing flows — invertible neural networks as fixed-point iterations

RL note. The Bellman optimality operator T: (TV)(s)=maxa​[R(s,a)+γ∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)V(s′)] is a γ-contraction in the ℓ∞ norm (where γ<1 is the discount factor). Therefore the sequence Vn+1​=TVn​ converges to V∗ by the Banach theorem. This is the theoretical guarantee behind value iteration.

7. Cauchy Sequences in Deep Learning Numerics

In floating-point arithmetic, exact limits don’t exist — we work with Cauchy-like stopping criteria:

∣θn+1​−θn​∣<δ(parameter change small) ∣Ln+1​−Ln​∣<δ(loss change small)

These are practical implementations of the Cauchy criterion: the sequence has “essentially stopped moving.” The relationship between these two criteria (loss converging ≠ parameters converging in non-convex landscapes) is a deep practical issue in training large models.

Practice Problems

  1. ε-N proof. Prove from the definition that limn→∞​2n2−3n2+1​=21​.
  1. Squeeze. Prove limn→∞​nsin(n2)​=0.
  1. MCT. Let a1​=2​ and an+1​=2an​​. Prove the sequence converges and find its limit.
  1. Subsequence. Show that an​=sin(nπ/2) diverges by finding two subsequences with different limits.
  1. Growth rates. Order the following by rate of growth: lnn, n0.01, 1.001n, n!, nn/n!.
  1. Recursive limit. Define a1​=0, an+1​=an​+3an​+2​. Assuming the limit L exists, find it. Then prove the sequence converges using MCT.
  1. ML connection. The gradient descent sequence satisfies |\theta_n - \theta^_| \leq (1 - \eta\mu)^n |\theta_0 - \theta^_|. Given μ=0.1, η=5, |\theta_0 - \theta^_| = 100, find the smallest n such that |\theta_n - \theta^_| < 0.001.
