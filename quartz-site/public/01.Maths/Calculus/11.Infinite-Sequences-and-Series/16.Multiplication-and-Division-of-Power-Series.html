16.Multiplication and Division of Power Series

16.Multiplication and Division of Power Series

Apr 22, 202611 min read

“Power series are the polynomials of analysis — and like polynomials, they can be multiplied and divided, but only with the correct bookkeeping.”

Tags

#calculus #power-series #analysis #sequences-and-series #AI-foundations

0. Preliminary Setup

Recall that a power series centered at a is an expression of the form f(x)=∑n=0∞​cn​(x−a)n with radius of convergence R≥0. Throughout this note we center at a=0 (i.e., Maclaurin series) for notational cleanliness. Everything generalizes trivially.

We work inside the intersection of the two radii of convergence, so every manipulation below is on a domain where both series converge absolutely — absolute convergence is what licenses rearrangement of terms.

Key Theorem (Mertens, 1867): If ∑an​ converges absolutely and ∑bn​ converges (conditionally or absolutely), then their Cauchy product converges and equals (∑an​)(∑bn​). In the power series setting both series converge absolutely inside the open disk, so Mertens applies freely.

1. Multiplication of Power Series

1.1 The Cauchy Product — Definition

Let f(x)=∑n=0∞​an​xn,g(x)=∑n=0∞​bn​xn converge on ∣x∣<Rf​ and ∣x∣<Rg​ respectively. Then on ∣x∣<R=min(Rf​,Rg​):

f(x)⋅g(x)=n=0∑∞​cn​xn,cn​=k=0∑n​ak​bn−k​​

The coefficient cn​ is called the n-th Cauchy convolution of the sequences (an​) and (bn​).

1.2 Why the Cauchy Product? — A Structural Argument

Think of it algebraically first. If we were multiplying two polynomials: (a0​+a1​x+a2​x2+⋯)(b0​+b1​x+b2​x2+⋯) then to collect all terms with xn, we need every product ak​xk⋅bn−k​xn−k where k ranges from 0 to n. This gives exactly cn​=∑k=0n​ak​bn−k​.

The passage from polynomial to power series is justified by absolute convergence — we may rearrange the double sum (∑j=0∞​aj​xj)!!(∑i=0∞​bi​xi)=∑j=0∞​∑i=0∞​aj​bi​xi+j and reindex by n=i+j to get the Cauchy product.

2. Division of Power Series

2.1 Setup

We want to compute h(x)=f(x)/g(x) as a power series, given f(x)=∑n=0∞​an​xn,g(x)=∑n=0∞​bn​xn,b0​=0.

The condition b0​=0 (i.e., g(0)=0) is essential — it ensures g is invertible near 0.

Write h(x)=∑n=0∞​dn​xn and demand f=g⋅h, i.e.,

an​=∑k=0n​bk​dn−k​for all n≥0.

2.2 The Recursive Formula

Solving for dn​:

dn​=b0​1​(an​−k=1∑n​bk​dn−k​)​

This is a recursive determination of coefficients: d0​→d1​→d2​→⋯, each depending only on previously computed values.

Base case: d0​=b0​a0​​

2.3 Worked Example — tanx=sinx/cosx

sinx=x−6x3​+120x5​−⋯,(a0​,a1​,a2​,a3​,a4​,a5​)=(0,1,0,−61​,0,1201​) cosx=1−2x2​+24x4​−⋯,(b0​,b1​,b2​,b3​,b4​)=(1,0,−21​,0,241​)

Write tanx=d0​+d1​x+d2​x2+d3​x3+⋯

d0​=b0​a0​​=0 d1​=a1​−b1​d0​=1−0=1 d2​=a2​−b1​d1​−b2​d0​=0−0−(−21​)(0)=0 d3​=a3​−b1​d2​−b2​d1​−b3​d0​=−61​−0−(−21​)(1)−0=−61​+21​=31​ d4​=a4​−b2​d2​−b4​d0​−⋯=0 d5​=a5​−b2​d3​−b4​d1​=1201​−(−21​)(31​)−241​(1)=1201​+61​−241​=152​

tanx=x+3x3​+152x5​+⋯​

This matches the known Taylor series of tanx perfectly. ✓

Here f(x)=1, so a0​=1, an​=0 for n≥1.

d0​=1,d1​=0,d2​=−b2​d0​=21​,d3​=0,d4​=245​

Again, consistent with the known expansion. ✓

2.5 Radius of Convergence of the Quotient

A natural question: what is Rh​?

Theorem. If g(0)=0, then h=f/g has a power series expansion with radius of convergence at least min(Rf​,Rg​,r), where r is the distance from 0 to the nearest zero of g in C.

For tanx: the zeros of cosx nearest the origin are at x=±π/2, so Rtan​=π/2. This is a theorem from complex analysis — the radius of convergence of a power series equals the distance to the nearest singularity in C.

3. Visualization

3.1 Cauchy Convolution as a Diagonal Sum

The coefficient cn​ in the product series comes from summing along the n-th anti-diagonal of the array [aj​bk​]:

         b_0    b_1    b_2    b_3   ...
a_0  [ a0b0 | a0b1 | a0b2 | a0b3 ]   <- diagonal 0,1,2,3
a_1  [ a1b0 | a1b1 | a1b2 | a1b3 ]
a_2  [ a2b0 | a2b1 | a2b2 | a2b3 ]
a_3  [ a3b0 | a3b1 | a3b2 | a3b3 ]

c_0 = a0b0
c_1 = a0b1 + a1b0          (anti-diagonal 1)
c_2 = a0b2 + a1b1 + a2b0   (anti-diagonal 2)
c_3 = a0b3 + a1b2 + a2b1 + a3b0

This is exactly discrete convolution — a central operation in signal processing and deep learning.

3.2 Truncation and Approximation

In practice, one computes partial products up to degree N:

f(x)≈∑n=0N​an​xn,g(x)≈∑n=0N​bn​xn

The product f⋅g truncated to degree N gives a polynomial approximation. The error is O(xN+1) — we lose no accuracy below degree N.

Approximation of e^x · sin(x) near x = 0:

x = 0.5:
  Exact:        0.4207
  Degree-3 approx (x + x^2 + x^3/3):  0.5 + 0.25 + 0.0417 ≈ 0.7917  [crude]
  Degree-5 approx:  much closer

As N → ∞, the truncated product → exact product on |x| < R.

4. Connections to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is where power series multiplication becomes profoundly modern.

4.1 Convolution = Cauchy Product in Disguise

The discrete convolution of two sequences (an​) and (bn​): (a∗b)[n]=∑k=0n​a[k],b[n−k] is exactly the Cauchy product of their generating functions.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) — the backbone of image recognition — apply learned filters via this operation. The filter weights are the bk​; the input patch is the ak​. The output feature map is cn​.

In 1D: a CNN layer is literally computing cn​=∑k​ak​bn−k​, which is the coefficient of xn in the product of two generating power series.

In 2D (images), this extends to 2D Cauchy-type sums over patches — same algebraic structure, higher index dimension.

4.2 Softmax and Taylor Approximations

The softmax function in classification: softmax(zi​)=∑j​ezj​ezi​​ involves ex, whose Taylor expansion ∑xn/n! is a power series. When computing softmax in low-precision hardware (e.g., on TPUs or in quantized models), engineers sometimes approximate ex via a truncated power series — the multiplication of approximated polynomials introduces controlled rounding errors.

4.3 Activation Functions and Their Series

Modern activation functions have series expansions used for analysis:

When we analyze how gradients behave near x=0 (the vanishing gradient problem), we expand activations in Taylor series and multiply them — this is power series multiplication at the heart of deep learning theory.

4.4 Rational Approximations — Padé Approximants

Division of power series underlies Padé approximants: [m/n]f​(x)=Qn​(x)Pm​(x)​ where Pm​,Qn​ are polynomials chosen so Pm​/Qn​ matches as many Taylor coefficients of f as possible. This requires solving exactly the power series division problem recursively.

Padé approximants are used in:

  • Model compression: approximating complex activation functions with rational functions for faster inference
  • Neural ODEs: numerical solvers for continuous-depth networks
  • Krylov methods in large-scale linear algebra (used in training)

4.5 The Z-Transform (Discrete Signal / Sequence Models)

In sequence modeling (RNNs, SSMs like Mamba), the Z-transform of a sequence (hn​) is the formal power series: H(z)=∑n=0∞​hn​z−n Convolving two sequences in time corresponds to multiplying their Z-transforms — directly the Cauchy product. This is how state-space models analyze the composition of filters and the stability of recurrent dynamics.

5. Formal Power Series — A Purely Algebraic Perspective

One can define the Cauchy product without reference to convergence at all.

The set of formal power series R[[x]] over a ring R forms a commutative ring under:

  • Addition: (an​)+(bn​)=(an​+bn​)
  • Multiplication: (an​)⋅(bn​)=(cn​) where cn​=∑k=0n​ak​bn−k​

No convergence is required. An element ∑an​xn is a unit (has a multiplicative inverse) if and only if a0​ is a unit in R.

This is why b0​=0 is necessary for division — it’s the algebraic invertibility condition.

In computer algebra systems (CAS) like Mathematica, Maple, and SymPy, power series arithmetic is implemented via formal power series truncated to a user-specified order — no floating-point convergence, just exact ring arithmetic.
