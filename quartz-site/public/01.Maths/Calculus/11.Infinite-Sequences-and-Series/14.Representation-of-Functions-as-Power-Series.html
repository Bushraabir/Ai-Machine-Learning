“The art is not in knowing that ex=∑xn/n! — it’s in knowing how to manufacture a series for anything, from first principles, using the one series you already know.”

0. The Central Idea

We want to write a given function f(x) as a power series: f(x)=∑n=0∞​cn​(x−a)n

There are two philosophies for doing this:

  1. Top-down (Taylor’s method): Compute cn​=f(n)(a)/n! directly from derivatives. Works in principle for any smooth f, but can be tedious.
  2. Bottom-up (algebraic manipulation): Start from the one series you know cold — the geometric series — and reach everything else by substitution, differentiation, integration, multiplication, or addition. This is faster, more elegant, and more illuminating.

This note develops the bottom-up philosophy rigorously, then connects it to the top-down view.

1. The Master Series: Geometric

Everything starts here.

Theorem. For ∣x∣<1: 1−x1​=∑n=0∞​xn=1+x+x2+x3+⋯

Proof. Let SN​=∑n=0N​xn. Then SN​−xSN​=1−xN+1, so SN​=1−x1−xN+1​. For ∣x∣<1, xN+1→0, hence SN​→1−x1​. □

This single series, via the four operations below, generates the power series representation of virtually every elementary function.

2. The Four Operations

2.1 Substitution

Replace x with any expression g(x), provided ∣g(x)∣<1.

1−g(x)1​=∑n=0∞​[g(x)]n

Example 1. Represent 1+x21​.

Write 1+x21​=1−(−x2)1​. Substitute u=−x2: 1+x21​=∑n=0∞​(−x2)n=∑n=0∞​(−1)nx2n,∣x∣<1

Example 2. Represent 1−x31​.

1−x31​=∑n=0∞​x3n=1+x3+x6+x9+⋯,∣x∣<1

Example 3. Represent 4+x21​.

Factor out the 4 first to get the standard form: 4+x21​=41​⋅1+x2/41​=41​⋅1−(−x2/4)1​

Substitute u=−x2/4: =41​∑n=0∞​(−4x2​)n=∑n=0∞​4n+1(−1)nx2n​,∣x∣<2

The radius of convergence: ∣u∣<1⇒∣x2/4∣<1⇒∣x∣<2.

2.2 Differentiation

Theorem. If f(x)=∑n=0∞​cn​(x−a)n has radius R, then on (a−R,a+R): f′(x)=∑n=1∞​n,cn​(x−a)n−1 with the same radius R.

Strategy: If you want a series for f′ but you know a series for f — differentiate term by term. Equivalently, if you want a series for f and you know a series for f′ — integrate (see §2.3).

Example. Represent (1−x)21​.

Observe that dxd​[1−x1​]=(1−x)21​.

Differentiate the geometric series term by term: (1−x)21​=dxd​∑n=0∞​xn=∑n=1∞​nxn−1=∑n=0∞​(n+1)xn,∣x∣<1

So (1−x)21​=1+2x+3x2+4x3+⋯

Example. Represent (1−x)31​.

Differentiate again and divide by 2: dxd​[(1−x)21​]=(1−x)32​=∑n=1∞​n(n+1)xn−1

(1−x)31​=∑n=0∞​2(n+1)(n+2)​xn,∣x∣<1

General pattern: (1−x)k1​=∑n=0∞​(k−1n+k−1​)xn. This is the negative binomial series.

2.3 Integration

Theorem. If f(x)=∑n=0∞​cn​(x−a)n has radius R, then on (a−R,a+R): ∫f(x),dx=C+∑n=0∞​n+1cn​​(x−a)n+1 with the same radius R.

This is the main tool for representing functions with no elementary antiderivative.

Example 1. Represent ln(1+x).

Recall dxd​[ln(1+x)]=1+x1​.

Get the series for 1+x1​ first (substitute u=−x in geometric series): 1+x1​=∑n=0∞​(−1)nxn=1−x+x2−x3+⋯,∣x∣<1

Integrate from 0 to x (using ln(1+0)=0 to pin the constant): ln(1+x)=∫0x​1+tdt​=∑n=0∞​n+1(−1)nxn+1​=x−2x2​+3x3​−4x4​+⋯

Reindexing: ln(1+x)=∑n=1∞​n(−1)n−1xn​, R=1.

Endpoint check:

  • x=1: ln2=1−21​+31​−⋯ (alternating harmonic — converges by AST ✓)
  • x=−1: ln0=−1−21​−31​−⋯ (negative harmonic — diverges ✓)

Interval of convergence: (−1,1].

Example 2. Represent arctanx.

Recall dxd​[arctanx]=1+x21​ (already computed in §2.1): 1+x21​=∑n=0∞​(−1)nx2n

Integrate from 0 to x: arctanx=∑n=0∞​2n+1(−1)nx2n+1​=x−3x3​+5x5​−⋯,R=1

At x=1: converges by AST to arctan(1)=π/4.

4π​=1−31​+51​−71​+⋯​

Example 3. Represent arcsinx near 0.

Using the binomial series (1+u)−1/2=∑n=0∞​(n−1/2​)un, substitute u=−x2: 1−x2​1​=∑n=0∞​4n(n!)2(2n)!​x2n

Integrate: arcsinx=∑n=0∞​4n(n!)2(2n+1)(2n)!​x2n+1=x+6x3​+403x5​+⋯,R=1

2.4 Algebra: Addition and Multiplication

Two power series centered at the same point with radii R1​,R2​ can be added/subtracted/multiplied on their common interval of convergence (radius ≥min(R1​,R2​)).

Example: Represent 1−x3x2−x+1​ as a power series.

Note: 1−x3=(1−x)(1+x+x2), so: 1−x3x2−x+1​=(1−x)(1+x+x2)x2−x+1​

Alternatively, use partial fractions and series separately, then add. Or just observe: 1−x3x2−x+1​=(x2−x+1)⋅1−x31​=(x2−x+1)∑n=0∞​x3n

Distribute: =∑n=0∞​x3n+2−∑n=0∞​x3n+1+∑n=0∞​x3n =∑n=0∞​(x3n−x3n+1+x3n+2) =(1−x+x2)+(x3−x4+x5)+⋯,∣x∣<1

3. Partial Fractions + Power Series

When a rational function has repeated or complex factors, decompose via partial fractions first, then series-expand each piece.

Example. Represent (x2−4)(x+1)2x−4​ as a power series centered at 0.

Step 1. Factor: x2−4=(x−2)(x+2). (x−2)(x+2)(x+1)2x−4​=x−2A​+x+2B​+x+1C​

Solving: multiply both sides and compare coefficients.

  • x=2: 2(2)−4=A(4)(3)⇒0=12A⇒A=0
  • x=−2: −8=B(−4)(−1)⇒B=−2
  • x=−1: −6=C(−3)(1)⇒C=2

So: (x2−4)(x+1)2x−4​=x+2−2​+x+12​

Step 2. Series-expand each term: x+2−2​=1+x/2−1​=−∑n=0∞​2n(−1)nxn​=∑n=0∞​2n(−1)n+1xn​,∣x∣<2

x+12​=1+x2​=2∑n=0∞​(−1)nxn,∣x∣<1

Step 3. Add (valid for ∣x∣<min(1,2)=1): f(x)=∑n=0∞​(−1)n(2−2n−11​)xn

4. Definite Integrals via Power Series

Many definite integrals have no elementary closed form. Power series provide systematic numerical approximation.

General strategy:

  1. Write the integrand as a power series.
  2. Integrate term by term.
  3. Evaluate the resulting numerical series to the desired accuracy (using error bounds for alternating series or comparison test).

Example. Compute ∫00.5​1+x71​,dx to 6 decimal places.

From the geometric series: 1+x71​=∑n=0∞​(−1)nx7n

Integrate term by term: ∫00.5​1+x7dx​=∑n=0∞​7n+1(−1)n​(21​)7n+1

=21​−8⋅281​+15⋅2151​−⋯=0.5−0.000488+negligible0.000002​​−⋯

≈0.499512

Error from AST: less than the first omitted term ≈2×10−6 — so the answer is accurate to 6 decimal places.

Example. Compute ∫01​e−x2,dx (the Gaussian, fundamental to probability and the normal distribution).

e−x2=∑n=0∞​n!(−1)nx2n​

∫01​e−x2,dx=∑n=0∞​n!(2n+1)(−1)n​=1−31​+101​−421​+2161​−⋯

≈0.7468

(True value: 2π​​,erf(1)≈0.74682.)

5. Visualization: Building a Function from Its Series

5.1 The Geometric Series Machine

Input: f(x) = 1/(1 - x)

  Partial sums converge to f on (-1, 1):

  S₀ = 1
  S₁ = 1 + x
  S₂ = 1 + x + x²
  S₃ = 1 + x + x² + x³
  ...

  For x = 0.5:
    S₀ = 1.000   (true: 2.000)
    S₁ = 1.500
    S₃ = 1.875
    S₇ = 1.992
    S₁₅= 1.9999

  Convergence is exponential in N when |x| < 1.

5.2 ln(1+x) Built by Integration

           1/(1+t)            integrate 0→x           ln(1+x)
  ─────────────────────  ──────────────────────→  ─────────────────
  1 - t + t² - t³ + ...                           x - x²/2 + x³/3 - ...

  Each partial sum of the integrand gives a
  polynomial approximation to ln(1+x):

  P₁(x) = x               (linear)
  P₂(x) = x - x²/2        (quadratic)
  P₃(x) = x - x²/2 + x³/3 (cubic)

  At x = 1:  1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... = ln 2 ≈ 0.693

5.3 arctan via Integration: Unwinding π

  1/(1+x²)                integrate 0→x              arctan x
  ─────────────────  ──────────────────────→   ─────────────────────
  Σ (-1)ⁿ x²ⁿ                                 Σ (-1)ⁿ x²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n+1)

  At x = 1:  arctan(1) = π/4

  Partial sums:
  1             = 1.0000
  1 - 1/3       = 0.6667
  1-1/3+1/5     = 0.8667
  ...            converges slowly to π/4 ≈ 0.7854

  (This slow convergence is why Machin's formula
   π/4 = 4arctan(1/5) - arctan(1/239) is used instead.)

6. The Validity Question: When Does the Series Equal the Function?

Getting a power series from f (via Taylor or algebraic manipulation) does not automatically guarantee it equals f on the full interval of convergence. Two conditions must hold:

  1. The series converges (radius of convergence >0, checked via ratio test etc.)
  2. The remainder Rn​(x)→0 as n→∞.

From Taylor’s theorem: f(x)=Tn​(x)+Rn​(x),Rn​(x)=(n+1)!f(n+1)(c)​xn+1

For the geometric series / substitution method, we proved equality directly (algebraically). For integrals and derivatives of known series, the term-by-term theorems guarantee equality on (a−R,a+R).

The dangerous case: functions like f(x)=e−1/x2 (with f(0)=0) are C∞ at 0, all derivatives vanish there, so the Taylor series is identically 0 — yet f≡0. Such functions are not analytic at 0. This is pathological; all the standard elementary functions are analytic on their natural domains.

7. The Uniqueness Theorem in Action

Theorem. If f(x)=∑n=0∞​cn​xn on some open interval containing 0, then cn​=n!f(n)(0)​ uniquely.

Consequence: There is only one way to write a function as a power series centered at a given point. Any clever method (substitution, integration, differentiation) that produces a convergent series representation is guaranteed to produce the Taylor series, automatically.

This means: you never need to compute derivatives to find Taylor coefficients — use algebra instead, and the uniqueness theorem guarantees correctness.

Example. Find the Maclaurin series for f(x)=x2sin(x3).

Direct Taylor computation would require computing f(n)(0) — a nightmare.

Instead: use sinu=∑n=0∞​(2n+1)!(−1)nu2n+1​, substitute u=x3: sin(x3)=∑n=0∞​(2n+1)!(−1)nx3(2n+1)​=x3−6x9​+120x15​−⋯

Multiply by x2: x2sin(x3)=x5−6x11​+120x17​−⋯=∑n=0∞​(2n+1)!(−1)nx6n+5​

By uniqueness, this is exactly the Maclaurin series. The coefficient of x5 is 1, so f(5)(0)/5!=1⇒f(5)(0)=120. We extracted a derivative value without computing a single derivative.

8. Applications in Machine Learning and Deep Learning

8.1 Linearization of Activation Functions

Representing activations as power series near operating points reveals their effective behavior.

Sigmoid σ(x)=1+e−x1​:

e−x=∑n=0∞​n!(−x)n​=1−x+2x2​−⋯

σ(x)=2−x+x2/2−⋯1​

Using 1+u1​=1−u+u2−⋯ with u=e−x−1, near x=0: σ(x)=21​+4x​−48x3​+⋯

The linear term dominates near 0 (slope 1/4); the cubic term creates the characteristic S-curve saturation. Network initialization schemes that keep pre-activations near 0 exploit exactly this linear regime.

GELU ≈x⋅Φ(x) where Φ is the Gaussian CDF:

Φ(x)=21​+2π​1​(x−6x3​+40x5​−⋯)

The power series representation of GELU explains its smoothness near 0 and its behavior as a soft gate.

8.2 Taylor Expansion of Loss — the Geometry of Optimization

For loss L(θ), Taylor expand around current iterate θt​:

L(θt​+δ)=L(θt​)+1st order∇L⊤δ​​+21​2nd orderδ⊤Hδ​​+O(∣δ∣3)

The learning rate η is precisely a proxy for the radius of convergence of the Taylor expansion — too large, and the quadratic term invalidates the linear approximation.

8.3 Kernel Methods: The Infinite Feature Map

For the polynomial kernel k(x,z)=(x⋅z+c)d, the power series expansion shows it corresponds to an explicit feature map of degree-d monomials.

For the RBF/Gaussian kernel: k(x,z)=e−∣x−z∣2/2σ2

Expand using eu=∑un/n! and e−∣x∣2/2σ2e−∣z∣2/2σ2 factorization: k(x,z)=e−∣x∣2/2σ2⋅e−∣z∣2/2σ2⋅∑n=0∞​σ2nn!(x⋅z)n​

This is a weighted infinite sum of polynomial kernels — an infinite-dimensional feature space. The power series is convergent for all x,z, which is exactly why the RBF kernel is positive definite everywhere.

8.4 Efficient Attention via Series Truncation

Standard softmax attention requires computing eq⋅k for all query-key pairs: Attention(q,k,v)=softmax(d​QK⊤​)V

The Performer (Choromanski et al., 2020) approximates ex≈∑n=0N​xn/n! (truncated power series), then uses the factorization: eq⋅k≈ϕ(q)⊤ϕ(k)

where ϕ is a random feature map derived from the truncated series. This reduces attention from O(n2) to O(n) — a computational gain enabled entirely by power series manipulation.

8.5 Representing Non-Linearities in Infinite-Width Networks

In the infinite-width limit, a neural network with activation σ implements a kernel: K(x,z)=Ew​[σ(w⋅x)σ(w⋅z)]

If σ has the Hermite expansion σ(t)=∑k​ak​Hk​(t) (where Hk​ are Hermite polynomials — themselves a power-series-like basis for L2(γ)), then: K(x,z)=∑k​ak2​(x⋅z)k

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) at initialization is exactly a power series in (x⋅z) whose coefficients are determined by the squared Hermite coefficients of σ. Choosing activations with specific power series properties (e.g., maximizing a12​) leads to better-conditioned NTKs and faster training.

11. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (Stewart §11.9 style). Find the Maclaurin series for f(x)=(1+4x)2x​.

Step 1. Note dxd​[−4(1+4x)1​]=(1+4x)21​.

Alternatively: differentiate the series for 1+4x1​.

1+4x1​=1−(−4x)1​=∑n=0∞​(−4x)n=∑n=0∞​(−4)nxn,∣x∣<41​

Step 2. Differentiate: (1+4x)2−4​=∑n=1∞​n(−4)nxn−1⟹(1+4x)21​=∑n=1∞​n(−4)n−1xn−1

Reindex (m=n−1): (1+4x)21​=∑m=0∞​(m+1)(−4)mxm

Step 3. Multiply by x: (1+4x)2x​=∑n=0∞​(n+1)(−4)nxn+1=∑n=1∞​n(−4)n−1xn,∣x∣<41​

Problem 2 (MIT 18.02 style). Represent f(x)=ln(1−x1+x​) as a power series.

ln1−x1+x​=ln(1+x)−ln(1−x)

ln(1+x)=∑n=1∞​n(−1)n−1xn​=x−2x2​+3x3​−⋯

ln(1−x)=−∑n=1∞​nxn​=−x−2x2​−3x3​−⋯

Subtract: ln1−x1+x​=(x−2x2​+3x3​−⋯)−(−x−2x2​−3x3​−⋯)

=2x+32x3​+52x5​+⋯=2∑n=0∞​2n+1x2n+1​,∣x∣<1

Note: Only odd powers survive. This is the series for 2tanh−1(x) — useful in log-sum computations in softmax normalization.

Problem 3. Use power series to evaluate x→0lim​x2x−ln(1+x)​.

ln(1+x)=x−2x2​+3x3​−⋯

x−ln(1+x)=2x2​−3x3​+⋯

x2x−ln(1+x)​=21​−3x​+⋯→21​ as x→0

Power series convert indeterminate-form limits into trivial substitutions — no L’Hôpital required.
