1. Motivation — The Continuous Analogue of a Sum

We know how to evaluate many integrals exactly. We have no general method for evaluating series exactly.

The Integral Test exploits the relationship between a series ∑an​ and the integral ∫f(x),dx when f(n)=an​. The key insight is geometric: the sum is a Riemann sum approximation of the integral, and if f is monotone, the over- and under-estimates are under tight control.

This gives us two things:

  1. A convergence criterion: same verdict for sum and integral.
  2. An estimation tool: the partial sum error can be bounded by computable integrals.

The second point — estimating the remainder — is the deeper and more practically useful result. It transforms the Integral Test from a mere yes/no verdict into a quantitative tool.

2. The Integral Test — Statement and Proof

Theorem (Integral Test). Let f:[1,∞)→R be a function that is:

  • Continuous on [1,∞)
  • Positive: f(x)>0 for all x≥1
  • Decreasing: f′(x)≤0 for all x≥1

Let an​=f(n). Then:

∑n=1∞​an​converges⟺∫1∞​f(x),dxconverges

Proof.

Since f is decreasing, for each k≥1 and x∈[k,k+1]:

f(k+1)≤f(x)≤f(k)

Integrating over [k,k+1]:

f(k+1)⋅1≤∫kk+1​f(x),dx≤f(k)⋅1

That is:

ak+1​≤∫kk+1​f(x),dx≤ak​

Summing from k=1 to k=n−1:

∑k=2n​ak​≤∫1n​f(x),dx≤∑k=1n−1​ak​

Adding a1​ to the left inequality and an​ to the right:

Sn​−a1​≤∫1n​f(x),dx≤Sn​−an​​

Rearranging:

∫1n​f(x),dx+an​≤Sn​≤∫1n​f(x),dx+a1​

(⇒) If ∫1∞​f,dx=L<∞, then Sn​≤L+a1​<∞ for all n. Since Sn​ is increasing (all an​>0) and bounded, it converges.

(⇐) If ∫1∞​f,dx=∞, then ∫1n​f,dx→∞, so Sn​≥∫1n​f,dx+an​→∞. The series diverges. □

The sum is squeezed between two shifted versions of the integral. Same finiteness. Same verdict.

Conditions Are Necessary

The theorem requires all three conditions. Counterexamples when each fails:

Condition violatedExampleProblem
Not positivef(x)=sin(πx)∫1∞​sin(πx),dx diverges, ∑sin(πn)=0 converges
Not decreasingf(x)=sin2(πx)+x21​Rectangles no longer sandwich the integral
Not continuousPathological step functionsIntegral may not reflect the sum

The decreasing condition is the load-bearing assumption. It guarantees the rectangle comparison is valid. Never apply the Integral Test to a non-monotone function without first verifying the verdict another way.

3. The p-Series — The Fundamental Application

Theorem (p-Series). For p∈R:

∑n=1∞​np1​{converges​p>1 diverges​p≤1​

Proof. Apply the Integral Test with f(x)=x−p.

Case p=1: ∫1∞​x−p,dx=[1−px1−p​]1∞​

  • p>1: 1−p<0, so x1−p→0 as x→∞. Integral =p−11​. Converges.
  • p<1: 1−p>0, so x1−p→∞. Integral diverges. Diverges.

Case p=1: ∫1∞​xdx​=lnx​1∞​=∞Diverges.□

  │
  p < 0    S_n grows faster than any power

Known Exact Values

The Basel Problem and its relatives (all proved using Fourier series or complex analysis):

∑n=1∞​n21​=6π2​,∑n=1∞​n41​=90π4​,∑n=1∞​n61​=945π6​

In general: n=1∑∞​n2k1​=2(2k)!(−1)k+1(2π)2kB2k​​ where B2k​ are Bernoulli numbers.

For odd p≥3: no closed form is known. Whether ∑n31​ (Apéry’s constant ζ(3)≈1.202) is a rational multiple of π3 is an open problem.

4. Estimating the Sum — Trapping the Remainder

This is the deepest practical result in the section. We don’t just know the series converges — we can bound how far Sn​ is from the true sum S.

Setup

Let ∑n=1∞​an​=S converge by the Integral Test. Define:

  • Sn​=∑k=1n​ak​ — the n-th partial sum.
  • Rn​=S−Sn​=∑k=n+1∞​ak​ — the remainder (tail).

The Remainder Inequality

Theorem. Under the conditions of the Integral Test:

∫n+1∞​f(x),dx≤Rn​≤∫n∞​f(x),dx​

Proof. Apply the same rectangle argument to the tail. For k≥n+1:

f(k+1)≤∫kk+1​f(x),dx≤f(k)

Summing from k=n+1 to ∞ (justified since both converge):

∑k=n+2∞​f(k)≤∫n+1∞​f(x),dx≤∑k=n+1∞​f(k)

The middle is ∫n+1∞​f. The left sum is Rn+1​ and the right sum is Rn​:

Rn+1​≤∫n+1∞​f(x),dx≤Rn​

From the right inequality: Rn​≥∫n+1∞​f(x),dx. From the left, shifting index:

Rn​≤∫n∞​f(x),dx□

Bounding the Sum

Adding Sn​ throughout:

Sn​+∫n+1∞​f(x),dx≤S≤Sn​+∫n∞​f(x),dx

This traps S in an interval of width:

∫n∞​f(x),dx−∫n+1∞​f(x),dx=∫nn+1​f(x),dx≈an​

Example: Estimating ∑n21​ Using 10 Terms

S10​=1+41​+91​+⋯+1001​=1.5497677...

Upper bound for R10​: R10​≤∫10∞​x−2,dx=[−x1​]10∞​=101​=0.1

Lower bound for R10​: R10​≥∫11∞​x−2,dx=111​≈0.0909

Therefore: S10​+111​≤S≤S10​+101​ 1.6407≤S≤1.6498

True value: S=6π2​≈1.6449 ✓ (trapped in the interval).

Error of S10​ alone: at most 0.1 (i.e., <10). Not impressive for 10 terms. This motivates the improved estimate below.

5. The Euler-Maclaurin Formula — The Deep Version

The Integral Test remainder bounds are sharp but crude. The Euler-Maclaurin formula reveals exactly why the sum differs from the integral, expressed as a series of correction terms.

Theorem (Euler-Maclaurin). If f has sufficiently many continuous derivatives on [m,n]:

∑k=mn​f(k)=∫mn​f(x),dx+2f(m)+f(n)​+∑j=1p​(2j)!B2j​​[f(2j−1)(n)−f(2j−1)(m)]+Rp​

where B2j​ are Bernoulli numbers: B2​=61​, B4​=−301​, B6​=421​, …

Interpretation. The sum and integral differ by:

  • The trapezoidal correction 2f(m)+f(n)​
  • Higher-order corrections proportional to derivatives at the endpoints

Truncated to first correction:

∑k=1n​f(k)≈∫1n​f(x),dx+2f(1)+f(n)​+121​[f′(n)−f′(1)]+⋯

This is the analytical backbone of why ∑n1​≈lnn+γ (see §7).

The Euler-Maclaurin formula is one of the most powerful results in classical analysis. It connects the trapezoid rule (numerical integration) to infinite series, and the Bernoulli numbers are the fingerprints of the correction.

6. Improving Estimates — The Accelerated Approximation

The interval [Sn​+∫n+1∞​f,;Sn​+∫n∞​f] has width ∫nn+1​f≈an​.

Better estimate: Take the midpoint of the bounding interval:

S≈Sn​+21​[∫n∞​f(x),dx+∫n+1∞​f(x),dx]

The error of this midpoint estimate is at most half the width, i.e. 21​∫nn+1​f≈2an​​.

Example (continued). For ∑n21​, n=10:

S≈S10​+21​[101​+111​]=1.5498+21​(0.1+0.0909)=1.5498+0.09545=1.6452

Error: ∣1.6452−1.6449∣≈0.0003.

Compare to the crude bound: error was up to 0.1. The midpoint estimate reduced the error by a factor of ∼300 with no extra computation.

7. The Euler-Mascheroni Constant

The most beautiful application of the Integral Test estimates is the precise asymptotic of the harmonic series.

Define:

γn​=(1+21​+31​+⋯+n1​)−lnn=Hn​−lnn

Claim: γn​ converges to a limit γ≈0.5772156649…

Proof that γn​ converges.

Step 1: γn​ is decreasing.

γn​−γn+1​=ln(n+1)−lnn−n+11​=ln!(1+n1​)−n+11​

Since ln(1+x)≥1+xx​ for x>0 (from concavity of ln):

ln!(1+n1​)≥1+1/n1/n​=n+11​

So γn​−γn+1​≥0. The sequence is decreasing. ✓

Step 2: γn​ is bounded below.

From the Integral Test comparison:

Hn​=∑k=1n​k1​≥∫1n+1​xdx​=ln(n+1)>lnn

So γn​=Hn​−lnn>0. Bounded below by 0. ✓

By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, γn​→γ. □

Therefore:

Hn​=lnn+γ+O!(n1​)

What γ Represents

The Euler-Mascheroni constant γ is the precise accumulation of the “error” between the harmonic series (discrete) and ln (continuous) as n→∞.

H_n vs ln(n):

H_n

│  ╱
│╱
└────────────────────→ n

The vertical gap H_n - ln(n) → γ ≈ 0.5772...
It never closes, but it stops growing.

Whether γ is rational, irrational, or transcendental is an open problem — one of the most stubborn in mathematics.

8. Beyond the Integral Test — Kummer’s and Raabe’s Tests

When the Ratio Test gives L=1 (inconclusive) and the Integral Test is difficult to apply, these refined tests handle the borderline.

Raabe’s Test

Theorem. Let L=n→∞lim​n!(an+1​an​​−1).

LConclusion
L>1Converges absolutely
L<1Diverges
L=1Inconclusive

Why this detects p-series: For an​=1/np:

n!(an+1​an​​−1)=n!((1+n1​)p−1)→n⋅np​=p

So Raabe’s Test gives L=p: converges iff p>1. Exactly the p-series result — recovered by Raabe’s Test where the Ratio Test fails.

Derivation intuition: Raabe’s Test measures how fast an+1​/an​ approaches 1. If it approaches 1 like 1−p/n (the p-series rate), then L=p. The series converges iff p>1.

Kummer’s Test

A more general framework. Let cn​ be any sequence of positive constants. Define:

Kn​=cn​an+1​an​​−cn+1​

If Kn​→K>0: converges. If Kn​→K<0 and ∑1/cn​ diverges: diverges.

  • Setting cn​=1 recovers the Ratio Test.
  • Setting cn​=n recovers Raabe’s Test.

10. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

10.1 Estimating Generalisation Error — Remainder Bounds in Practice

In statistical learning theory, the generalisation gap (difference between training and test error) is bounded by sums involving the complexity of the hypothesis class.

For kernel methods, the generalisation bound often takes the form:

error≤C∑k=n+1∞​λk​

where λ1​≥λ2​≥⋯ are the eigenvalues of the kernel matrix, and n is the number of training samples used.

This tail ∑k=n+1∞​λk​ is exactly the remainder Rn​ of the series ∑λk​.

For a Gaussian (RBF) kernel, the eigenvalues decay like λk​∼e−αk — a geometric sequence. The Integral Test bound Rn​≤∫n∞​e−αx,dx=αe−αn​ gives an exponentially fast guarantee.

For a Laplace kernel, λk​∼k−p — a p-series. The bound Rn​≤(p−1)np−11​ gives a polynomial rate. The learning curves of kernel SVMs are directly determined by the p-series type remainder estimates.

10.2 p-Series and the Complexity of Neural Network Function Classes

The spectral bias (or frequency principle) of neural networks states that networks learn low-frequency components of target functions first. This is formalised using the Fourier decomposition of the target f:

f(x)=∑k=−∞∞​f^​(k)e2πikx

The approximation error after capturing frequencies up to ∣k∣≤N is:

error2∼∑∣k∣>N​∣f^​(k)∣2

For a function with s continuous derivatives, ∣f^​(k)∣=O(k−s), so the tail is:

∑k>N​k−2s∼∫N∞​x−2s,dx=2s−1N1−2s​(s>1/2)

This is precisely the Integral Test remainder estimate for a p-series with p=2s. The smoother the function (s large), the faster the Fourier tail decays, the fewer neurons needed.

10.3 Harmonic Series and the Coupon Collector Problem in ML

The coupon collector problem: how many samples must you draw (with replacement) from n categories to see all categories at least once?

The expected number of draws is:

E[T]=n⋅Hn​=n(1+21​+31​+⋯+n1​)≈n(lnn+γ)

This appears in ML in the context of:

  • Class-balanced sampling: how many examples needed to see all classes?
  • Vocabulary coverage: in NLP, how many tokens to see all vocabulary items?
  • Exploration in RL: in a finite MDP with n states, how many steps to visit all states?

The logarithmic growth Hn​≈lnn+γ — a direct consequence of the Integral Test and remainder estimation — means the cost grows slowly, but not as slowly as one might hope.

10.4 Euler-Maclaurin and Numerical Integration in ML

The Euler-Maclaurin formula is the theoretical basis for the trapezoidal rule in numerical integration, and its error analysis.

In normalising flows and variational inference, one must evaluate partition functions (normalisation constants):

Z=∫p∗(x),dx

or compute expectations E[f(X)]. When these are computed numerically over a grid, the error is exactly the Euler-Maclaurin remainder:

(trapezoidal rule)−(true integral)=12h2​[f′(b)−f′(a)]+O(h4)

where h is the grid spacing. This h2 convergence rate (second-order accuracy) corresponds to keeping only the first Bernoulli correction.

Spectral methods (used in scientific ML / physics-informed neural networks) achieve exponential convergence for smooth periodic functions — corresponding to keeping all Euler-Maclaurin correction terms, which then sum to a convergent series.

10.5 Remainder Estimates and Early Stopping

In iterative training algorithms, a key question is: when have we converged enough?

For an algorithm whose error after t steps satisfies εt​≤C⋅f(t) (where f is a known function), the total residual error over all remaining steps is:

∑s=t+1∞​εs​≤C∑s=t+1∞​f(s)

This is the remainder Rt​ of the series ∑f(s).

For subgradient methods: εt​∼1/t​, so Rt​∼∫t∞​s−1/2,ds=2t​→∞ — the total remaining error is infinite! This reflects the fact that subgradient methods don’t converge to a point — they converge in a Cesàro (average) sense.

For gradient descent on strongly convex losses: εt​∼ρt (geometric), so Rt​≤1−ρρt+1​→0 exponentially. Training can be provably stopped once εt​<δ — the remaining error is at most 1−ρδρ​.

The Integral Test remainder framework makes this rigorous.

10.6 Zipf’s Law and the p-Series in Language Models

Natural language follows Zipf’s law: the k-th most frequent word has frequency ∝ks1​ with s≈1.

The normalisation constant (sum over all vocabulary items) is:

Z=∑k=1V​ks1​

For s≈1, this is the partial sum of the harmonic series: Z≈lnV+γ.

For a vocabulary of size V=105: Z≈ln(105)+γ≈11.51+0.577≈12.09.

Language model perplexity and entropy involve:

H=−∑k=1V​pk​logpk​=logZ+Zs​∑k=1V​kslnk​

The sum ∑kslnk​ is handled by differentiating the p-series with respect to s:

−dsd​∑k=1∞​k−s=∑k=1∞​kslnk​=−ζ′(s)

where ζ is the Riemann zeta function — the p-series for real s>1, extended analytically.

12. Common Pitfalls

MistakeCorrection
Applying the Integral Test to a non-decreasing functionThe bounds fail. Verify f′(x)≤0 explicitly before applying.
Concluding ∑an​=∫f,dxThe test says same convergence/divergence. The sum = the integral. For ∑n21​, the integral ∫1∞​x−2,dx=1, but ∑n21​=6π2​=1.
Using the upper bound Rn​≤∫n∞​f and claiming exact errorThis is an upper bound. The true error may be much smaller. Use the interval [∫n+1∞​f,;∫n∞​f] for a tight bound.
Forgetting to check starting indexIf f is only decreasing for x≥N0​>1, start the integral at N0​. Finitely many initial terms don’t affect convergence.
Using Raabe’s Test with L=1 without further analysisRaabe’s Test is also inconclusive at L=1. Gauss’s test or logarithmic tests are needed.
Confusing Rn​ with an+1​The AST gives Rn​≤bn+1​ (next term). The Integral Test gives Rn​≤∫n∞​f. These are different bounds. For slowly decaying series, the integral bound is tighter.
