1. Motivation — A New Kind of Convergence

All the series we have studied so far via the Comparison Test lived in the non-negative world: every term an​≥0. Convergence there is purely a race against growth — the partial sums are monotone, and the only question is whether they are bounded.

Alternating series are fundamentally different. The signs flip with every term:

∑n=1∞​(−1)n+1bn​=b1​−b2​+b3​−b4​+⋯(bn​>0)

Here convergence can occur through cancellation — positive and negative terms partially annihilate each other. The partial sums do not march monotonically; they oscillate, approaching the limit from alternating sides.

This is both a strength and a subtlety: a series can converge this way even when the terms bn​ do not decay fast enough for ∑bn​ to converge on its own.

The canonical example: n=1∑∞​n(−1)n+1​=1−21​+31​−41​+⋯=ln2

The absolute series ∑1/n diverges. Yet the alternating version converges — entirely due to cancellation.

2. The Alternating Series Test (Leibniz’s Theorem)

Theorem

Let bn​ be a sequence of positive terms. The alternating series

∑n=1∞​(−1)n+1bn​

converges if both of the following conditions hold:

  1. Decreasing: bn+1​≤bn​ for all n≥N (eventually non-increasing)
  2. Vanishing: n→∞lim​bn​=0

Proof

Assume WLOG that both conditions hold from n=1. Consider the even-indexed partial sums:

S2m​=(b1​−b2​)+(b3​−b4​)+⋯+(b2m−1​−b2m​)

Each parenthesised group is non-negative because bn​ is decreasing (b2k−1​≥b2k​). So the sequence S2m​ is monotone increasing.

We can also write:

S2m​=b1​−(b2​−b3​)−(b4​−b5​)−⋯−(b2m−2​−b2m−1​)−b2m​

Each parenthesised group is again non-negative, and b2m​>0, so:

S2m​≤b1​

The sequence S2m​ is monotone increasing and bounded above by b1​. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, it converges to some limit S:

S2m​→S

Now consider odd-indexed partial sums:

S2m+1​=S2m​+b2m+1​

Since b2m+1​→0 by hypothesis:

S2m+1​=S2m​+b2m+1​→S+0=S

Both even and odd subsequences of partial sums converge to the same limit S. Therefore:

∑n=1∞​(−1)n+1bn​=S□

Geometric insight from the proof: S1​≥S3​≥S5​≥⋯≥S≥⋯≥S4​≥S2​ The odd partial sums decrease to S from above; the even partial sums increase to S from below. The limit S is trapped between every pair (S2m​,S2m+1​).

3. The Alternating Series Estimation Theorem

This is one of the most practically useful facts in all of series theory.

Theorem

Under the same hypotheses as the Alternating Series Test (both conditions satisfied), the error in approximating the sum S by the n-th partial sum Sn​ satisfies:

∣S−Sn​∣≤bn+1​

That is, the error is bounded by the first omitted term, and the true sum lies between Sn​ and Sn+1​.

Proof

From the proof above, we know:

S2m​≤S≤S2m+1​

Case: n=2m (even). Then:

S−Sn​=S−S2m​≤S2m+1​−S2m​=b2m+1​=bn+1​

and S−Sn​≥0, so ∣S−Sn​∣≤bn+1​.

Case: n=2m+1 (odd). Then:

Sn​−S=S2m+1​−S≤S2m+1​−S2m​=b2m+1​=bn+1​

and Sn​−S≥0, so ∣S−Sn​∣≤bn+1​=bn+1​. □

Practical power: To approximate an alternating series to within ε, simply find the first term bn+1​<ε and stop at Sn​. No deeper analysis needed.

4. Absolute vs. Conditional Convergence

This distinction is the conceptual heart of the chapter.

Definitions

Let ∑an​ be any series (signs arbitrary).

  • ∑an​ is absolutely convergent if ∑∣an​∣ converges.
  • ∑an​ is conditionally convergent if ∑an​ converges but ∑∣an​∣ diverges.

Theorem (Absolute Convergence Implies Convergence)

∑∣an​∣<∞⟹∑an​<∞

Proof. Define pn​=max(an​,0) and qn​=max(−an​,0), so that an​=pn​−qn​ and ∣an​∣=pn​+qn​. Since 0≤pn​≤∣an​∣ and 0≤qn​≤∣an​∣, both ∑pn​ and ∑qn​ converge by comparison with ∑∣an​∣. Therefore ∑an​=∑pn​−∑qn​ converges. □

The Hierarchy

Absolutely Convergent⊊Conditionally Convergent⊊Divergent

| Series | ∑an​ | ∑∣an​∣ | Type | |---|---|---|---| | ∑n2(−1)n+1​ | Converges | Converges (p=2) | Absolutely convergent | | ∑n(−1)n+1​ | Converges (=ln2) | Diverges (harmonic) | Conditionally convergent | | ∑3n​(−1)n+1​ | Converges (AST) | Diverges (p=1/3) | Conditionally convergent | | ∑n+1(−1)n+1n​ | Diverges (bn​→0) | Diverges | Divergent |

5. The Riemann Rearrangement Theorem

“Conditional convergence is a deal with the devil.”

Theorem (Riemann, 1854)

If ∑an​ is conditionally convergent, then for any L∈R (or L=±∞), there exists a rearrangement of the terms of ∑an​ that converges to L.

Proof sketch. Let pn​ be the positive terms and qn​ the absolute values of negative terms, in order. Since ∑an​ converges conditionally, both ∑pn​=+∞ and ∑qn​=+∞ (if either were finite, ∑∣an​∣ would converge). Also pn​→0 and qn​→0.

To hit target L: take enough positive terms to just exceed L, then enough negative terms to just dip below L, then repeat. Since pn​,qn​→0, the overshoots shrink to zero, and the partial sums of the rearrangement converge to L. □

Moral: For conditionally convergent series, the sum depends on the order of summation. Rearranging terms is mathematically dangerous. Absolutely convergent series are immune — their sum is invariant under rearrangement (a theorem due to Dirichlet).

6. Visual Intuition

The Oscillating Partial Sums

 S₁ = b₁                              ●  ← overshoots from above
 S₂ = b₁ - b₂            ●            ← undershoots from below
 S₃ = b₁ - b₂ + b₃                ●   ← overshoots again
 S₄ = ...              ●               ← undershoots again
                                ↕ b_{n+1}  ← error bound
 S   (true limit)          ────────────── ←

Each partial sum overshoots or undershoots the true limit S by at most bn+1​. The sequence zigzags toward S, tightening with each step.

Why Both Conditions Are Necessary

If bn​→0: The terms never become negligible. The partial sums oscillate with bounded amplitude and never settle. (Divergence Test fires.)

If bn​ is not decreasing: A large term can cause a jump that the following small term cannot undo.

Counterexample — non-decreasing bn​ with bn​→0: let

bn​={1/n​n odd 1/n2​n even​

Here b2​=1/4<b3​=1/3: the sequence is not monotone. This series actually still converges (the odd-term partial sums can be analysed separately), but the standard Leibniz proof does not apply, and more delicate analysis is required.

7. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Standard Alternating Series

∑n=1∞​n(−1)n+1​=1−21​+31​−⋯

  • bn​=1/n>0. ✓
  • bn+1​=1/(n+1)<1/n=bn​. ✓ (Decreasing)
  • limbn​=0. ✓

Converges by the Alternating Series Test. The sum is ln2≈0.6931.

Approximation: How many terms to get within 0.01? Need bn+1​=1/(n+1)<0.01, so n+1>100, i.e., n≥100. So S100​ approximates ln2 to within 0.01.

Example 2 — Absolute Convergence Check First

∑n=1∞​n2(−1)n​

∑∣an​∣=∑1/n2 converges (p=2). So this series is absolutely convergent — the AST is not even needed; absolute convergence is the stronger conclusion.

Example 3 — Fails One Condition

∑n=1∞​(−1)n+12n−1n​

bn​=2n−1n​→21​=0.

Diverges by the Divergence Test. No need to check monotonicity.

Example 4 — Estimation

S=∑n=1∞​n!(−1)n+1​=1−21​+61​−241​+⋯

(This is 1−e−1=1−1/e≈0.6321.)

Approximate S to within 0.001. Need bn+1​=1/(n+1)!<0.001:

  • b5​=1/120≈0.0083 — too large.
  • b6​=1/720≈0.00139 — too large.
  • b7​=1/5040≈0.000198<0.001 ✓

So S≈S6​=1−1/2+1/6−1/24+1/120−1/720≈0.6319, error <0.001.

Example 5 — Limit Comparison Needed First

∑n=1∞​(−1)n+1nlnn​

Does this converge?

  • bn​=(lnn)/n>0. ✓
  • limn→∞​(lnn)/n=0 (L’Hôpital: 1/n→0). ✓
  • Is bn​ eventually decreasing? Let f(x)=(lnx)/x. Then f′(x)=(1−lnx)/x2<0 for x>e. So bn​ is decreasing for n≥3. ✓

Converges by AST.

Note: ∑(lnn)/n diverges by comparison with ∑1/n (since lnn>1 for n≥3). So this is conditionally convergent.

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

8.1 Taylor Series Approximations and Error Bounds in Numerical ML

Many activation functions, loss functions, and probability distributions are approximated via Taylor series, which are alternating when expanded around a point with alternating-sign derivatives. The Alternating Series Estimation Theorem is the engine behind truncation error bounds.

Example — Sigmoid function:

σ(x)=1+e−x1​=21​+4x​−48x3​+⋯

When approximating σ(x) for small x in hardware-constrained inference (e.g., edge devices), one truncates after k terms. The estimation theorem guarantees the error is at most the next term — an explicit, computable bound that hardware engineers can use to set precision requirements.

8.2 Stochastic Gradient Descent — Sign-Alternating Gradient Updates

In sign-SGD and 1-bit Adam (used in distributed training to reduce communication bandwidth), gradients are compressed to just their sign: sign(∇θ​L). The resulting update sequence

θt+1​=θt​−η⋅sign(gt​)

can, in certain loss landscapes, produce parameter trajectories that oscillate about the optimum in an alternating fashion. The convergence analysis of sign-SGD mirrors the alternating series analysis: the oscillations are controlled (and convergence is guaranteed) precisely when the effective “term sizes” η∣gt​∣ decrease to zero — analogous to bn​→0.

8.3 Fourier Series and Signal Representations

The Fourier series of many functions of interest in signal processing and spectral methods for PDEs are alternating series. For instance:

f(x)=xon (−π,π):f(x)=2∑n=1∞​n(−1)n+1​sin(nx)

In Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and spectral neural operators, solutions are sometimes approximated by truncated Fourier series. The alternating structure controls the sign of the approximation error, and the Estimation Theorem tells you the residual after N modes.

8.4 The Rearrangement Theorem as a Warning for Distributed Computing

The Riemann Rearrangement Theorem has a direct practical moral for distributed ML:

When computing a sum (e.g., a large dot product, a softmax denominator, or a cross-entropy loss) across many workers in parallel, each worker processes a different ordering of the terms. For absolutely convergent sums, order is irrelevant — the result is identical regardless of scheduling. For sums that are only conditionally convergent, different orderings can give different numerical results.

In 32-bit floating-point arithmetic, catastrophic cancellation (negative terms nearly cancelling large positives) is a real concern. The lesson: prefer numerically stable formulations (e.g., log-sum-exp) that ensure the computation is effectively absolutely convergent within the working precision, making the result order-invariant.

8.5 Alternating Least Squares (ALS)

Alternating Least Squares is a core algorithm for matrix factorisation used in recommender systems (Netflix problem, collaborative filtering). In ALS, one alternately fixes one factor matrix and optimises the other:

minU,V​∣M−UV⊤∣F2​solved by alternating: U←argminU​∣M−UV⊤∣2,thenV←argminV​∣M−UV⊤∣2

The loss decreases monotonically at each step (it cannot increase when one factor is optimally updated), playing an analogous role to the monotone subsequences S2m​ and S2m+1​ in the Leibniz proof. Convergence is guaranteed to a critical point, though not necessarily the global minimum.

8.6 Conditional vs. Absolute Convergence in Infinite-Sum Losses

Some regularisation penalties and Bayesian priors involve infinite sums over model parameters (e.g., in Bayesian neural networks). The distinction between absolute and conditional convergence determines whether the prior is well-defined independently of how the parameters are ordered or indexed. Well-posedness of such models demands absolute convergence of the corresponding series.

9. Summary of Tests — When to Use Which

SituationReach for
Terms alternate in sign, seem to go to 0Alternating Series Test
Want the stronger statementCheck absolute convergence first (Comparison / Ratio / Root)
Already know it converges — want error boundAlternating Series Estimation Theorem
Worried about rearrangement / numerical orderCheck for absolute convergence
Terms don’t go to 0Divergence Test — done

The two conditions are both necessary:

  • bn​ decreasing without bn​→0 → Divergence Test kills it.

11. Exercises

  1. Determine whether n=1∑∞​n​(−1)n−1​ converges absolutely, conditionally, or diverges.

  2. How many terms of n=0∑∞​n!(−1)n​ are needed to compute e−1 to within 10−6?

  3. Show that n=2∑∞​lnn(−1)n​ converges conditionally. (Hint: lnn is increasing and unbounded.)

  4. (Rearrangement) The series 1−1/2+1/3−1/4+⋯ sums to ln2. Find a rearrangement that sums to 23​ln2. (Hint: Riemann’s construction — take two positive terms, then one negative, repeat.)

  5. (ML-flavoured) In a distributed training setup, gradients g1​,g2​,… alternate in sign with ∣gn​∣=1/n. Show that while ∑gn​ converges conditionally, reordering the computation across workers (e.g., grouping all positive before negative) can give a different limiting value if accumulated in finite precision. What condition on gn​ would make the computation order-invariant?

  6. (Challenge) Prove that if ∑an​ converges absolutely, then any rearrangement ∑aσ(n)​ (where σ:N→N is a bijection) converges to the same sum. (This is Dirichlet’s Rearrangement Theorem.)
