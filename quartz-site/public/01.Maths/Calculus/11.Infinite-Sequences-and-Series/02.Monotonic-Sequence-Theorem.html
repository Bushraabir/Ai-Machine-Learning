Motivation

Consider the sequence defined recursively:

a1​=1,an+1​=21​(an​+an​2​)

Does this converge? To what? You might suspect 2​ — and you’d be right — but proving it converges requires more than intuition. The Monotonic Sequence Theorem (MST) gives you a two-step proof structure:

  1. Show the sequence is monotone (increasing or decreasing).
  2. Show it is bounded (above or below, as appropriate).

Conclusion: it converges. Then you find the limit by setting L=limn→∞​an​ and solving the resulting equation.

Personal note: The MST is one of the workhorses of analysis. It proves the existence of limits without ever exhibiting the limit explicitly. This “existence before computation” pattern recurs constantly — in fixed-point theorems, in the intermediate value theorem, in the convergence of optimization algorithms.

Definitions

Definition. A sequence an​ is:

  • Increasing if an+1​>an​ for all n≥1
  • Nondecreasing if an+1​≥an​ for all n≥1
  • Decreasing if an+1​<an​ for all n≥1
  • Nonincreasing if an+1​≤an​ for all n≥1
  • Monotone if it is nondecreasing or nonincreasing

Definition. A sequence an​ is:

  • Bounded above if ∃M∈R such that an​≤M for all n
  • Bounded below if ∃m∈R such that an​≥m for all n
  • Bounded if it is both bounded above and below

Definition (Supremum). The least upper bound (supremum) of a set S⊆R, written supS, is the smallest M such that s≤M for all s∈S. It need not be an element of S.

The Theorem — Statement and Proof

Theorem (Monotonic Sequence Theorem). Every bounded monotone sequence of real numbers converges.

More precisely:

  • If an​ is nondecreasing and bounded above, then limn→∞​an​=supan​:n≥1.
  • If an​ is nonincreasing and bounded below, then limn→∞​an​=infan​:n≥1.

Proof (nondecreasing case).

Let S=an​:n≥1. Since an​ is bounded above, S is a nonempty subset of R bounded above, so by the Completeness Axiom of R, the supremum L=supS exists.

We claim limn→∞​an​=L.

Let ε>0 be arbitrary. Since L=supS, L−ε is not an upper bound for S. Therefore there exists N such that:

aN​>L−ε

Since an​ is nondecreasing, for all n≥N:

L−ε<aN​≤an​≤L<L+ε

Therefore ∣an​−L∣<ε for all n≥N.

Since ε>0 was arbitrary, limn→∞​an​=L. □

Proof (nonincreasing case).

Let bn​=−an​. Then bn​ is nondecreasing and bounded above (by −infan​). By the first case, bn​→L′, so an​=−bn​→−L′=infan​. □

Remark on necessity. Both conditions are necessary:

  • an​=n is monotone but not bounded → diverges to ∞.
  • an​=(−1)n is bounded but not monotone → diverges (oscillates).

The Completeness Axiom — What’s Really Going On

The proof invokes the Completeness Axiom (also called the Least Upper Bound Property):

Axiom. Every nonempty subset of R that is bounded above has a supremum in R.

This is what distinguishes R from Q. Consider:

S=q∈Q:q2<2

S is a nonempty subset of Q, bounded above (by 2). But supS=2​∈/Q. The rationals have a “hole” at 2​.

The Monotonic Sequence Theorem would fail over Q: the sequence of rational approximations to 2​,

1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142, …

is nondecreasing and bounded above (by 2) in Q, but converges to 2​∈/Q — i.e., it does not converge in Q.

The MST is logically equivalent to the Completeness Axiom. You can take either one as the fundamental axiom of R and derive the other. Other equivalent formulations: the Cauchy completeness criterion, the Nested Interval Theorem, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem.

Corollaries and Variants

Corollary 1. A nondecreasing sequence either converges or diverges to +∞.

Proof. If bounded above, MST gives convergence. If not bounded above, for every M there exists N with aN​>M, and since the sequence is nondecreasing, an​≥aN​>M for all n≥N. So an​→+∞. □

Corollary 2. A sequence defined by an+1​=f(an​) converges if: (i) f is continuous, (ii) the sequence is monotone, (iii) the sequence is bounded. Moreover, the limit L satisfies L=f(L) (it is a fixed point of f).

Proof. MST gives an​→L. By continuity of f: L=liman+1​=limf(an​)=f(liman​)=f(L). □

Corollary 3 (Ratio test for sequences). If an​>0 and an+1​/an​≤r<1 for all large n, then an​→0.

Proof. The sequence is eventually decreasing (geometric comparison). It is bounded below by 0. MST gives convergence; the fixed-point equation forces the limit to be 0. □

Worked Examples

Example 1 — The Sequence an​=(1+n1​)n

This defines e. We show it is increasing and bounded above by 3.

Monotone: By the AM-GM inequality applied to (n+1) numbers — one copy of 1 and n copies of (1+1/n):

(n+11+n(1+1/n)​)n+1≥1⋅(1+n1​)n

(n+12n+1​)n+1≥(1+n1​)n

(1+n+11​)n+1≥(1+n1​)n

So an+1​≥an​: the sequence is nondecreasing. ✓

Bounded: By the binomial theorem:

an​=∑k=0n​(kn​)nk1​=∑k=0n​k!1​⋅nkn(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)​≤∑k=0n​k!1​≤∑k=0∞​k!1​

Now k!≥2k−1 for k≥1, so:

an​≤1+∑k=1∞​2k−11​=1+2=3

By MST, limn→∞​an​ exists. We call it e≈2.71828. ✓

Example 2 — Newton’s Method for c​

a1​=c>1,an+1​=21​(an​+an​c​)

Bounded below by c​: By AM-GM, an+1​=21​(an​+c/an​)≥an​⋅c/an​​=c​. So an​≥c​ for all n≥2. ✓

Decreasing (for n≥2):

an+1​−an​=21​(an​c​−an​)=2an​c−an2​​≤0

since an​≥c​⇒an2​≥c. ✓

By MST, L=liman​ exists. Setting L=21​(L+c/L) gives L2=c, so L=c​.

This is Newton’s method in disguise — and the proof of convergence is the MST applied to a recursively defined sequence.

Example 3 — A Decreasing Bounded Sequence

an​=nn+1​=1+n1​

Decreasing: an+1​=1+1/(n+1)<1+1/n=an​. ✓

Bounded below by 1: an​=1+1/n>1 for all n. ✓

By MST, limn→∞​an​=inf1+1/n=1.

Example 4 — Fibonacci Ratios

Let Fn​ be the Fibonacci sequence: F1​=F2​=1, Fn+1​=Fn​+Fn−1​. Define rn​=Fn+1​/Fn​.

Claim: rn​ converges to the golden ratio ϕ=(1+5​)/2.

One approach: show the even-indexed terms are decreasing and bounded below, odd-indexed terms are increasing and bounded above, and both bounds squeeze to the same value. Alternatively:

Assume rn​→L. Then dividing Fn+2​=Fn+1​+Fn​ by Fn+1​: rn+1​=1+rn​1​→1+L1​

So L=1+1/L, giving L2−L−1=0, hence L=ϕ. (MST supplies the existence; then we solve for L.)

The Theorem in Action — Finding Limits

The standard proof pattern using MST:

Step 1 — Show monotone. Use induction. For an+1​=f(an​), show a2​≥a1​, then assume ak+1​≥ak​ and prove ak+2​≥ak+1​ (by applying f and using monotonicity of f).

Step 2 — Show bounded. Use induction again. Show a1​≤M; assume ak​≤M; show f(ak​)≤M.

Step 3 — Invoke MST. Conclude L=liman​ exists.

Step 4 — Find L. Take limits on both sides of an+1​=f(an​): L=f(L). Solve.

Example. a1​=2, an+1​=41​(an2​+3).

Step 1 (decreasing): an+1​−an​=41​(an2​+3)−an​=41​(an​−1)2−0… Actually let’s check: a2​=(4+3)/4=7/4<2=a1​. Assume 1<an​≤2; then an+1​=(an2​+3)/4≤(4+3)/4=7/4<2. And an+1​−1=(an2​−1)/4>0. So an​ is decreasing and bounded below by 1.

Step 3: MST gives L=liman​ exists.

Step 4: L=(L2+3)/4⇒4L=L2+3⇒L2−4L+3=0⇒(L−1)(L−3)=0.

Since L≥1 and L≤2, we have L=1.

Connection to AI / ML / Deep Learning

The Monotonic Sequence Theorem is not merely background mathematics — it is the conceptual ancestor of convergence proofs throughout optimization and machine learning.

1. Gradient Descent — Monotone Decrease of Loss

In standard gradient descent:

wn+1​=wn​−η∇L(wn​)

For a convex loss L with L-Lipschitz gradient, choosing step size η≤1/L gives:

L(wn+1​)≤L(wn​)−2η​∣∇L(wn​)∣2

So the loss sequence L(wn​) is nonincreasing. It is also bounded below by L∗=minw​L(w)≥0. By the MST, L(wn​) converges.

This is literally the MST applied to the loss sequence. The convergence proof for gradient descent is the MST in disguise.

The bound η≤1/L is the Lipschitz condition ensuring the loss sequence is actually monotone. Violating it (learning rate too large) breaks monotonicity → oscillation → divergence. This is why learning rate tuning matters.

2. EM Algorithm — Monotone Likelihood

The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm for fitting latent variable models (Gaussian mixtures, HMMs, etc.) produces iterates θ(n) satisfying:

L(θ(n+1))≥L(θ(n))

where L(θ)=logp(data∣θ) is the log-likelihood. The log-likelihood is bounded above (by 0 for a proper probability model).

By the MST: L(θ(n)) converges.

The proof of the EM inequality uses Jensen’s inequality on the E-step and optimality on the M-step — but the conclusion of convergence is the MST.

Caveat: The MST gives convergence of the values L(θ(n)). Convergence of the parameter sequence θ(n) itself is a separate (harder) question requiring additional assumptions on the likelihood surface.

3. Value Iteration in Reinforcement Learning

In dynamic programming / reinforcement learning, the value iteration algorithm updates:

Vn+1​(s)=maxa​[R(s,a)+γ∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)Vn​(s′)]

This is the application of the Bellman optimality operator T: Vn+1​=TVn​.

Convergence proof:

  1. T is a contraction mapping with constant γ<1 (discount factor) in the ℓ∞ norm.
  2. Therefore ∣TnV0​−V∗∣ is a geometrically decreasing sequence.
  3. A geometrically decreasing positive sequence is monotone and bounded below by 0 → MST gives convergence to 0.
  4. Hence Vn​→V∗ (optimal value function).

The Banach Fixed-Point Theorem (contraction mapping theorem) is a generalization of the MST to complete metric spaces — it says “a sequence defined by a contraction converges to a unique fixed point.” The proof is the MST in a metric space.

4. Convergence of Neural Network Training — ELBO Monotonicity

In Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), the training objective is the ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund):

ELBO(ϕ,θ)=Eqϕ​(z∣x)​[logpθ​(x∣z)]−KL(qϕ​(z∣x)∣p(z))

Coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI), used in mean-field VI, updates each variational parameter to maximize the ELBO holding others fixed. Each update monotonically increases the ELBO:

ELBO(n+1)≥ELBO(n)

The ELBO is bounded above by logpθ​(x). By the MST: ELBO converges.

This is the same structure as the EM algorithm (and EM is a special case of VI).

5. AdaGrad / RMSProp — Accumulated Squared Gradients

In AdaGrad, the accumulated sum of squared gradients:

Gn​=∑k=1n​gk2​

is nondecreasing (each term gk2​≥0). In AdaGrad this is unbounded — which causes the effective learning rate η/Gn​​ to decay to zero (can be too aggressive). RMSProp replaces this with an exponential moving average:

Gn​=βGn−1​+(1−β)gn2​,β∈(0,1)

This is bounded above (by maxgk2​) and nondecreasing when gn2​≥Gn−1​… actually it oscillates. The MST perspective explains why AdaGrad can be ill-behaved (the monotone unbounded sequence Gn​→∞) and why RMSProp fixes it (introducing a forgetting factor).

6. Convergence of MCMC — Monotone Coupling

In Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods (Gibbs sampling, Metropolis-Hastings), convergence to the stationary distribution π is sometimes proved via monotone coupling:

Given two chains Xn​ and Yn​ started at different initial states, construct a coupling such that once they meet, they stay together. The probability of not yet having met, P(not coupled by time n), is a nonincreasing sequence bounded below by 0. By the MST, it converges — and one shows the limit must be 0.

This is a probabilistic MST, but the structure is identical.

The proof recipe for recursive sequences: an+1​=f(an​)(1) monotone, (2) bounded​MST​L existsL=f(L)​find L

The logical backbone:

Completeness of R⟺MST⟺Cauchy criterion⟺Bolzano-Weierstrass⟺Nested Interval Theorem

All four are equivalent — different faces of the same fundamental property that distinguishes R from Q.

Practice Problems

  1. Prove the MST for the nonincreasing case directly (without reducing to the nondecreasing case).
  1. Let a1​=1 and an+1​=2+an​​. Prove by induction that an​ is increasing and bounded above by 2. Find the limit.
  1. Let an​=∑k=1n​k21​. Show this sequence converges by applying the MST. (Bound it above using a telescoping comparison.)
  1. Give an example of a bounded sequence that does not converge. Why doesn’t the MST apply?
  1. Give an example of an unbounded monotone sequence. What does Corollary 1 say about it?
  1. (Completeness) Exhibit a nondecreasing sequence in Q that is bounded above in Q but whose supremum is irrational. What does this say about applying the MST over Q?
  1. (ML connection) Suppose L(wn​) is a nonincreasing sequence of loss values bounded below by 0. By the MST, L(wn​)→L∗. Does this imply wn​ converges? Construct a counterexample or give conditions under which it does.
