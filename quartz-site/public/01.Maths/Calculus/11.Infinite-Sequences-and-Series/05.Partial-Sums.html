1. Motivation — Why Partial Sums?

Adding infinitely many numbers is not a primitive operation. It is not something we “do” — it is something we define, carefully, using the concept of a limit.

The strategy: replace the infinite problem with a sequence of finite problems.

Given a1​,a2​,a3​,…, we ask:

What happens to the running total as we include more and more terms?

That running total at stage n is the n-th partial sum Sn​. The infinite series is then simply limn→∞​Sn​ — if this limit exists.

The partial sum sequence is the lens through which we see the series.

2. Formal Definition

Definition. Let an​n=1∞​ be a sequence. The n-th partial sum is:

Sn​=∑k=1n​ak​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​

This defines a new sequence: S1​,S2​,S3​,…

S1​=a1​ S2​=a1​+a2​ S3​=a1​+a2​+a3​ ⋮

The infinite series is defined as:

∑n=1∞​an​:=limn→∞​Sn​

The series converges if this limit is a finite number L, and we write ∑n=1∞​an​=L. Otherwise the series diverges.

The Fundamental Relationship Between Terms and Partial Sums

There is a two-way street between an​ and Sn​:

an​=Sn​−Sn−1​,n≥2​

This identity is used constantly — for example, it is the only tool in the proof of the Divergence Test.

Proof of Divergence Test using this identity: If ∑an​=L, then Sn​→L and Sn−1​→L, so: limn→∞​an​=limn→∞​(Sn​−Sn−1​)=L−L=0□

3. The Telescoping Series — A Case Where Sn​ is Explicit

A telescoping series is one where Sn​ has a closed form because most terms cancel.

Prototype: Suppose ak​=f(k)−f(k+1) for some function f. Then:

Sn​=∑k=1n​[f(k)−f(k+1)]

Writing this out:

Sn​=[f(1)−f(2)]+[f(2)−f(3)]+[f(3)−f(4)]+⋯+[f(n)−f(n+1)]

Every interior term cancels:

Sn​=f(1)−f(n+1)​

The “telescope” collapses:

[f(1) - f(2)]
         [f(2) - f(3)]
                  [f(3) - f(4)]
                           [f(4) - f(5)]
                                    ···
                                        [f(n) - f(n+1)]
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
= f(1)                                       - f(n+1)

Convergence: ∑k=1∞​[f(k)−f(k+1)]=f(1)−limn→∞​f(n+1), provided this limit exists.

Example 3.1 (Classic Telescoping)

∑n=1∞​n(n+1)1​

Key step: Partial fractions:

n(n+1)1​=n1​−n+11​

So f(n)=n1​ and:

Sn​=1−n+11​

Therefore:

∑n=1∞​n(n+1)1​=limn→∞​(1−n+11​)=1□

Example 3.2 (Telescoping with Square Roots)

∑n=1∞​n​+n+1​1​

Rationalise: multiply by n+1​−n​n+1​−n​​:

n​+n+1​1​=(n+1)−nn+1​−n​​=n+1​−n​

So f(n)=−n​ (sign reversed), and the partial sum telescopes:

Sn​=n+1​−1→∞

The series diverges, despite the terms going to zero. A reminder that an​→0 is necessary but not sufficient.

4. The Geometric Series — The Canonical Closed Form

For r=1, the partial sum of ∑k=0n​ark has an explicit formula:

Sn​=a⋅1−r1−rn+1​

Derivation. Write Sn​=a+ar+ar2+⋯+arn. Multiply by r:

rSn​=ar+ar2+⋯+arn+arn+1

Subtract:

Sn​−rSn​=a−arn+1⟹Sn​(1−r)=a(1−rn+1)⟹Sn​=a⋅1−r1−rn+1​□

Taking the limit: If ∣r∣<1, then rn+1→0, so:

∑n=0∞​arn=1−ra​

6. Partial Sums and the Definition of Convergence

Let us be fully rigorous.

Definition (Convergence of a Series). The series ∑n=1∞​an​ converges to L∈R if for every ε>0 there exists N∈N such that:

n>N⟹∣Sn​−L∣<ε

This is just the ε-N definition of the limit, applied to the sequence Sn​.

What this means concretely: After N terms, all partial sums are within ε of L. The “tail” of the series — ∑k=n+1∞​ak​=L−Sn​ — becomes arbitrarily small.

The Tail of a Series

Define the n-th tail as:

Rn​=∑k=n+1∞​ak​=L−Sn​

The series converges iff Rn​→0.

This is directly analogous to the remainder in Taylor’s theorem. In fact, when a Taylor series converges, Rn​ is precisely the Taylor remainder Rn​(x) studied in §10 of the Series note.

7. The Cauchy Criterion for Series

The Cauchy criterion reframes convergence without needing to know the limit L in advance — essential for theoretical arguments.

Theorem (Cauchy Criterion for Series). ∑an​ converges if and only if for every ε>0 there exists N such that for all m>n>N:

∣Sm​−Sn​∣=∣an+1​+an+2​+⋯+am​∣<ε

Proof. (⇒) If Sn​→L, then for large enough n,m: ∣Sm​−Sn​∣≤∣Sm​−L∣+∣L−Sn​∣<2ε​+2ε​=ε.

(⇐) Sn​ is a Cauchy sequence in R, hence convergent (by completeness of R). □

This is why completeness of R matters — in Q, Cauchy sequences need not converge (e.g., the decimal approximations to 2​).

Immediate corollary: If ∑an​ converges, then for all m>n large enough, ∣an+1​+⋯+am​∣<ε. Taking m=n+1: ∣an+1​∣<ε, which gives us an​→0 — the Divergence Test again, this time from the Cauchy perspective.

8. Partial Sums of Famous Series

8.1 Harmonic Series: Sn​≈lnn+γ

The partial sums of ∑n1​ grow, but with a precise asymptotic:

Sn​=1+21​+31​+⋯+n1​=lnn+γ+O!(n1​)

where γ=0.5772156649… is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, defined as:

γ=limn→∞​(Sn​−lnn)

9. Partial Summation (Abel’s Summation by Parts)

The discrete analogue of integration by parts. Rarely taught in introductory courses but essential for rigorous analysis.

Theorem (Abel’s Summation by Parts). Let an​ and bn​ be sequences. Define An​=∑k=1n​ak​ (the partial sums of a). Then:

∑k=1n​ak​bk​=An​bn+1​−∑k=1n​Ak​(bk+1​−bk​)

Analogy with integration by parts: Compare with ∫u,dv=uv−∫v,du, where:

ContinuousDiscrete
ubn​
dv=f,dxan​
v=∫f,dxAn​=∑k=1n​ak​
dubk+1​−bk​=Δbk​

Application — Dirichlet’s Test: If An​ is bounded and bn​↘0 monotonically, then ∑an​bn​ converges. This generalises the AST (where an​=(−1)n, and An​ oscillates between −1 and 0, hence bounded).

10. Partial Sums of Power Series

A power series partial sum is a polynomial:

Sn​(x)=∑k=0n​ck​(x−a)k=c0​+c1​(x−a)+⋯+cn​(x−a)n

This is the n-th degree Taylor polynomial Tn​(x) when ck​=k!f(k)(a)​.

Convergence is Pointwise (and Uniform Inside the Radius)

For ∣x−a∣<R, the partial sums Sn​(x)→f(x) as n→∞. This convergence is:

  • Pointwise on the open interval (a−R,a+R)
  • Uniform on any closed subinterval [a−r,a+r] with r<R (by the Weierstrass M-test)

The partial sums “peel off” from the true function further from x=0. Adding more terms extends the region of accurate approximation — this is precisely why the radius of convergence matters.

11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The concept of a partial sum — a finite approximation to an infinite process — is the operational reality of nearly all numerical computation in ML.

11.1 Truncated Series = Partial Sums in Inference

Every time a Taylor series is used to approximate a function in a neural network (activation functions, losses, Hessians), what’s actually used is a partial sum Sn​(x) — a polynomial truncation.

Example — the softplus activation: softplus(x)=ln(1+ex)=ln!(∑n=0∞​n!enx​)

For numerical stability and hardware efficiency, this is replaced by piecewise approximations that are, effectively, partial sums of related expansions.

Example — the sigmoid: σ(x)=1+e−x1​

For fixed-point arithmetic on edge devices, one uses the degree-n Taylor partial sum Sn​(x) around x=0, accepting the approximation error ∣S−Sn​∣≤∣Rn​(x)∣.

11.2 Loss Landscape via Second-Order Partial Sums

The second-order Taylor approximation to a loss L(θ) around current parameters θ0​ is:

L(θ)≈S2​(θ)=L(θ0​)+∇L(θ0​)⊤(θ−θ0​)+21​(θ−θ0​)⊤H(θ−θ0​)

where H=∇2L(θ0​) is the Hessian.

This is the partial sum S2​ of the Taylor series of L — the first 3 terms (constant, linear, quadratic).

  • Newton’s method minimizes this S2​ exactly.
  • Gradient descent only uses S1​ (the linear term), ignoring curvature.
  • Adam, RMSProp use diagonal approximations to H to improve on S1​ without the full cost of Newton.

The quality of the optimisation step is directly related to how well S2​ approximates L — which depends on the remainder R2​(θ).

11.3 Iterative Algorithms as Partial Sum Sequences

Many iterative algorithms in ML produce sequences xn​ where the solution is limn→∞​xn​, and the n-th iterate is the analogue of a partial sum.

Conjugate Gradient (CG): Solves Ax=b (used in second-order optimisers). The n-th iterate xn​ lives in the Krylov subspace spanned by b,Ab,A2b,…,An−1b — a polynomial in A applied to b, i.e., exactly a partial sum of the Neumann series:

A−1=λ1​(I−B)−1=λ1​∑k=0∞​Bk(∣B∣<1)

The n-th CG iterate corresponds to the n-th partial sum of this operator series.

11.4 Partial Sums and Mini-Batch Gradient Estimates

The true gradient of a loss over a dataset of size N is:

∇L=N1​∑i=1N​∇ℓi​(θ)

This is a finite partial sum of contributions from all data points. Mini-batch SGD computes a partial sum over a random subset of size B≪N:

∇L=B1​∑i∈B​∇ℓi​(θ)

This is an unbiased estimator of the full gradient. The convergence analysis of SGD is precisely the convergence of a stochastic sequence of partial-sum approximations.

The variance of this estimator is ∝B1​ — larger batches give more accurate partial sums of the gradient, at greater computational cost.

11.5 The Transformer as a Partial-Sum Machine

In attention mechanisms, the output is:

Attention(Q,K,V)i​=∑j=1n​αij​Vj​,αij​=∑k​exp(Qi​⋅Kk​/d​)exp(Qi​⋅Kj​/d​)​

This is a weighted partial sum over all n tokens. The key distinction vs. a raw series:

  • The weights αij​ sum to 1 (a probability simplex).
  • For very long sequences (n→∞ in principle), one studies whether this sum is dominated by a few large terms or spread out — directly related to the “spikiness” of the softmax, controlled by the temperature d​.

Linear attention (Performers, etc.) rewrites the attention sum as:

∑j=1n​ϕ(Qi​)⊤ϕ(Kj​)Vj​=ϕ(Qi​)⊤(∑j=1n​ϕ(Kj​)Vj⊤​)

The inner parentheses is a partial sum that can be built incrementally — this is the basis of efficient linear-time attention, directly exploiting the structure of partial sums.

11.6 Convergence Rate = Approximation Quality

In learning theory, the generalisation error of a model trained on n samples is often bounded by:

E[error]≤C⋅n​1​

This is the same n​1​ decay as the tail of the p-series with p=21​… and as the partial sum error of ∑k​1​. It is not a coincidence — both arise from the same central limit theorem asymptotics.

The convergence rate of partial sums — how fast ∣S−Sn​∣→0 — is the mathematical object being studied when one asks: how many data points (or training steps) are needed to achieve accuracy ε?

12. Worked Examples — Computing Sn​ Explicitly

Example 1: Telescoping via Partial Fractions

∑n=1∞​n2+2n2​=∑n=1∞​n(n+2)2​

Partial fractions:

n(n+2)2​=n1​−n+21​

Write out Sn​ explicitly:

Sn​=(11​−31​)+(21​−41​)+(31​−51​)+⋯+(n1​−n+21​)

This is a shifted telescoping — terms cancel with a gap of 2. The surviving terms are:

Sn​=1+21​−n+11​−n+21​

As n→∞:

∑n=1∞​n(n+2)2​=1+21​=23​□

Example 2: Recognising a Geometric Partial Sum

∑n=010​3⋅(52​)n

S10​=3⋅1−2/51−(2/5)11​=3⋅3/51−(2/5)11​=5[1−(52​)11]

(2/5)11=211/511=2048/48828125≈0.0000419

S10​≈5(1−0.0000419)≈4.9998

Compare to the infinite sum: 1−2/53​=3/53​=5. After just 11 terms, we’re within 0.02 of the limit.

Example 3: The n-th Partial Sum of an Arithmetico-Geometric Series

∑k=1n​krk

This arises in the analysis of weighted averages and discounted rewards in reinforcement learning.

Method: Differentiate the geometric partial sum.

From: ∑k=0n​rk=1−r1−rn+1​

Differentiate both sides with respect to r:

∑k=1n​krk−1=drd​[1−r1−rn+1​]=(1−r)2−(n+1)rn(1−r)+(1−rn+1)​

Multiply both sides by r:

k=1∑n​krk=r⋅(1−r)21−(n+1)rn+nrn+1​​

As n→∞ with ∣r∣<1: rn→0, giving:

∑k=1∞​krk=(1−r)2r​

ML relevance: In the discounted return of reinforcement learning, the value function is V=∑t=0∞​γtrt​ for discount factor γ∈(0,1). If rewards are linear in time (rt​=t), this is exactly ∑kγk=(1−γ)2γ​.

Example 4: Bounding the Tail (Error Estimation)

Problem. How many terms of ∑n=1∞​n21​ must we sum to guarantee error <0.01?

Tail estimate via Integral Test:

RN​=∑n=N+1∞​n21​<∫N∞​x21​,dx=N1​

Require N1​<0.01, so N>100.

Better bound (trapping the tail between two integrals):

∫N+1∞​x2dx​<RN​<∫N∞​x2dx​

N+11​<RN​<N1​

So N=100 guarantees R100​<0.01. ✓

13. Common Pitfalls

MistakeCorrect Understanding
Thinking Sn​ and an​ are the same sequencean​=Sn​−Sn−1​. They are related but distinct.
”The series converges because an​→0”an​→0 is necessary, not sufficient.
Computing Sn​ without checking the telescoping patternAlways write out the first few terms to spot cancellation.
Forgetting that endpoints of power series must be checked separatelyThe radius of convergence R tells you nothing about $
Assuming Sn​ converges monotonicallyIt may oscillate while converging (alternating series).
”A larger partial sum means the series converges faster”Convergence speed is about how fast ∥S−Sn​∥ decays, not the size of Sn​.
