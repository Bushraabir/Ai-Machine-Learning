1. From Sequences to Series — The Conceptual Leap

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers: a1​,a2​,a3​,…

A series is what happens when you add them all up. But “adding infinitely many numbers” is not a self-evident operation — it requires the machinery of limits.

The question is never “what is ∑n=1∞​an​?” in isolation. The question is always: does the sequence of partial sums converge, and if so, to what?

Personal note: Students often confuse the sequence (an​) with the series ∑an​. Keep them distinct. The series is not the terms — it is the limit of accumulated partial sums.

2. Formal Definition

Definition. Given a sequence an​n=1∞​, define the n-th partial sum:

Sn​=∑k=1n​ak​=a1​+a2​+⋯+an​

The infinite series ∑n=1∞​an​ is defined to be:

∑n=1∞​an​=limn→∞​Sn​

provided this limit exists and is finite. If it does, we say the series converges. Otherwise it diverges.

So a series is, at its heart, a limit problem — the limit of a sequence of partial sums.

Visualization: The Partial Sum Staircase

S_n
|
|                                       ___________  ← L (limit)
|                            __________/
|                  _________/
|        _________/
|_______/
|
+----+----+----+----+----+----+----+----> n
  1    2    3    4    5    6    7

If the staircase levels off to a finite height L, the series converges to L.

3. The Geometric Series — Our Anchor Example

The geometric series is the most important series to internalize:

∑n=0∞​arn=a+ar+ar2+ar3+⋯

Theorem. The geometric series converges if and only if ∣r∣<1, and in that case:

∑n=0∞​arn=1−ra​,∣r∣<1

Proof sketch. The n-th partial sum is:

Sn​=a⋅1−r1−rn​,r=1

As n→∞: if ∣r∣<1 then rn→0, giving Sn​→1−ra​. If ∣r∣≥1, rn does not go to zero, so Sn​ diverges. □

Example. n=0∑∞​2n1​=1−21​1​=2

Visual: Adding halves
|←——————————————————————————————————————→| = 2
|←————————→| + |←———→| + |←→| + |←| + ...
     1         1/2      1/4   1/8  ...

Each step covers half the remaining gap. You approach 2 but never exceed it in finite steps — yet the limit is exactly 2.

4. Convergence and Divergence

Divergence can occur in several modes:

ModeExampleBehaviour of Sn​
Diverges to +∞∑1Sn​=n→∞
Diverges to −∞∑(−1)Sn​=−n→−∞
Oscillates (bounded)∑(−1)nSn​ alternates −1,0,−1,0,…
Oscillates (unbounded)∑(−2)nSn​ blows up with sign changes

The oscillating bounded case is subtle — the partial sums don’t go to infinity, yet the series still diverges because no limit exists.

5. The Divergence Test (A Necessary but Not Sufficient Condition)

Theorem (Divergence Test / n-th Term Test). If ∑an​ converges, then limn→∞​an​=0.

Contrapositive (the useful direction): If limn→∞​an​=0 (or the limit doesn’t exist), then ∑an​ diverges.

Proof. If ∑an​=L, then an​=Sn​−Sn−1​→L−L=0. □

Critical warning: The converse is false. an​→0 does not imply convergence.

Classic counterexample: The harmonic series ∑n1​: here an​=n1​→0, yet the series diverges (see §6).

This is perhaps the most important “trap” in the entire theory of series. Burn it into memory.

6. The Harmonic Series — A Famous Warning

∑n=1∞​n1​=1+21​+31​+41​+⋯=∞

Proof (Cauchy’s grouping argument).

1+21​+≥21​31​+41​​​+≥21​51​+61​+71​+81​​​+⋯

Each bracketed group contributes at least 21​, and there are infinitely many such groups. Therefore Sn​→∞. □

Quantitative aside: Sn​≈lnn+γ where γ≈0.5772 is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. The series diverges, but glacially slowly — you need e10≈22,000 terms to exceed Sn​=10.

7. Tests for Convergence

7.1 The Integral Test

Setup: f is continuous, positive, decreasing on [1,∞), and an​=f(n).

Theorem. ∑n=1∞​an​converges⟺∫1∞​f(x),dxconverges

Visual intuition:

  f(x)
  |  █
  | ██ █
  |███ ██ █
  |████████ █ █  █   █
  +----+----+----+----+---> n
  1    2    3    4    5

Rectangles (area = a_n) compared to integral curve f(x)

The sum and the integral are “sandwiched” between each other by the comparison of rectangles to curve area.

Canonical example: The p-series: ∑n=1∞​np1​converges⟺p>1

Proof: ∫1∞​x−p,dx converges iff p>1. □

pSeriesVerdict
p=2∑n21​=6π2​Converges
p=1∑n1​Diverges
p=1/2∑n​1​Diverges

7.2 The Comparison Test

Theorem. Let 0≤an​≤bn​ for all large n.

  • If ∑bn​ converges, then ∑an​ converges.
  • If ∑an​ diverges, then ∑bn​ diverges.

Think: smaller than convergent ⇒ converges. Larger than divergent ⇒ diverges.

Example. ∑n2+11​: since n2+11​<n21​ and ∑n21​ converges, this converges.

7.3 The Limit Comparison Test

More practical when direct inequality is messy.

Theorem. Let an​,bn​>0 and suppose: limn→∞​bn​an​​=c,0<c<∞

Then ∑an​ and ∑bn​ either both converge or both diverge.

Strategy: Find a known “benchmark” series ∑bn​ that behaves like ∑an​ for large n, then take the ratio.

Example. ∑5n4−n3n2+2​: dominant terms give ∼5n43n2​=5n23​. Compare with ∑n21​: ratio →53​∈(0,∞). Since ∑n21​ converges, so does the original.

7.4 The Ratio Test

Ideal for series involving factorials or exponentials.

Theorem. Let L=n→∞lim​​an​an+1​​​.

LConclusion
L<1Converges absolutely
L>1Diverges
L=1Inconclusive

Intuition: When L<1, for large n the terms behave like a geometric series with ratio L.

Example. ∑nnn!​:

an​an+1​​=(n+1)n+1(n+1)!​⋅n!nn​=(n+1)n+1(n+1)⋅nn​=(n+1n​)n=(1−n+11​)n→e1​<1

Converges. □

7.5 The Root Test

Theorem. Let L=n→∞lim​n∣an​∣​.

Same decision table as the Ratio Test. Particularly suited when an​=(f(n))n.

Example. ∑(2n+1n​)n:

n∣an​∣​=2n+1n​→21​<1⇒ converges

7.6 The Alternating Series Test (Leibniz’s Theorem)

Theorem. The alternating series n=1∑∞​(−1)n−1bn​ converges if:

  1. bn​>0 for all n
  2. bn​ is decreasing: bn+1​≤bn​
  3. limn→∞​bn​=0

Error bound: If S is the sum and Sn​ is the n-th partial sum, then ∣S−Sn​∣≤bn+1​.

Example. The alternating harmonic series: ∑n=1∞​n(−1)n−1​=1−21​+31​−41​+⋯=ln2

Note: the absolute values give the harmonic series (diverges), yet the alternating version converges — this is the phenomenon of conditional convergence.

8. Absolute vs. Conditional Convergence

Definition. A series ∑an​ is:

  • Absolutely convergent if ∑∣an​∣ converges.
  • Conditionally convergent if ∑an​ converges but ∑∣an​∣ diverges.

Theorem. Absolute convergence ⇒ convergence (but not vice versa).

Proof. Note 0≤an​+∣an​∣≤2∣an​∣. If ∑∣an​∣ converges, by Comparison ∑(an​+∣an​∣) converges. Then ∑an​=∑(an​+∣an​∣)−∑∣an​∣ is a difference of two convergent series. □

The Riemann Rearrangement Theorem

A conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real number — or to ±∞.

This is one of the most startling theorems in analysis. Infinity is not commutative. Rearranging terms of a conditionally convergent series is like redistributing infinitely many signed dollars from an account that shouldn’t be solvent but somehow is.

9. Power Series

A power series centered at a is:

∑n=0∞​cn​(x−a)n=c0​+c1​(x−a)+c2​(x−a)2+⋯

Radius of Convergence

Theorem. For any power series, exactly one of three cases holds:

  1. Converges only at x=a (radius R=0)
  2. Converges for all x (radius R=∞)
  3. Converges absolutely for ∣x−a∣<R, diverges for ∣x−a∣>R, and the boundary ∣x−a∣=R must be checked separately.

Finding R: Apply the Ratio Test to ∑cn​(x−a)n:

R=limn→∞​​cn+1​cn​​​(when the limit exists)

Or equivalently (Hadamard): R1​=limsupn→∞​∣cn​∣1/n.

Visualization: The Interval of Convergence

Diverges   |←— Converges Absolutely —→|   Diverges
           |                           |
     ------+---------------------------+------
         a-R                         a+R
           ↑                           ↑
     Check endpoint               Check endpoint

Operations on Power Series

Inside the radius of convergence, power series can be:

  • Differentiated term by term: f′(x)=∑n=1∞​ncn​(x−a)n−1
  • Integrated term by term: ∫f(x),dx=∑n=0∞​n+1cn​​(x−a)n+1+C

Both operations preserve the radius of convergence (but may affect endpoint behaviour).

10. Taylor and Maclaurin Series

Definition. The Taylor series of f at x=a is:

f(x)=∑n=0∞​n!f(n)(a)​(x−a)n

When a=0, this is the Maclaurin series.

Taylor’s Theorem with Remainder: The n-th degree Taylor polynomial approximates f:

f(x)=Tn​(x)+Rn​(x)

where Rn​(x)=(n+1)!f(n+1)(c)​(x−a)n+1 for some c between a and x.

The series represents f exactly when Rn​(x)→0 as n→∞.

The Essential Maclaurin Series (Memorise These)

ex=∑n=0∞​n!xn​=1+x+2!x2​+3!x3​+⋯R=∞

sinx=∑n=0∞​(2n+1)!(−1)nx2n+1​=x−3!x3​+5!x5​−⋯R=∞

cosx=∑n=0∞​(2n)!(−1)nx2n​=1−2!x2​+4!x4​−⋯R=∞

ln(1+x)=∑n=1∞​n(−1)n−1xn​=x−2x2​+3x3​−⋯R=1

1−x1​=∑n=0∞​xn=1+x+x2+x3+⋯∣x∣<1

(1+x)k=∑n=0∞​(nk​)xn∣x∣<1(Binomial Series)

Euler’s Formula (via Taylor Series)

Substituting ix into the Maclaurin series for ex:

eix=∑n=0∞​n!(ix)n​=(1−2!x2​+4!x4​−⋯)+i(x−3!x3​+5!x5​−⋯)=cosx+isinx

Setting x=π: eiπ+1=0.

Euler’s identity emerges directly from power series. This is not magic — it is the natural consequence of extending real-valued series to the complex plane.

11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

Series and their convergence are not abstract curiosities — they are the computational substrate of modern AI. Here are the concrete connections.

11.1 Taylor Series and Activation Function Approximation

Neural networks compute compositions of activation functions like σ(x)=1+e−x1​ (sigmoid) and tanh(x).

The Taylor expansion gives:

σ(x)=21​+4x​−48x3​+⋯

For hardware-efficient inference (on embedded devices or FPGAs), these series truncations are used directly to approximate activations without evaluating full exponentials.

The GELU activation used in BERT and GPT is defined via: GELU(x)=x⋅Φ(x)≈0.5x(1+tanh!(π2​​(x+0.044715x3)))

This is a truncated series approximation — the x3 term is the second non-trivial term in the Taylor expansion of the tanh composition.

11.2 Gradient Descent and Convergence of Series

Gradient descent updates parameters via:

θt+1​=θt​−η∇θ​L(θt​)

The sequence of losses L(θ0​),L(θ1​),L(θ2​),… is a sequence we hope converges. But the cumulative update to parameters forms a series:

θ∞​=θ0​−η∑t=0∞​∇θ​L(θt​)

Convergence of this series (in the ℓ2 norm) is precisely what’s being studied when one proves convergence guarantees for gradient descent. The Ratio Test analogue here is the Lipschitz condition on the gradient and the learning rate η<2/L (where L is the Lipschitz constant).

11.3 Infinite Series in Attention Mechanisms

The softmax function:

softmax(xi​)=∑j=1n​exj​exi​​

relies on the exponential, whose Taylor series ex=∑n=0∞​n!xn​ is crucial for numerical analysis, stable computation (log-sum-exp trick), and understanding the function’s analytic properties.

In Transformers, the attention mechanism computes: Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QKT​)V

The denominator dk​​ is chosen precisely so that for large dk​, the dot products don’t drive the softmax into the saturation regime — a concern directly tied to the behaviour of the exponential function and its series expansion in extreme input regions.

11.4 Fourier Series and Signal Decomposition

Fourier series decompose periodic signals into sinusoids:

f(x)=2a0​​+∑n=1∞​(an​cosLnπx​+bn​sinLnπx​)

This is the theoretical foundation for:

  • Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs): The convolution theorem states that convolution in the spatial domain corresponds to multiplication in the frequency domain. CNNs learn to detect frequency-like features.
  • Speech recognition: Audio preprocessing (MFCC features) is a truncated Fourier series applied to short windows of speech.
  • Positional encoding in Transformers: The sinusoidal positional encoding is:

PE(pos,2i)=sin!(100002i/dpos​),PE(pos,2i+1)=cos!(100002i/dpos​)

This is a truncated Fourier-like basis — each position is encoded by its projection onto a set of sinusoids at different frequencies.

11.5 Power Series and the Exponential Map in Optimisation

In matrix analysis for deep learning, the matrix exponential is:

eA=∑n=0∞​n!An​

This appears in:

  • Lie group theory for parameterising rotation matrices (used in 3D deep learning, robotics).
  • Continuous normalizing flows (Neural ODEs), where the flow map is solved via an ODE whose solution involves the matrix exponential.
  • Preconditioned optimizers (like KFAC or Shampoo), which must compute matrix square roots and inverses — often approximated via truncated series.

11.6 Convergence Theory for Neural Networks

The Universal Approximation Theorem implicitly relies on series theory: a neural network with enough hidden units can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary precision. The proof constructs approximations via sums (effectively partial sums of a series), and the convergence in the sup norm (or L2 norm) is precisely the series convergence we study here.

In Bayesian deep learning and Gaussian Processes, kernel functions are often expressed as power series:

k(x,x′)=∑n=0∞​an​(x⋅x′)n

(e.g., the arc-cosine kernel, the RBF kernel via its Maclaurin expansion). The convergence and truncation of these series determines the approximation quality.

12. Worked Examples

Example 1: Full Analysis

Determine convergence of n=1∑∞​n+1(−1)nn​​.

Step 1: Check absolute convergence. ∣an​∣=n+1n​​∼n​1​. By LCT with ∑n​1​ (a p-series with p=21​<1), the absolute series diverges. Not absolutely convergent.

Step 2: Check conditional convergence via AST. Let bn​=n+1n​​.

  • bn​>0? Yes.
  • bn​→0? Yes: bn​=n+1n​​→0.
  • bn​ decreasing? Consider bn​−bn+1​… or check b′(x)<0 for f(x)=x+1x​​: f′(x)=(x+1)22x​1​(x+1)−x​​=2x​(x+1)21−x​<0 for x>1. Yes.

By AST: conditionally convergent. □

Example 2: Power Series Radius

Find the radius of convergence and interval of convergence of n=1∑∞​n​(−3)nxn​.

Ratio test:

​an​an+1​​​=​n+1​(−3)n+1xn+1​⋅(−3)nxnn​​​=3∣x∣⋅n+1n​​→3∣x∣

Converges when 3∣x∣<1, i.e. ∣x∣<31​. So R=31​.

Endpoints:

  • x=31​: ∑n​(−3)n⋅(1/3)n​=∑n​(−1)n​. By AST: converges.
  • x=−31​: ∑n​(−3)n⋅(−1/3)n​=∑n​1​. p-series with p=21​: diverges.

Interval of convergence: (−31​,31​]. □

Example 3: Taylor Series Application (ML-relevant)

Compute ∫01​e−x2,dx to 4 decimal places using series.

e−x2=∑n=0∞​n!(−1)nx2n​ (substitute −x2 for x in ex series).

∫01​e−x2,dx=∑n=0∞​n!(−1)n​∫01​x2n,dx=∑n=0∞​n!(2n+1)(−1)n​

=1−31​+101​−421​+2161​−⋯≈0.7475✓

(The true value, related to the error function: 2π​​,erf(1)≈0.7468.)

This integral appears in the Gaussian/normal distribution, the error function, and is directly relevant to the probabilistic foundations of logistic regression and Bayesian inference.

13. Common Pitfalls

MistakeCorrection
”an​→0 so the series converges”False. The harmonic series is the canonical counterexample.
”The Ratio Test gives L=1 so the series converges”L=1 is inconclusive. Use another test.
”A power series converges at the radius endpoint”Not always. Must check each endpoint separately.
”Rearranging a series doesn’t change its sum”True for absolutely convergent series only. Dangerous for conditional.
”If the series of absolute values diverges, the series diverges”No — the series may still converge conditionally.
