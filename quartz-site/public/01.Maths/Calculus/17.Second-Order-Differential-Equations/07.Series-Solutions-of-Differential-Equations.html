07.Series Solutions of Differential Equations

07.Series Solutions of Differential Equations

Apr 23, 202620 min read

“When the equation resists closed form, we do not surrender — we expand. A power series is not an approximation. It is an exact representation, dressed in infinitely many terms.”

§ 0 — Personal Preamble

Every ODE we have solved so far has yielded solutions expressible in terms of elementary functions: exponentials, sinusoids, polynomials. But nature is not obliged to produce equations with such convenient solutions.

The equation y′′−xy=0 (Airy’s equation, governing light diffraction) has no solution in terms of elementary functions. Neither does (1−x2)y′′−2xy′+n(n+1)y=0 (Legendre’s equation, governing gravitational and quantum potentials on spheres).

Series solutions are the systematic answer. We assume the solution is a power series, substitute into the ODE, and determine the coefficients recursively. The result is not an approximation — it is an exact representation of the solution as a convergent infinite series.

This method is the gateway to the special functions of mathematical physics — Legendre polynomials, Bessel functions, Hermite polynomials — which are the eigenfunctions of quantum mechanics, the basis of modern signal processing, and the activation-function cousins of deep learning.

References:

  • Stewart, James. Calculus, 8th ed. — §17.4 (Series Solutions)
  • MIT OCW 18.02 — Power Series and ODE notes (Haynes Miller)

§ 1 — Review: Power Series

1.1 Definition

A power series centered at x=a is:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (x - a)^n = c_0 + c_1(x-a) + c_2(x-a)^2 + \cdots \tag{1.1}

We work almost exclusively with a=0 (series centered at the origin):

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n x^n \tag{1.2}

1.2 Radius of Convergence

Every power series has a radius of convergence R∈[0,∞] such that the series:

  • Converges absolutely for ∣x−a∣<R
  • Diverges for ∣x−a∣>R
  • May converge or diverge at the endpoints x=a±R

By the ratio test: R=limn→∞​​cn+1​cn​​​ (when this limit exists).

1.3 Term-by-Term Differentiation

If y=∑n=0∞​cn​xn converges on ∣x∣<R, then on the same interval:

y' = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n c_n x^{n-1} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+1)c_{n+1} x^n \tag{1.3}

y'' = \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_n x^{n-2} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+2)(n+1)c_{n+2} x^n \tag{1.4}

The re-indexing trick — shifting the summation index so that all series start at xn with the same power n — is the single most important algebraic manipulation in series solutions. Always re-index before matching coefficients.

1.4 Identity Principle

If ∑n=0∞​an​xn=0 for all x in an interval containing 0, then an​=0 for all n.

This is the key that unlocks series solutions: we force all coefficients to vanish, generating a recurrence relation.

§ 2 — Ordinary and Singular Points

The behavior of solutions near a point x0​ depends critically on the structure of the ODE there.

Write the ODE in standard form:

y'' + P(x)y' + Q(x)y = 0 \tag{2.1}

Definition. x0​ is an ordinary point of (2.1) if P(x) and Q(x) are both analytic (representable as convergent power series) at x0​.

Definition. x0​ is a singular point if it is not an ordinary point — i.e., at least one of P,Q fails to be analytic at x0​.

2.1 Regular vs. Irregular Singular Points

At a singular point x0​, classify further:

Definition. x0​ is a regular singular point if (x−x0​)P(x) and (x−x0​)2Q(x) are both analytic at x0​.

Definition. x0​ is an irregular singular point otherwise.

The Method of Frobenius (§4) applies at regular singular points. Irregular singular points require more advanced methods (asymptotic expansions) beyond this note.

2.3 Theorem: Radius of Convergence Guarantee

Theorem. If x0​ is an ordinary point of (2.1), and P(x),Q(x) have power series convergent for ∣x−x0​∣<R, then every solution of (2.1) has a power series representation convergent in the same disk ∣x−x0​∣<R.

This theorem justifies the power series ansatz: we are guaranteed convergence at least up to the nearest singular point.

Practical corollary: The radius of convergence of the series solution is at least as large as the distance from x0​ to the nearest singular point in the complex plane.

§ 3 — Power Series Method at Ordinary Points

3.1 The Algorithm

Step 1. Verify x0​=0 is an ordinary point.

Step 2. Assume y=∑n=0∞​cn​xn.

Step 3. Compute y′,y′′ and substitute into the ODE.

Step 4. Re-index all series so every term involves xn.

Step 5. Collect terms at each power of xn and set the coefficient to zero (Identity Principle).

Step 6. Extract a recurrence relation giving cn+k​ in terms of earlier cj​.

Step 7. Determine c0​,c1​ (free parameters — they become the two arbitrary constants of the general solution). Compute subsequent coefficients from the recurrence.

Step 8. Identify the two linearly independent solutions y1​,y2​ (one involving c0​, the other c1​).

3.2 Example 1 — Airy’s Equation: y′′−xy=0

This governs light near a caustic — the bright line on the bottom of a swimming pool. No elementary solution exists.

Step 1. P=0, Q=−x: analytic everywhere. x=0 is ordinary.

Step 2. Let y=∑n=0∞​cn​xn.

Step 3. y′′=∑n=2∞​n(n−1)cn​xn−2 xy=∑n=0∞​cn​xn+1

Step 4. Re-index y′′: let m=n−2, so n=m+2: y′′=∑m=0∞​(m+2)(m+1)cm+2​xm

Re-index xy: let m=n+1, so n=m−1: xy=∑m=1∞​cm−1​xm

The ODE y′′−xy=0 becomes: ∑m=0∞​(m+2)(m+1)cm+2​xm−∑m=1∞​cm−1​xm=0

Step 5. Separate the m=0 term: m=0:2⋅1⋅c2​=0⇒c2​=0

For m≥1: (m+2)(m+1)cm+2​−cm−1​=0

Recurrence relation: \boxed{c_{m+2} = \frac{c_{m-1}}{(m+2)(m+1)}, \quad m \geq 1} \tag{3.1}

Step 6. This relates cn+3​ to cn​ (shift by 3). So c0​,c1​,c2​ are the “seeds,” but c2​=0.

Free parameters: c0​ and c1​.

Coefficients from c0​ (even-ish group):

m=1: c3​=3⋅2c0​​=6c0​​

m=4: c6​=6⋅5c3​​=6⋅30c0​​=180c0​​

m=7: c9​=9⋅8c6​​=180⋅72c0​​=12960c0​​

Coefficients from c1​:

m=2: c4​=4⋅3c1​​=12c1​​

m=5: c7​=7⋅6c4​​=12⋅42c1​​=504c1​​

All cn​ with n≡2(mod3): zero (since c2​=0 and the recurrence propagates this).

Two linearly independent solutions:

y1​=c0​!(1+6x3​+180x6​+12960x9​+⋯)

y2​=c1​!(x+12x4​+504x7​+45360x10​+⋯)

General solution: y=c0​y1​+c1​y2​.

The functions Ai(x) and Bi(x) (Airy functions) are specific linear combinations of y1​,y2​ normalized by convention.

Radius of convergence: R=∞ (no singular points anywhere).

3.4 Example 2 — Hermite’s Equation: y′′−2xy′+2ny=0

This is the ODE of the quantum harmonic oscillator. When n∈Z≥0​, the series solution terminates — giving a polynomial. These are the Hermite polynomials.

Let y=∑k=0∞​ck​xk.

y′=∑k=1∞​kck​xk−1,y′′=∑k=2∞​k(k−1)ck​xk−2

Substituting and re-indexing:

∑k=0∞​(k+2)(k+1)ck+2​xk−∑k=0∞​2kck​xk+∑k=0∞​2nck​xk=0

Collecting xk:

(k+2)(k+1)ck+2​+(2n−2k)ck​=0

Recurrence:

\boxed{c_{k+2} = \frac{2(k-n)}{(k+2)(k+1)}c_k} \tag{3.2}

Key observation: When n is a non-negative integer, cn+2​=(n+2)(n+1)2(n−n)​⋅cn​=0.

And since ck+2​ depends only on ck​, this zero propagates: cn+4​=cn+6​=⋯=0.

The series terminates at xn — giving a polynomial of degree n.

The Hermite polynomials (choosing c0​ or c1​ to give standard normalization):

H0​(x)=1,H1​(x)=2x,H2​(x)=4x2−2 H3​(x)=8x3−12x,H4​(x)=16x4−48x2+12

These are the wavefunctions of the quantum harmonic oscillator (up to a Gaussian factor e−x2/2).

3.5 Example 3 — A Simple Nonstandard Equation

y′′+xy′+y=0,y(0)=1,;y′(0)=0

P=x, Q=1: ordinary everywhere.

Let y=∑cn​xn:

∑n=0∞​(n+2)(n+1)cn+2​xn+∑n=0∞​ncn​xn+∑n=0∞​cn​xn=0

∑n=0∞​[(n+2)(n+1)cn+2​+(n+1)cn​]xn=0

Recurrence:

c_{n+2} = \frac{-(n+1)c_n}{(n+2)(n+1)} = \frac{-c_n}{n+2} \tag{3.3}

ICs: c0​=y(0)=1, c1​=y′(0)=0.

Since c1​=0: all odd coefficients vanish.

Even coefficients: c2​=−2c0​​=−21​ c4​=−4c2​​=81​ c6​=−6c4​​=−481​

y=1−2x2​+8x4​−48x6​+⋯=∑n=0∞​2n⋅n!(−1)n​x2n

Recognition: This is e−x2/2 — check: ∑n=0∞​n!(−x2/2)n​=∑2nn!(−1)n​x2n. ✓

The series solution has recognized the Gaussian — a function whose series representation is more illuminating than its closed form.

§ 4 — The Method of Frobenius (Regular Singular Points)

At a regular singular point, the power series method fails because we cannot guarantee analytic solutions. Instead, we look for solutions of the form:

y = x^r \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^{n+r}, \quad c_0 \neq 0 \tag{4.1}

where r is a (possibly non-integer, possibly complex) number to be determined. This is a Frobenius series or generalized power series.

4.1 The Indicial Equation

Consider (2.1) with regular singular point at x=0. Multiply through by x2:

x2y′′+x⋅[xP(x)]⋅y′+[x2Q(x)]⋅y=0

Since xP(x) and x2Q(x) are analytic at 0:

xP(x)=∑n=0∞​pn​xn,x2Q(x)=∑n=0∞​qn​xn

Substituting the Frobenius ansatz y=∑cn​xn+r and collecting the lowest power xr (from n=0):

c0​[r(r−1)+p0​r+q0​]=0

Since c0​=0, we must have:

\boxed{r(r-1) + p_0 r + q_0 = 0} \tag{4.2}

This is the indicial equation — a quadratic in r whose roots r1​,r2​ (with Re(r1​)≥Re(r2​)) determine the form of the two linearly independent solutions.

4.2 Three Cases for the Solutions

Let ρ=r1​−r2​.

Case 1: ρ∈/Z (roots do not differ by an integer)

Two independent Frobenius series: y1​=xr1​∑n=0∞​an​xn,y2​=xr2​∑n=0∞​bn​xn

Case 2: ρ=0 (repeated root r1​=r2​=r)

y1​=xr∑n=0∞​an​xn y2​=y1​lnx+xr∑n=1∞​bn​xn

The lnx term is unavoidable — it is forced by the repeated root, analogous to repeated roots giving tert in constant-coefficient equations.

Case 3: ρ∈Z+ (roots differ by a positive integer)

y1​=xr1​∑n=0∞​an​xn y2​=cy1​lnx+xr2​∑n=0∞​bn​xn

The constant c may or may not be zero — it must be determined from the recurrence. If c=0, the second solution is also a pure Frobenius series.

4.3 Example — Bessel’s Equation of Order ν

x^2 y'' + xy' + (x^2 - \nu^2)y = 0, \quad \nu \geq 0 \tag{4.3}

This governs vibrating circular membranes (drums), heat diffusion in cylinders, and wave propagation in cylindrical coordinates. It is arguably the most important ODE in classical physics.

Standard form: y′′+x1​y′+(1−x2ν2​)y=0.

P=1/x, Q=1−ν2/x2: x=0 is a regular singular point.

xP(x)=1⇒p0​=1, x2Q(x)=x2−ν2⇒q0​=−ν2.

Indicial equation: r(r−1)+r−ν2=0⇒r2−ν2=0⇒r=±ν.

So r1​=ν, r2​=−ν, ρ=2ν.

Case ν∈/Z (non-integer order): Both Frobenius series work independently.

Let y=∑n=0∞​cn​xn+r with r=ν. Substituting into (4.3):

The recurrence (after careful computation) is:

cn​=n(n+2ν)−cn−2​​,n≥2;c1​=0

All odd coefficients vanish. For even n=2m:

c2m​=m!,Γ(ν+m+1)⋅22m(−1)mc0​​

(using the Gamma function to handle non-integer ν). Choosing c0​=2νΓ(ν+1)1​:

\boxed{J_\nu(x) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^m}{m!,\Gamma(\nu + m + 1)}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+\nu}} \tag{4.4}

This is the Bessel function of the first kind of order ν. It is one of the most important functions in all of applied mathematics.

For integer ν=n∈Z≥0​:

J_n(x) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^m}{m!,(m+n)!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+n} \tag{4.5}

Special cases:

J0​(x)=1−4x2​+64x4​−2304x6​+⋯

J1​(x)=2x​−16x3​+384x5​−⋯

4.5 Example — Legendre’s Equation: (1−x2)y′′−2xy′+n(n+1)y=0

In standard form: y′′−1−x22x​y′+1−x2n(n+1)​y=0.

Singular points at x=±1. Point x=0 is ordinary.

Power series method (at x=0) gives recurrence:

c_{k+2} = -\frac{(n-k)(n+k+1)}{(k+2)(k+1)}c_k \tag{4.6}

When n∈Z≥0​: the series terminates at k=n (since cn+2​=0), giving a polynomial.

The Legendre polynomials (normalized by Pn​(1)=1):

P0​(x)=1,P1​(x)=x,P2​(x)=21​(3x2−1) P3​(x)=21​(5x3−3x),P4​(x)=81​(35x4−30x2+3)

Orthogonality: \int_{-1}^1 P_m(x)P_n(x),dx = \frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{mn} \tag{4.7}

The Legendre polynomials form an orthogonal basis for L2[−1,1] — they are the analogue of Fourier modes for the interval [−1,1].

They appear in the solution of Laplace’s equation in spherical coordinates (gravitational and electric potentials), quantum mechanical angular momentum eigenfunctions (spherical harmonics), and approximation theory.

§ 5 — The Recurrence Relation as Algorithm

The recurrence relation is the heart of the method. It is always a linear relation, often of the form:

cn+k​=f(n),cn​orcn+k​=f(n),cn​+g(n),cn−j​

Generating coefficients systematically:

For the Airy equation recurrence cm+3​=(m+3)(m+2)cm​​ (rewriting 3.1):

Dependency graph:

c₀ ──→ c₃ ──→ c₆ ──→ c₉ ──→ ...   (y₁ branch)
c₁ ──→ c₄ ──→ c₇ ──→ c₁₀ ──→ ...  (y₂ branch)
c₂ = 0 ──→ c₅=0 ──→ c₈=0 ──→ ...  (zero branch)

The three families are decoupled.
Two free parameters c₀, c₁ → two independent solutions.

This dependency graph structure is general: the recurrence has “step” k means indices split into k independent families, each seeded by a different cj​ (0≤j<k).

§ 6 — Convergence Analysis

6.1 The Key Theorem (Restated Sharply)

Theorem. If x0​ is an ordinary point of y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0, then the general solution is: y=c0​y1​(x)+c1​y2​(x) where y1​,y2​ are power series centered at x0​, convergent at least for ∣x−x0​∣<R, where R is the distance from x0​ to the nearest singularity of P or Q in the complex plane.

Why the complex plane? The equation y′′+1+x21​y=0 has P=0, Q=(1+x2)−1 — analytic on all of R. Yet the radius of convergence of the series solution at x0​=0 is R=1, because the singular points x=±i (in C) are distance 1 from the origin.

6.2 Ratio Test for Bessel Functions

For J0​(x)=∑m=0∞​(m!)2(−1)m​(2x​)2m:

​am​am+1​​​=4(m+1)2x2​→0 as m→∞

So R=∞ — J0​ converges everywhere. This is expected: Bessel’s equation has its only singular point at x=0, and J0​ is the solution regular at x=0.

§ 7 — Worked Example: Full Frobenius Solution

Euler’s Equation as a Test Case

x^2 y'' + \alpha xy' + \beta y = 0 \tag{7.1}

x=0 is a regular singular point. xP=α, x2Q=β, so p0​=α, q0​=β.

Indicial equation: r(r−1)+αr+β=0.

With the Frobenius ansatz y=xr∑cn​xn:

Substituting, the n-th term gives: [(r+n)(r+n−1)+α(r+n)+β],cn​=0

For n≥1: the bracket is (r+n)(r+n−1)+α(r+n)+β. This is the indicial polynomial evaluated at r+n — and since r is a root of the indicial equation, r+n (for n≥1) need not be a root, so the bracket =0, forcing cn​=0 for all n≥1.

The Frobenius series collapses to a single term: y=c0​xr.

This recovers the known result: Euler’s equation always has solutions y=xr. The Frobenius method is consistent. ■

§ 8 — Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

8.1 Hermite Polynomials and Probabilistic ML

The Hermite polynomials Hn​(x) from §3.4 are orthogonal with respect to the Gaussian weight e−x2: ∫−∞∞​Hm​(x)Hn​(x)e−x2,dx=π​,2nn!,δmn​

In probabilistic machine learning:

  • Gaussian processes: the covariance functions of stationary GPs can be expanded in Hermite polynomials — this gives the connection between GP kernels and frequency-domain representations
  • Score-based generative models (diffusion models, DDPM): the score function ∇x​logpt​(x) for a Gaussian prior satisfies a Hermite-polynomial expansion
  • Gauss-Hermite quadrature: numerical integration of expectations under Gaussian distributions (used in variational inference, Laplace approximation) is based on the roots of Hn​(x)

The quantum harmonic oscillator wavefunctions ψn​(x)=Hn​(x)e−x2/2 are precisely the basis functions that diagonalize the quantum number operator — analogous to how Fourier modes diagonalize translation-invariant operators, and how eigenvectors diagonalize weight matrices in linear neural networks.

8.2 Bessel Functions and Graph Neural Networks

Bessel functions appear naturally on graphs and manifolds. The graph Laplacian ΔG​ has eigenfunctions that, on regular lattices, reduce to discrete Fourier modes. On radially symmetric graphs (like ego networks), the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian are discrete analogues of Bessel functions.

Spectral Graph Convolutional Networks (Kipf & Welling, 2017) use the eigenfunctions of the graph Laplacian as the basis for convolution. The “spectral” filters are essentially polynomial approximations to Bessel-type functions. The ChebNet approximation uses Chebyshev polynomials (themselves solutions to a Sturm-Liouville ODE) to avoid explicit eigendecomposition.

8.3 Legendre Polynomials and Positional Encoding

The HiPPO framework (Gu et al., 2020) for long-range sequence modeling — which underlies the S4 and Mamba architectures — computes online approximations to signal history using Legendre polynomial projections:

u(t)≈∑n=0N−1​cn​(t)Pn​(τ)

where cn​(t) satisfies a linear ODE that can be updated in O(N) per step. The optimal A matrix in state-space models comes directly from the ODE recurrence (4.6) for Legendre polynomials.

This is series solutions of ODEs embedded into the architecture of a language model.

8.4 The Neural ODE as a Series Solution

A Neural ODE h˙=f(h,t,θ) with small t can be expanded via the Picard iteration / Magnus expansion:

h(t)=h(0)+tf(h(0),0)+2t2​(∂t∂f​+f⋅∇h​f)+⋯

This is a Taylor series in t — exactly the power series method applied to a nonlinear ODE. The truncated Magnus expansion gives the ResNet (each term = one residual block). A full series solution = infinitely many layers = continuous depth.

8.5 Special Functions as Activation Functions

The standard neural network activation functions are non-analytic at most points. But polynomial network architectures use:

  • Hermite activations: σ(x)=Hn​(x)e−x2/2 — guaranteed orthogonal feature maps under Gaussian input distributions. This directly minimizes feature correlation in layers receiving Gaussian-distributed inputs.
  • Legendre activations on bounded domains: σ(x)=Pn​(x) for x∈[−1,1] — used in FNO (Fourier Neural Operator) and related PDE-solving architectures.

The recurrence relations for these polynomials (e.g., (n+1)Pn+1​=(2n+1)xPn​−nPn−1​) allow efficient computation of all activation values up to order N in O(N) operations — making them computationally competitive with ReLU networks.

8.6 Airy Functions and Phase Transitions in Learning

The Airy function Ai(x) describes the edge of a semicircle law in random matrix theory. The empirical spectral distribution of a random weight matrix W/n​ converges to the Marchenko-Pastur law, and fluctuations at the spectral edge are governed by the Tracy-Widom distribution — whose density involves Ai(x).

This matters for deep learning initialization theory: the distribution of singular values of weight matrices at initialization, and how these evolve during training, follows random matrix universality. The Airy-governed edge controls how information propagates through very deep networks — too close to the edge and gradients explode; too far and they vanish.
