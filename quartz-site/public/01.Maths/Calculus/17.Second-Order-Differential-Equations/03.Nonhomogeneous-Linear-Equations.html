03.Nonhomogeneous Linear Equations

Apr 23, 202624 min read

“The homogeneous equation describes the soul of the system. The nonhomogeneous equation describes what happens when the world pushes back.”

§ 0 — The Setup and the Question

We have spent considerable effort understanding the homogeneous equation L[y]=0. Now we turn on an external force. The nonhomogeneous second order linear ODE is:

y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x)​

where g(x)≡0 is the forcing function (also called the nonhomogeneous term, the input, or the driving term).

The fundamental question: Given that we understand L[y]=0 completely, how do we find all solutions to L[y]=g?

The answer has a breathtaking algebraic structure — one that appears in linear algebra, functional analysis, and throughout applied mathematics. Understanding it deeply means you will never need to memorize a method; you will derive every method from first principles.

§ 1 — The Structure Theorem

This is the most important theorem of the subject. Everything else is a consequence.

1.1 Statement

Theorem (Structure of Solutions). Let p,q,g be continuous on an open interval I. Let y1​,y2​ be a fundamental set of solutions of the homogeneous equation L[y]=0, and let yp​ be any particular solution of L[y]=g. Then every solution of L[y]=g on I has the form:

y=yh​ — homogeneous general solutionc1​y1​+c2​y2​​​+particular solutionyp​​​

for some constants c1​,c2​∈R.

1.2 Proof

Part 1 — yh​+yp​ solves L[y]=g:

L[yh​+yp​]=L[yh​]+L[yp​]=0+g=g✓

Part 2 — Every solution has this form:

Let y be any solution of L[y]=g. Consider z=y−yp​. Then: L[z]=L[y]−L[yp​]=g−g=0

So z solves the homogeneous equation. Since y1​,y2​ is a fundamental set, z=c1​y1​+c2​y2​ for some constants. Therefore: y=yp​+z=yp​+c1​y1​+c2​y2​■

1.3 The Linear Algebra Interpretation

In linear algebra, if Ax=b has a solution xp​, then the general solution is:

x=xp​+xnull​

where xnull​∈ker(A). This is exactly the structure theorem above:

  • A↔L (the linear operator)
  • xp​↔yp​ (particular solution)
  • ker(A)↔spany1​,y2​ (null space, 2-dimensional)
  • b↔g (the forcing)

The solution set of L[y]=g is a coset of the solution space of L[y]=0 — an affine subspace, not a vector space (it does not contain 0 unless yp​=0).

The operational consequence: We need only find one particular solution. Any one will do — the constants c1​,c2​ absorb the difference between different choices. This is the license to be clever about how we find yp​.

§ 2 — Method I: Undetermined Coefficients

2.1 The Idea

Valid for constant coefficient equations ay′′+by′+cy=g(x) when g(x) belongs to a special class of functions closed under differentiation: polynomials, exponentials, sines, cosines, and their products.

The principle: L[yp​] must equal g(x). If g is a sum of exponentials times polynomials times trig, then yp​ should live in the same “family” — because differentiating such functions stays within the family. We guess the form, substitute, and solve for the unknown coefficients.

2.2 The Trial Function Table

For constant coefficient L=aD2+bD+c and the given g(x), the trial yp​ is:

The multiplicity rule for s:

  • s=0 if no term in the trial is a solution of L[y]=0.
  • s=1 if some term in the trial is a solution of L[y]=0 (simple resonance).
  • s=2 if some term in the trial is a solution corresponding to a double root (double resonance).

Why does the multiplicity rule exist? If the trial yp​ satisfies the homogeneous equation, then L[yp​]=0=g(x) — the substitution gives 0=g and no coefficient can be determined. Multiplying by xs (with s chosen minimally) moves the trial out of the kernel of L, making it possible to match g.

Problem: y′′+9y=2sin(3x).

Physical meaning of resonance: The forcing sin(3x) is at exactly the natural frequency of the oscillator (ω0​=3). The system has no way to “absorb” the energy — each cycle, the driving force is perfectly in phase with the response, pumping in more energy. The amplitude grows without bound as 3x​cos3x. In a physical system with no damping, this is catastrophic. Resonance is why bridges need careful engineering and why MRI machines have such tight frequency tolerances.

§ 3 — Method II: Variation of Parameters

3.1 Motivation

Undetermined coefficients is fast but has two crippling restrictions:

  1. The equation must have constant coefficients.
  2. g(x) must belong to the “nice” family (polynomials × exponentials × trig).

Variation of parameters works for any g(x) and any equation where we can find a fundamental set — including variable-coefficient equations.

The price: we must compute two integrals, which may not have closed forms.

3.2 Derivation from First Principles

Given the fundamental set y1​,y2​ of L[y]=0, promote the constants to functions:

yp​=u1​(x)y1​(x)+u2​(x)y2​(x)

We have two unknowns u1​,u2​ and one equation L[yp​]=g. We impose a constraint to simplify — specifically, to eliminate second derivatives of u1​,u2​:

\textbf{Constraint: } u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2 = 0 \tag{C}

Under this constraint, differentiate yp​:

yp′​=u1′​y1​+u1​y1′​+u2′​y2​+u2​y2′​=u1​y1′​+u2​y2′​(using C)

yp′′​=u1′​y1′​+u1​y1′′​+u2′​y2′​+u2​y2′′​

Substitute into yp′′​+pyp′​+qyp​=g:

u1′​y1′​+u1​y1′′​+u2′​y2′​+u2​y2′′​+p(u1​y1′​+u2​y2′​)+q(u1​y1​+u2​y2​)=g

u1′​y1′​+u2′​y2′​+u1​(y1′′​+py1′​+qy1​)+u2​(y2′′​+py2′​+qy2​)=g

Since y1​,y2​ solve L[y]=0, the bracketed terms vanish:

\textbf{ODE condition: } u_1'y_1' + u_2'y_2' = g \tag{ODE}

3.3 The System and Its Solution

Equations (C) and (ODE) form a 2×2 linear system for u1′​ and u2′​:

(y1​​y2​ y1′​​y2′​​)(u1′​ u2′​​)=(0 g​)

The coefficient matrix has determinant W(x)=y1​y2′​−y1′​y2​=0. By Cramer’s rule:

u1′​=−Wy2​g​,u2′​=Wy1​g​

Integrating:

u1​(x)=−∫W(x)y2​(x),g(x)​,dx,u2​(x)=∫W(x)y1​(x),g(x)​,dx

The particular solution:

yp​(x)=−y1​(x)∫W(x)y2​(x),g(x)​,dx+y2​(x)∫W(x)y1​(x),g(x)​,dx​

3.4 The Formula as a Green’s Function

Combining the integrals:

yp​(x)=∫x0​x​W(t)y1​(t)y2​(x)−y1​(x)y2​(t)​,g(t),dt=∫x0​x​G(x,t),g(t),dt

where G(x,t)=W(t)y1​(t)y2​(x)−y1​(x)y2​(t)​ is the Green’s function of the operator L (with initial conditions). The particular solution is the convolution of the Green’s function with the forcing — every forcing value g(t) is weighted by how much influence it has at position x, given by G(x,t).

Problem: y′′+y=1+x21​.

Homogeneous: y1​=cosx, y2​=sinx, W=1.

u1′​=−1+x2sinx​,u2′​=1+x2cosx​

yp​=−cosx∫0x​1+t2sint​,dt+sinx∫0x​1+t2cost​,dt

This is a perfectly valid, well-defined function — it just cannot be written in closed form. Numerically, it is computed as easily as any integral. This is why variation of parameters is the theoretically complete method: it always produces the answer, even when the answer is not “nice.”

§ 4 — The Operator Method and Exponential Shift

4.1 The Annihilator Method

For constant coefficient equations, there is an elegant operator-theoretic method that systematizes undetermined coefficients.

The differential operator D=d/dx acts on functions. The ODE L[y]=ay′′+by′+cy=g is written as:

L(D)y=(aD2+bD+c)y=g

For the trial to work, we need L(D)[yp​]=g. If we can find a differential operator A(D) — the annihilator — such that A(D)[g]=0, then applying A(D) to both sides:

A(D)L(D)[y]=0

This is now a homogeneous equation with characteristic polynomial A(r)L(r)=0. Its general solution contains yh​ (from L(r)=0) plus extra terms from A(r)=0. These extra terms form the trial yp​ — we substitute back into the original equation to find the coefficients.

Example: y′′+4y=xcos2x.

Annihilator of xcos2x: (D2+4)2 (since e0⋅xcos2x has annihilator (D2+4), and the extra x requires squaring).

Apply (D2+4)2 to both sides: (D2+4)3y=0.

Characteristic roots of (r2+4)3=0: r=±2i (each with multiplicity 3).

General solution of (D2+4)3y=0: y=(c1​+c2​x+c3​x2)cos2x+(c4​+c5​x+c6​x2)sin2x

The homogeneous part of the original (from (D2+4)y=0): c1​cos2x+c4​sin2x. The particular trial is the remaining part: (c2​x+c3​x2)cos2x+(c5​x+c6​x2)sin2x. Substitute into the original ODE to find the four free coefficients.

4.2 The Exponential Shift Theorem

Theorem. For any polynomial p(D) with constant coefficients: p(D)[eαxf(x)]=eαxp(D+α)[f(x)]

Proof. By direct computation using the product rule and the fact that D[eαx]=αeαx: D[eαxf]=eαx(αf+f′)=eαx(D+α)[f]

Iterating: Dn[eαxf]=eαx(D+α)n[f]. Linearity extends to any polynomial. ■

Application: To find yp​ for p(D)[y]=eαxg(x), write yp​=eαxv(x):

eαxp(D+α)[v]=eαxg(x)⟹p(D+α)[v]=g(x)

This replaces the original operator with a shifted operator — often simpler.

Example: (D−2)2y=e2xsinx.

Try yp​=e2xv. Then (D−2)2[e2xv]=e2xD2[v]=e2xsinx.

So v′′=sinx⇒v=−sinx. Thus yp​=−e2xsinx.

Check: (D−2)2[−e2xsinx]=e2xD2[−sinx]=e2xsinx. ✓

§ 5 — Superposition for Multiple Forcing Terms

A direct consequence of linearity:

Principle of Superposition. If ypk​​ is a particular solution of L[y]=gk​(x) for k=1,2,…,n, then yp​=∑k=1n​ck​ypk​​ is a particular solution of L[y]=∑k=1n​ck​gk​(x).

Proof: L[∑ck​ypk​​]=∑ck​L[ypk​​]=∑ck​gk​. ■

This is why, in Example 6, we found three separate yp​‘s and added them. It is also why Fourier series is so powerful for PDEs: we can solve for each Fourier component separately, then superpose.

§ 7 — The Full Solution Pipeline

For any nonhomogeneous linear second order ODE:

Step 1: Write in standard form y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = g(x)

Step 2: Solve L[y] = 0
        → Find characteristic roots (constant coeff)
        → Or use reduction of order, series, etc.
        → Get fundamental set {y₁, y₂}

Step 3: Find one particular solution yₚ
        → Undetermined coefficients (if applicable)
        → Variation of parameters (always works)

Step 4: Write y = c₁y₁ + c₂y₂ + yₚ

Step 5: If IVP/BVP given, apply conditions to find c₁, c₂

§ 9 — Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

9.1 The Homogeneous/Particular Split in Representation Learning

The structure theorem says every solution is y=yh​+yp​:

  • yh​: lives in the null space of L — determined by initial conditions, independent of the forcing.
  • yp​: the particular response to the forcing — depends entirely on g, not on initial conditions.

In deep learning, this decomposition appears in the study of neural network representations:

  • The null space of the linear map at each layer corresponds to directions in weight space that do not affect the output. Training ignores these directions — they are the “homogeneous” part.
  • The particular solution corresponds to the minimum-norm solution found by gradient descent when the loss is underdetermined. The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse gives this minimum-norm solution — the direct linear algebra analogue of variation of parameters.

9.2 Undetermined Coefficients and Basis Functions in ML

The idea of undetermined coefficients — guessing a function form and solving for coefficients — is the philosophy behind:

Basis function regression: Model f(x)=∑k=1n​wk​ϕk​(x) where ϕk​ are fixed basis functions (polynomials, RBFs, Fourier modes). Fitting wk​ to data is undetermined coefficients for the statistical operator L[f]=f−data.

Kernel methods (SVMs, Gaussian Processes): The kernel is a Green’s function. The GP prediction μ(x)=∫K(x,x′)α(x′),dx′ is variation of parameters — the forcing g=α(x′) (the support vectors or posterior weights) is convolved with the Green’s function K(x,x′) of the kernel operator.

Neural tangent kernel (NTK): In the infinite-width limit, training a neural network is governed by the NTK, and the solution to the training dynamics is:

f(t)=f(∞)+e−Θt(f(0)−f(∞))

This is exactly the structure theorem: f(∞) is the particular solution (the target), and e−Θt(f(0)−f(∞)) is the homogeneous part (decaying transient from initial conditions). Training is convergence of the transient to zero.

9.3 Forcing and Fine-Tuning: Transfer Learning as Nonhomogeneous ODE

Pretrain a model on a large dataset (find yh​ — the homogeneous solution, the “soul” of the model). Fine-tune on a small task-specific dataset (add yp​ — the particular solution, the task-specific response to the “forcing” of the new data).

The total model =yh​+yp​ (pretrained knowledge + task adaptation). The structure theorem says: yp​ is not unique — any particular solution works, and the constants in yh​ absorb the difference. In practice, different fine-tuning methods (LoRA, adapters, full fine-tuning) correspond to different choices of yp​. LoRA, in particular, restricts yp​ to be low-rank — a structured ansatz, analogous to undetermined coefficients (guess a restricted form, solve for coefficients).

9.4 Resonance and Adversarial Examples

Resonance in the ODE occurs when the forcing frequency matches a natural frequency of the homogeneous equation — the response grows without bound.

The analogue in neural networks is adversarial examples: inputs that are precisely tuned to match the “natural frequencies” of the network’s decision boundary, producing catastrophically wrong outputs with imperceptible input changes. Just as resonance is not a bug in the ODE (it is mathematically correct behavior) but is catastrophic physically, adversarial sensitivity is a mathematically correct consequence of the network’s geometry, but practically disastrous.

More formally: the Hessian of the loss ∇2L has eigenvectors — directions of sharp curvature. Perturbing inputs in these directions (the “resonant modes”) causes the largest change in the output. Adversarial attacks are, in this sense, forcing at the resonant frequency of the network operator.

9.5 Variation of Parameters and Attention

Variation of parameters computes:

yp​(x)=∫G(x,t)g(t),dt

The Green’s function G(x,t) weights the forcing at t by its influence at x. Self-attention computes:

Outputi​=∑j​Aij​Vj​

where Aij​=softmax(Qi​⋅KjT​/d​) weights the values Vj​ by the relevance of position j to position i. This is a discrete, learned variation of parameters: Aij​ is a data-dependent Green’s function, Vj​ is the “forcing” at position j, and the output is the “particular solution” at position i.

The key difference: in classical VoP, G(x,t) is determined by the differential operator. In attention, Aij​ is learned from data — the network discovers its own Green’s function.

9.6 Superposition and Ensemble Methods

The principle of superposition — L[y]=∑gk​⇒yp​=∑ypk​​ — is the theoretical basis of ensemble methods in machine learning:

  • Gradient boosting: Sequentially solve for ypk​​ where each gk​ is the residual from the previous model. The total model ∑ypk​​ is the superposition of particular solutions to residual forcing terms.
  • Mixture of Experts (MoE): Each expert handles a “component” of the forcing function (the input distribution). The gating network superimposes expert outputs — a learned superposition of particular solutions.
  • Random forests: Each tree is a particular solution to a noisy version of the forcing (bootstrap sample). The ensemble average superimposes, reducing variance while maintaining consistency.

9.7 Steady State, Transients, and Training Dynamics

The ODE decomposition y=yh​+yp​ with yh​ decaying (transient) and yp​ persisting (steady state) is mirrored in neural network training:

  • Early training (transient phase): The network is far from the optimum. The homogeneous component (the response to initial conditions — random initialization) dominates. The loss decreases rapidly.
  • Late training (steady state): The transient has decayed. The network is near yp​ — the particular solution corresponding to the data. The loss plateaus. Further training does not help.

Learning rate warmup delays the forcing ramp-up, letting the optimizer “find its feet” — analogous to turning on the forcing g(x) gradually to avoid resonant transients. Cosine annealing mirrors the smooth approach to steady state of a damped forced oscillator.

§ 10 — Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

MistakeWhat goes wrongFix
Not checking for resonance in undetermined coefficientsSubstituting a homogeneous solution as trial gives 0=g; coefficients are undeterminedCheck each component of the trial against every homogeneous solution; multiply by xs
Forgetting the constraint u1′​y1​+u2′​y2​=0 in VoP derivationWithout the constraint, the system is underdetermined and the algebra doesn’t simplifyThe constraint is a choice — it is the price for having only first derivatives of u1​,u2​ in the equations
Using the non-standard form in VoPVoP formula assumes the leading coefficient is 1Always divide by P(x) first; g(x) in the formula is g/P, not the original right-hand side
Adding particular solutions that differ by a homogeneous solutionThis gives a “wrong” looking yp​ but is actually fineAny particular solution works; the constants c1​,c2​ absorb the difference
Applying undetermined coefficients to variable-coefficient equationsThe method fails — differentiating p(x)y introduces new functions that cannot be matchedUse variation of parameters or reduce to a solvable form
Thinking the particular solution must satisfy any boundary/initial conditionyp​ is just one solution; c1​,c2​ are determined by the conditionsApply BCs/ICs to the full solution yh​+yp​, not to yp​ alone

§ 11 — Summary

The Master Formula

y=yh​c1​y1​+c2​y2​​​+particularyp​​​​

where y1​,y2​ is the fundamental set of L[y]=0 and yp​ is any solution of L[y]=g.

Finding yp​: Decision Tree

Is the equation constant-coefficient?
│
├─ YES: Is g(x) a polynomial × exponential × trig product?
│       │

│       │         → Check for resonance → multiply by xˢ
