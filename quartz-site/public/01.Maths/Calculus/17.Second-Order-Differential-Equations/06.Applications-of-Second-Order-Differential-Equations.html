06.Applications of Second Order Differential Equations

06.Applications of Second-Order Differential Equations

Apr 23, 202617 min read

“The same equation governs the spring in your mattress, the charge in your phone, and the gradient descent oscillations in a neural network. Mathematics does not care about the physical costume — only the underlying structure.”

§ 0 — Personal Preamble

Second-order linear ODEs with constant coefficients:

ay'' + by' + cy = g(t) \tag{★}

are not merely a mathematical exercise. They are the universal language of oscillating systems — mechanical, electrical, biological, financial, and computational. The four applications in this note — free vibration, damped vibration, forced vibration, and RLC circuits — are all instances of (★). The parameters a,b,c change interpretation (mass, resistance, capacitance), but the mathematics is identical.

This note builds each application from physical first principles, derives the ODE rigorously, and solves it completely. Every phenomenon — resonance, critical damping, beats — is a mathematical theorem wearing a physical disguise.

References:

  • Stewart, James. Calculus, 8th ed. — §17.3–17.4
  • MIT OCW 18.02 — ODE and Applications notes

§ 1 — The Undamped Vibrating Spring (Simple Harmonic Motion)

1.1 Physical Setup

A mass m (kg) hangs from a spring of natural length L with spring constant k (N/m). We measure displacement x(t) from the equilibrium position (where the spring force exactly balances gravity).

   __|__
  |     |   ← spring (constant k)
  |_____|
     |
    [m]     ← mass
     |
   x(t)     ← displacement from equilibrium (positive downward)

1.2 Derivation from Newton’s Second Law

At equilibrium, the spring is stretched by δ where kδ=mg.

When displaced by x(t) from equilibrium, the net forces on m are:

  • Gravity: +mg (downward)
  • Spring restoring force: −k(δ+x) (upward; spring is further stretched by x)

Newton’s second law: mx¨=mg−kδ−kx=−kx (since kδ=mg cancels).

\boxed{m\ddot{x} + kx = 0} \tag{1.1}

Dividing by m, let ω0​=k/m​ (natural angular frequency, rad/s):

\ddot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \tag{1.2}

1.3 Solution

Characteristic equation: r2+ω02​=0⇒r=±iω0​.

x(t) = C_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + C_2\sin(\omega_0 t) \tag{1.3}

Amplitude-phase form. Let A=C12​+C22​​ and ϕ=arctan(C2​/C1​):

\boxed{x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t - \phi)} \tag{1.4}

where:

  • A = amplitude (m)
  • ω0​=k/m​ = natural frequency (rad/s)
  • T=2π/ω0​ = period (s)
  • ϕ = phase shift (rad), determined by initial conditions

Key fact: T=2πm/k​ is independent of amplitude A. This isochrony (Galileo, 1602) is the foundation of mechanical timekeeping.

1.4 Example

A 0.5 kg mass stretches a spring 0.1 m at equilibrium.

k=mg/δ=(0.5)(9.8)/0.1=49 N/m.
ω0​=49/0.5​=98​≈9.9 rad/s.
T=2π/9.9≈0.635 s.

With initial conditions x(0)=0.2 m (pulled down 20 cm), x˙(0)=0: x(0)=C1​=0.2,x˙(0)=ω0​C2​=0⇒C2​=0 x(t)=0.2cos(9.9t) m

1.5 Visualization

    |   *   *       *   *

No energy loss. Perpetual oscillation.
Amplitude A = constant for all t.

§ 2 — Damped Vibrations

2.1 Physical Setup

Now introduce a damping force (dashpot/shock absorber) proportional to velocity:

Fdamping​=−cx˙,c>0 (damping constant, N\cdotps/m)

The minus sign: damping opposes motion.

2.2 The ODE

Newton’s second law with spring + damping:

mx¨=−kx−cx˙

\boxed{m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0} \tag{2.1}

Dividing by m, define:

  • 2γ=c/m (damping coefficient, s⁻¹)
  • ω02​=k/m (natural frequency squared)

\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \tag{2.2}

Characteristic equation: r2+2γr+ω02​=0

r = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} \tag{2.3}

The discriminant Δ=γ2−ω02​ determines the regime.

2.3 Case I — Underdamped: γ<ω0​ (Δ<0)

Define ω1​=ω02​−γ2​ (damped natural frequency).

Roots: r=−γ±iω1​ (complex conjugate pair).

\boxed{x(t) = e^{-\gamma t}(C_1\cos(\omega_1 t) + C_2\sin(\omega_1 t)) = Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_1 t - \phi)} \tag{2.4}

Interpretation: Oscillation with exponentially decaying envelope Ae−γt. The system oscillates but energy leaks away.

x(t) — Underdamped

  A |   *
    |  * *          Ae^{-γt} envelope (dashed)
    | *   *       ·
    |*     *   · 
  0 |-------*·*--------*·------→ t
    |          ·  *   *  ·
    |           · * * ·
    |            ·*·
    |             ↑
    |         exponential decay
    
    Period T₁ = 2π/ω₁ > T₀ = 2π/ω₀
    (damping slows the oscillation)

2.4 Case II — Critically Damped: γ=ω0​ (Δ=0)

Repeated root: r=−γ (multiplicity 2).

\boxed{x(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t)e^{-\gamma t}} \tag{2.5}

Interpretation: No oscillation. Returns to equilibrium as fast as possible without overshooting. This is the design goal for car suspensions and door closers.

x(t) — Three regimes compared

  A |·
    | ·    (A) Underdamped: oscillates, decays slowly
    |  ·

    |    ·
    |     ·  ·  ·  (C) Overdamped: slow return, no oscillation
    |      · ·· ···················
  0 |────────────────────────────────→ t
    
    (A) wiggles under 0
    (B) touches 0 exactly once
    (C) approaches 0 from above, very slowly

2.5 Case III — Overdamped: γ>ω0​ (Δ>0)

Two distinct real negative roots: r1,2​=−γ±γ2−ω02​​<0.

\boxed{x(t) = C_1 e^{r_1 t} + C_2 e^{r_2 t}} \tag{2.6}

Interpretation: Returns to equilibrium without oscillating, but slower than critical damping. Think: a door closer set too stiff.

2.6 The Damping Ratio ζ

Engineers parametrize this with the damping ratio:

ζ=ω0​γ​=2mk​c​

ζRegimeBehavior
0UndampedPerpetual oscillation
0<ζ<1UnderdampedDecaying oscillation
ζ=1Critically dampedFastest return, no overshoot
ζ>1OverdampedSluggish return, no oscillation

2.7 Example

m=1 kg, k=25 N/m (ω0​=5 rad/s), c=6 N·s/m (γ=3).

ζ=3/5=0.6<1 → underdamped.

ω1​=25−9​=4 rad/s.

With x(0)=1, x˙(0)=0:

C1​=1. x˙(0)=−γC1​+ω1​C2​=0⇒C2​=γ/ω1​=3/4.

x(t)=e−3t!(cos4t+43​sin4t)=45​e−3tcos(4t−ϕ)

where ϕ=arctan(3/4)≈36.87°.

§ 3 — Forced Vibrations

3.1 Physical Setup

Now apply an external periodic force to the mass:

Fext​(t)=F0​cos(ωt)

where F0​ = forcing amplitude (N) and ω = forcing frequency (rad/s).

3.2 The ODE

m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = F_0\cos(\omega t) \tag{3.1}

\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega t) \tag{3.2}

The general solution splits: x(t)=xc​(t)+xp​(t) where xc​ (the complementary/transient solution from §2) decays to zero, and xp​ (the particular/steady-state solution) persists.

3.3 Steady-State Solution (Underdamped Case, γ>0)

Trial: xp​=Acos(ωt)+Bsin(ωt).

After substitution and matching coefficients:

xp​(t)=(ω02​−ω2)2+4γ2ω2F0​/m​[(ω02​−ω2)cos(ωt)+2γωsin(ωt)]

In amplitude-phase form, define the steady-state amplitude:

\boxed{G(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2}}} \tag{3.3}

So xp​(t)=G(ω)cos(ωt−δ) where tanδ=ω02​−ω22γω​.

3.4 Resonance: The Phenomenon and its Mathematics

Definition. The system is in resonance when G(ω) is maximized over ω.

Differentiate G(ω)−2 with respect to ω2 and set to zero:

\omega_{\text{res}} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2} \tag{3.4}

The resonant amplitude is:

G(\omega_{\text{res}}) = \frac{F_0/m}{2\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}} \tag{3.5}

Critical observation: As γ→0, G(ωres​)→∞. In an undamped system forced at exactly ω=ω0​, the amplitude grows without bound — the system tears itself apart. This is the Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940).

      |   |

3.6 The Undamped Resonance Case: Beats and Pure Resonance

Case A: ω=ω0​, γ=0 (Beats)

x¨+ω02​x=mF0​​cos(ωt)

Particular solution (by undetermined coefficients, no resonance):

xp​=ω02​−ω2F0​/m​cos(ωt)

With x(0)=x˙(0)=0:

x(t)=ω02​−ω2F0​/m​(cos(ωt)−cos(ω0​t))

Using sum-to-product:

\boxed{x(t) = \frac{2F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 - \omega}{2}t\right)\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}t\right)} \tag{3.6}

This is a high-frequency oscillation sin!(2ω0​+ω​t) modulated by a slow amplitude envelope sin!(2ω0​−ω​t). The result is beats — the periodic waxing and waning of amplitude heard when two tuning forks of nearly equal frequency are struck simultaneously.

x(t) — Beats (ω close to ω₀)

    Fast oscillation at (ω₀+ω)/2
    Slow envelope at (ω₀-ω)/2

Case B: ω=ω0​, γ=0 (Pure Resonance)

The trial xp​=Acos(ω0​t) overlaps with xc​ → modification rule: xp​=t(Acos(ω0​t)+Bsin(ω0​t)).

Substituting:

\boxed{x_p = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}t\sin(\omega_0 t)} \tag{3.7}

The amplitude grows linearly: 2mω0​F0​​t. The system is not periodic — it is catastrophically unstable.

x(t) — Pure Resonance (ω = ω₀, no damping)

3.7 Example: Forced Underdamped Spring

m=1 kg, k=9 N/m (ω0​=3), c=2 N·s/m (γ=1), forced at ω=2 rad/s with F0​=10 N.

Steady-state amplitude: G(2)=(9−4)2+4(1)2(4)​10​=25+16​10​=41​10​≈1.56 m

Phase lag: tanδ=9−42(1)(2)​=54​, so δ≈38.66°.

The transient xc​=e−t(C1​cos(22​t)+C2​sin(22​t)) decays and after several time constants, only xp​ remains.

§ 4 — Electric Circuits (RLC Circuits)

4.1 The Physical Components

An RLC series circuit consists of:

  • Resistor R (ohms, Ω) — dissipates energy (analogous to damping)
  • Inductor L (henries, H) — stores energy in magnetic field (analogous to mass)
  • Capacitor C (farads, F) — stores energy in electric field (analogous to spring)
  • EMF source E(t) (volts) — external forcing
       R           L
  ┌───/\/\/───────UUUU───────┐
  │                          │
 E(t)                       ═╪═  C
  │                          │
  └──────────────────────────┘
  
  Charge on capacitor: Q(t)
  Current: I = dQ/dt

4.2 Derivation via Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law

The voltage drops across each element:

  • Resistor: VR​=RI (Ohm’s law)
  • Inductor: VL​=LdtdI​=LQ¨​
  • Capacitor: VC​=Q/C

Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL): the sum of voltage drops equals the EMF:

L\ddot{Q} + R\dot{Q} + \frac{1}{C}Q = E(t) \tag{4.1}

This is identical in form to equation (3.1). The analogy is exact:

Mass mInductance L
Damping cResistance R
Spring constant k1/C (reciprocal capacitance)
Displacement xCharge Q
Velocity x˙Current I=Q˙​
External force F(t)EMF E(t)
Natural frequency k/m​1/LC​
Damping ratio c/2mk​R/2L/C​=R/(2Z0​)

4.3 Characteristic Impedance and Q-Factor

Define:

  • Resonant frequency: ω0​=1/LC​
  • Characteristic impedance: Z0​=L/C​ (ohms)
  • Quality factor: Qfactor​=Z0​/R=R1​L/C​

Qfactor​ is the electrical analogue of 1/(2ζ) in mechanics. High Q → sharp resonance → low damping.

4.4 Free Oscillations (E=0)

LQ¨​+RQ˙​+CQ​=0

Three cases mirror §2:

Underdamped (R<2L/C​): Q(t)=e−Rt/2L(Acos(ω1​t)+Bsin(ω1​t)) where ω1​=1/LC−R2/4L2​.

Circuit oscillates — energy sloshes between inductor (21​LI2) and capacitor (Q2/2C) while resistor dissipates it.

Critically damped (R=2L/C​): Q(t)=(A+Bt)e−Rt/2L

Overdamped (R>2L/C​): Q(t)=Aer1​t+Ber2​t

4.5 Forced Oscillations: AC Circuit

Let E(t)=E0​cos(ωt) (AC source). The steady-state charge and current:

Qp​(t)=(1/C−Lω2)2+R2ω2​E0​​cos(ωt−δ)

The steady-state current Ip​=Q˙​p​ has amplitude:

I_0 = \frac{E_0\omega}{\sqrt{(1/C - L\omega^2)^2 + R^2\omega^2}} = \frac{E_0}{Z(\omega)} \tag{4.2}

where Z(ω)=R2+(Lω−1/Cω)2​ is the impedance of the circuit (the electrical analogue of steady-state amplitude denominator in §3.3).

Resonance occurs at ω=ω0​=1/LC​: Z(ω0​)=R(minimum impedance)⇒I0​=E0​/R(maximum current)

The reactive terms Lω and 1/(Cω) cancel at resonance — the circuit is purely resistive.

4.6 Example

L=1 H, R=20 Ω, C=0.002 F (2 mF), E(t)=100cos(10t) V.

ω0​=1/1×0.002​=500​≈22.4 rad/s.

Forcing ω=10 rad/s ≠ ω0​.

Homogeneous: r=2−20±400−4/0.002​​=2−20±400−2000​​.

Δ=400−2000=−1600<0: underdamped.

r=−10±20i.

Qc​=e−10t(Acos(20t)+Bsin(20t)) — transient, decays with time constant τ=1/10=0.1 s.

Steady-state: Qp​(t)=Acos(10t)+Bsin(10t).

Impedance at ω=10: Z=400+(10−50)2​=400+1600​=2000​≈44.7 Ω.

I0​=E0​/Z=100/44.7≈2.24 A.

Phase: tanδ=(Lω−1/Cω)/R=(10−50)/20=−2, so δ≈−63.4° (current leads voltage since 1/Cω>Lω).

§ 6 — Energy Analysis

6.1 Spring Energy

The total mechanical energy is:

E(t) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2}_{\text{kinetic}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}kx^2}_{\text{potential}} \tag{6.1}

For undamped free vibration: dtdE​=mx˙x¨+kxx˙=x˙(mx¨+kx)=x˙⋅0=0. Energy is conserved.

With damping: dtdE​=x˙(mx¨+kx)=x˙(−cx˙)=−cx˙2≤0. Energy dissipates at rate cx˙2.

6.2 Circuit Energy

E_{\text{circuit}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}LI^2}_{\text{inductor}} + \underbrace{\frac{Q^2}{2C}}_{\text{capacitor}} \tag{6.2}

Rate of energy dissipation: −RI2 (Joule heating). This is the direct electrical analogue of −cx˙2.

§ 7 — Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

7.1 Oscillations in Gradient Descent (Momentum and Damping)

The momentum update in SGD with momentum:

vt+1​=βvt​−α∇L(θt​) θt+1​=θt​+vt+1​

can be written (near a minimum where ∇L≈Hθ, H = Hessian) as:

θt+1​−(1+β)θt​+βθt−1​=−αHθt​

This is a discretized second-order ODE:

θ¨+(1−β)θ˙+αHθ=0

Compare to the damped spring! The momentum parameter β plays the role of the damping coefficient c:

  • β=0: pure gradient descent (overdamped — slow)
  • β near 1: near-undamped — fast but may oscillate
  • Optimal β: analogous to critical damping — fastest convergence without oscillation

The learning rate α controls ω02​=αλ (where λ is an eigenvalue of H). Choosing α too large → resonance → divergence. Choosing α optimally → critical damping → fastest convergence.

7.2 Resonance and Loss Landscape Instability

The condition for gradient explosion in SDG mirrors resonance:

αλmax​>2(1+β)

(where λmax​ is the largest Hessian eigenvalue). This is the stability boundary — exactly the overdamped/underdamped phase boundary. Loss landscapes with sharp curvature (λmax​ large) → small learning rate required → slow training. This is why adaptive learning rates (Adam, RMSProp) are important: they effectively normalize by the curvature, maintaining consistent “damping” across parameter dimensions.

7.3 RNNs as Coupled Oscillators

A vanilla RNN: ht+1​=tanh(Wh​ht​+Wx​xt​).

Linearized near h=0: ht+1​≈Wh​ht​+Wx​xt​.

This is a system of first-order discrete ODEs — equivalent to a coupled system of second-order oscillators in the eigenspace of Wh​. Each eigenvalue λi​ of Wh​ controls:

  • ∣λi​∣<1: damped mode (information fades — vanishing gradients)
  • ∣λi​∣=1: undamped mode (information persists — LSTM/GRU are engineered to maintain this)
  • ∣λi​∣>1: resonant/growing mode (gradient explosion)

Orthogonal RNNs force ∣λi​∣=1 for all i — the electrical analogue of a lossless LC circuit.

7.4 Attention Resonance: Query-Key Alignment

In self-attention, the softmax of QKT/d​ produces large weights when query qi​ and key kj​ are aligned. This is amplitude amplification — the same mechanism as resonance in forced vibration. When qi​⋅kj​ is large (resonant match), the attention “rings” — that position dominates the output. Transformers tune this through temperature scaling (d​), analogous to adding damping to prevent single-position dominance.

7.5 Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Oscillators

To solve the spring equation with a PINN, the network uθ​(t) minimizes:

L(θ)=ODE residual∫0T​∣mu¨θ​+cu˙θ​+kuθ​−F(t)∣2,dt​​+IC residual∣uθ​(0)−x0​∣2+∣u˙θ​(0)−v0​∣2​​

The gradients u¨θ​ are computed by automatic differentiation through the network. The ODE residual enforces the physics; the IC residual pins the solution. Training converges to the true particular solution for any F(t), including forms that are analytically intractable.

Resonance near ω≈ω0​ creates stiff training dynamics — the PINN loss landscape itself becomes ill-conditioned, requiring careful learning rate scheduling. The physics of the problem bleeds into the mathematics of training.

7.6 Electrical Analogy for Neural Circuit Models

The Hodgkin-Huxley neuron model is a nonlinear RLC circuit:

Cm​V˙=−gNa​m3h(V−ENa​)−gK​n4(V−EK​)−gL​(V−EL​)+Iext​

The membrane capacitance Cm​ plays the role of capacitor C; the ion conductances play the role of resistors; the Nernst potentials are EMF sources. Action potentials are the nonlinear analogue of resonance: a suprathreshold stimulus (forcing) drives the system past a bifurcation into a large-amplitude oscillation.

Deep learning models of neural circuits (spiking neural networks, neural field theories) are direct descendants of this RLC analogy.
