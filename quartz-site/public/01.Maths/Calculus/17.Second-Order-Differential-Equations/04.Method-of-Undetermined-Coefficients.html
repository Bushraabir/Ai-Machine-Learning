04.Method of Undetermined Coefficients

04.Method of Undetermined Coefficients

Apr 23, 202611 min read

“The beauty of this method is not that it always works — it is that when it works, it works with remarkable elegance.”

§ 0 — Personal Preamble

This note lives at the intersection of structure and intuition. The method of undetermined coefficients is, at its heart, an educated guess made rigorous. We exploit the fact that certain families of functions are closed under differentiation — polynomials differentiate to polynomials, exponentials to exponentials, sinusoids to sinusoids. If the forcing function g(x) belongs to one of these families, we can guess the form of a particular solution, plug it in, and determine the coefficients algebraically.

It is not magic. It is pattern recognition elevated to theorem.

References consulted:

  • Stewart, James. Calculus, 8th ed. — §17.2
  • MIT OCW 18.02 Multivariable Calculus (Supplementary ODE notes)

§ 1 — The Setup: Second-Order Linear ODEs

We are working with equations of the form:

ay'' + by' + cy = g(x) \tag{1}

where a,b,c∈R, a=0, and g(x) is the forcing function (or nonhomogeneous term).

The General Solution Decomposition

Theorem (Structure of the General Solution). Let yc​ be the complementary solution — the general solution to the associated homogeneous equation ay′′+by′+cy=0 — and let yp​ be any particular solution to (1). Then every solution to (1) is of the form: y=yc​+yp​

Proof sketch. If y solves (1) and yp​ solves (1), then y−yp​ satisfies the homogeneous equation (by linearity of L=aD2+bD+c). Hence y−yp​=yc​ for some yc​∈ker(L). ■

The method of undetermined coefficients is a systematic procedure for finding yp​ when g(x) has a particular form.

§ 2 — The Admissible Forms of g(x)

The method applies when g(x) is a finite linear combination of functions from the following table:

Type of g(x)Trial Form yp​
Pn​(x) (polynomial of degree n)An​xn+An−1​xn−1+⋯+A0​
eαxAeαx
cos(βx) or sin(βx)Acos(βx)+Bsin(βx)
Pn​(x)eαx(An​xn+⋯+A0​)eαx
eαxcos(βx) or eαxsin(βx)eαx(Acos(βx)+Bsin(βx))
Products/sums of the aboveProducts/sums of corresponding trial forms

Why these families? Because dxd​ maps each family into itself. A polynomial differentiates to a polynomial. An exponential eαx differentiates to αeαx. A sinusoid differentiates to another sinusoid. This closure property guarantees that L[yp​] will have the same structural form as g(x), making coefficient matching possible.

§ 3 — The Algorithm

Step 1. Solve the homogeneous equation ay′′+by′+cy=0 to find yc​.

Step 2. Identify the form of g(x) and write down the trial particular solution yp​ with undetermined coefficients.

Step 3 (Modification Rule — Critical!). If any term in yp​ duplicates a term in yc​, multiply that term (or the entire trial solution if yc​ has repeated roots matching the trial) by xs, where s is the smallest positive integer that eliminates the duplication.

Step 4. Substitute yp​ into the ODE. Equate coefficients of like terms. Solve the resulting algebraic system.

Step 5. Write the general solution: y=yc​+yp​.

§ 4 — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Polynomial Forcing

y′′−3y′+2y=4x2

Step 1: Homogeneous solution. Characteristic equation: r2−3r+2=0⇒(r−1)(r−2)=0. yc​=C1​ex+C2​e2x

Step 2: Trial solution. g(x)=4x2 is a degree-2 polynomial, so try: yp​=Ax2+Bx+C

Step 3: No overlap with yc​ (which contains only exponentials). No modification needed.

Step 4: Substitute. yp′′​−3yp′​+2yp​=2A−3(2Ax+B)+2(Ax2+Bx+C) =2Ax2+(−6A+2B)x+(2A−3B+2C)=4x2

Matching coefficients:

  • x2: 2A=4⇒A=2
  • x1: −6A+2B=0⇒B=6
  • x0: 2A−3B+2C=0⇒C=7

yp​=2x2+6x+7

General solution: y=C1​ex+C2​e2x+2x2+6x+7​

Example 2 — Exponential Forcing (No Resonance)

y′′+y′−2y=3e4x

Homogeneous: r2+r−2=(r+2)(r−1)=0, so yc​=C1​e−2x+C2​ex.

Trial: yp​=Ae4x. No overlap with yc​.

Substitute: 16Ae4x+4Ae4x−2Ae4x=3e4x 18A=3⇒A=61​

y=C1​e−2x+C2​ex+61​e4x​

Example 3 — Resonance (The Modification Rule in Action)

y′′−y′=2ex

Homogeneous: r2−r=r(r−1)=0, so yc​=C1​+C2​ex.

Trial attempt: yp​=Aex. PROBLEM: ex already appears in yc​!

Modification: Multiply by x: yp​=Axex.

Substitute: yp′​=Aex+Axex,yp′′​=2Aex+Axex yp′′​−yp′​=(2Aex+Axex)−(Aex+Axex)=Aex=2ex A=2

y=C1​+C2​ex+2xex​

Why does resonance require x-multiplication? Physically: when the natural frequency of the system matches the forcing frequency, energy accumulates — the response grows. Mathematically: ex∈ker(L), so L[Aex]=0=2ex. The factor of x “shifts” the trial solution out of the kernel.

Example 4 — Sinusoidal Forcing

y′′+4y=sin(3x)

Homogeneous: r2+4=0⇒r=±2i, so yc​=C1​cos(2x)+C2​sin(2x).

Trial: yp​=Acos(3x)+Bsin(3x). No overlap (frequency 3 ≠ 2).

Substitute: yp′′​+4yp​=(−9A+4A)cos(3x)+(−9B+4B)sin(3x) =−5Acos(3x)−5Bsin(3x)=sin(3x)

So A=0, B=−51​.

y=C1​cos(2x)+C2​sin(2x)−51​sin(3x)​

Note on sinusoids: Always include both cos and sin in the trial, even if g(x) contains only one of them. Differentiation mixes them.

Example 5 — Superposition of Forcing Terms

y′′−2y′+y=xex+cosx

Homogeneous: (r−1)2=0⇒r=1 (repeated), so yc​=(C1​+C2​x)ex.

Use superposition: g(x)=g1​(x)+g2​(x) where g1​=xex and g2​=cosx.

For g1​=xex: Trial would be (Ax+B)ex, but ex and xex are both in yc​ (repeated root!). Multiply by x2: yp1​​=x2(Ax+B)ex=(Ax3+Bx2)ex

After substitution: 6Ax+2B=x⇒A=61​,,B=0. yp1​​=6x3​ex

For g2​=cosx: Trial yp2​​=Acosx+Bsinx (no overlap).

After substitution: A=0,,B=21​. yp2​​=21​sinx

General solution: y=(C1​+C2​x)ex+6x3​ex+21​sinx​

§ 6 — The Modification Rule: A Rigorous Justification

Theorem (Annihilator Interpretation). The trial solution must be chosen so that yp​∈/ker(L). If the natural guess y~​p​∈ker(L), then L[y~​p​]=0 identically, making it impossible to match the nonzero g(x). Multiplying by xs uses the fact that if r0​ is a root of the characteristic polynomial of multiplicity m, then xker0​x (for k<m) is in ker(L), but xmer0​x is not.

More precisely: if r0​ is a root of multiplicity m, then: ker(L)∋er0​x,xer0​x,…,xm−1er0​x but xmer0​x∈/ker(L).

So s=m (the multiplicity of the overlap) is the correct shift.

§ 7 — When This Method Fails (And What To Do)

The method of undetermined coefficients is not universal. It fails when g(x) is:

  • lnx, x−1, tanx, secx — these are not closed under differentiation
  • Any function not expressible as a finite combination of the admissible types

In these cases, use: Variation of Parameters (Stewart §17.3), which works for any continuous g(x) and is more general but computationally heavier.

§ 8 — Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

This is where classical analysis secretly lives inside modern learning systems.

8.1 — ODEs as Continuous-Time Dynamical Systems

Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) replace discrete residual layers: ht+1​=ht​+f(ht​,θ) with a continuous ODE: dtdh​=f(h(t),t,θ)

Understanding particular solutions — how a system responds to forcing — is directly analogous to understanding how a network’s hidden state evolves under the influence of input data.

8.2 — The Forcing Function as Input Signal

In a linear ODE model, g(x) is the input and y is the output. The particular solution yp​ is the system’s response to the input, while yc​ encodes the intrinsic dynamics (how it behaves without input).

In signal processing and sequence modeling (RNNs, LSTMs, SSMs):

  • yc​ ↔ hidden state dynamics (how the model evolves intrinsically)
  • yp​ ↔ input-driven response (how the model reacts to data)

8.3 — State Space Models and Mamba

Modern State Space Models (e.g., Mamba, S4) are literally discretized versions of: x′(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t) y(t)=Cx(t)

This is a first-order linear ODE system with forcing Bu(t). The particular solution theory underpins the transfer function of these models — the frequency-domain characterization of how different inputs are amplified or suppressed.

8.4 — Resonance as Gradient Explosion / Instability

The resonance phenomenon (§4, §5) has a deep ML analogue: gradient explosion in RNNs. When the recurrent weight matrix has eigenvalues close to 1 (analogous to ωforcing​≈ω0​), gradients grow without bound during backpropagation through time — mathematically the same secular growth as tsin(ω0​t).

LSTM’s gating mechanism and careful initialization (e.g., orthogonal initialization) are engineering solutions to this resonance problem.

8.5 — Fourier / Spectral Methods

The fact that cos(βx) and sin(βx) form closed families under L is the seed of spectral methods. In deep learning:

  • Attention mechanisms can be interpreted through the lens of kernel functions that decompose inputs spectrally
  • Positional encodings in Transformers use sin and cos precisely because they span a basis that is stable under linear operations — the same reason they work in undetermined coefficients

8.6 — Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs minimize: L=ODE residual∣L[uθ​]−g∣2​​+boundary terms​​

Here uθ​ is a neural network approximating the solution. The particular solution yp​ found by undetermined coefficients is the exact version of what a PINN tries to approximate. Understanding the analytic structure of yp​ helps in initializing PINNs, choosing architectures, and diagnosing training failures.

§ 10 — Exercises (Self-Diagnostics)

1. Find yp​ for y′′+2y′+y=e−x. (Hint: what is the multiplicity of r=−1?)

2. Find yp​ for y′′−y=x2ex. (Modification rule applies.)

3. Verify that for y′′+ω2y=cos(ωt), the particular solution is yp​=2ωt​sin(ωt) by direct substitution.

4. (Theoretical) Show that if g(x)=eαxPn​(x)cos(βx)+eαxQm​(x)sin(βx), the trial particular solution must include both cosine and sine terms with polynomials of degree max(n,m).

5. (ML connection) Consider the linear RNN update ht+1​=aht​+ut​. Show that this is a discretization of h′(t)=(a−1)h(t)+u(t), and identify the conditions under which resonance (gradient explosion) occurs in terms of a.
