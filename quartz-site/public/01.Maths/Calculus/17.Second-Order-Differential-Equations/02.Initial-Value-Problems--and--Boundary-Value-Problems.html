02.Initial Value Problems & Boundary Value Problems

Apr 23, 202621 min read

“An ODE without conditions is a family portrait. The conditions pick out one face from the crowd.”

§ 0 — The Central Question

Solving a differential equation gives you a family of solutions — parameterized by arbitrary constants. For a second order ODE, two constants c1​,c2​. The solution space is a plane.

Imposing conditions means picking a specific solution from this family. But conditions come in fundamentally different flavors, and the difference is not merely technical — it reflects a deep physical distinction about what kind of information we have.

§ 1 — Two Ways to Specify a Solution

1.1 Initial Value Problems (IVP)

An Initial Value Problem specifies the solution and its derivative at a single point x0​:

y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x),y(x0​)=y0​,y′(x0​)=y0′​

Both conditions are imposed at the same location x0​ — the “initial” point.

Physical meaning: You know the complete state of the system at time t0​: position and velocity. From this, you predict the future (and past). This is the Cauchy problem — the formulation of classical mechanics.

1.2 Boundary Value Problems (BVP)

A Boundary Value Problem specifies conditions at two different points x=a and x=b:

y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x),B1​[y](https://claude.ai/chat/a)=α,B2​[y](https://claude.ai/chat/b)=β

where B1​,B2​ are boundary operators (often evaluation of y or y′).

Physical meaning: You know something about the system at both ends of an interval, but nothing in between. This is the formulation of equilibrium problems — a beam deflecting between two supports, a wire with fixed endpoints, the temperature distribution in a rod with fixed endpoint temperatures.

1.3 The Fundamental Difference

IVPBVP
Conditions atOne point x0​Two points a, b
InformationState at one momentEndpoint constraints
Physical typeEvolution in timeSpatial equilibrium
Existence/UniquenessAlways (if continuous coefficients)Not guaranteed
Solution methodDirect integration, characteristic eq.Shooting, finite difference, eigenfunction expansion

Key insight: For an IVP, existence and uniqueness are essentially free. For a BVP, they are delicate — a BVP may have no solution, exactly one, or infinitely many. This is the central surprise of the theory.

§ 2 — Initial Value Problems: The Complete Theory

2.1 Existence and Uniqueness

Theorem (Picard–Lindelöf for second order linear ODEs). Let p, q, g be continuous on an open interval I containing x0​. Then the IVP:

y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x),y(x0​)=y0​,y′(x0​)=y0′​

has a unique solution defined on all of I.

Proof sketch. Write the IVP as a first order system by setting u=(y y′​):

u′=(0​1 −q(x)​−p(x)​)u+(0 g(x)​),u(x0​)=(y0​ y0′​​)

The right-hand side is linear in u with continuous coefficient matrix. A linear system with continuous coefficients satisfies a global Lipschitz condition, so Picard iteration converges on all of I. Uniqueness follows from the Gronwall inequality. ■

What this theorem buys: Once you know y(x0​) and y′(x0​), the solution is completely and uniquely determined everywhere. No ambiguity whatsoever.

2.2 The Solution Algorithm

Step 1. Solve the homogeneous equation L[y]=0 to find a fundamental set y1​,y2​.

Step 2. Find a particular solution yp​ (undetermined coefficients or variation of parameters).

Step 3. Write the general solution: y=c1​y1​+c2​y2​+yp​.

Step 4. Apply initial conditions to determine c1​,c2​:

{c1​y1​(x0​)+c2​y2​(x0​)=y0​−yp​(x0​) c1​y1′​(x0​)+c2​y2′​(x0​)=y0′​−yp′​(x0​)​

The coefficient matrix of this system is (y1​(x0​)​y2​(x0​) y1′​(x0​)​y2′​(x0​)​), whose determinant is W(x0​)=0 (since y1​,y2​ is a fundamental set). So the system always has a unique solution. This is exactly why IVPs always have unique solutions when the homogeneous equation is solved correctly.

2.3 Worked Example: Full IVP

Problem: Solve y′′−3y′+2y=e3x, y(0)=1, y′(0)=0.

Step 1: Characteristic equation: r2−3r+2=(r−1)(r−2)=0. So y1​=ex, y2​=e2x.

Step 2: Since e3x is not a homogeneous solution, try yp​=Ae3x: 9A−9A+2A=1⟹A=21​ So yp​=21​e3x.

Step 3: General solution: y=c1​ex+c2​e2x+21​e3x.

Subtracting: c2​=−2. Then c1​=25​.

y=25​ex−2e2x+21​e3x​

2.4 Nonlinear IVPs: Local vs. Global Existence

For nonlinear IVPs y′′=f(x,y,y′), the situation is more delicate.

Theorem (Local Existence). If f, ∂f/∂y, ∂f/∂y′ are continuous near (x0​,y0​,y0′​), then the IVP has a unique solution on some interval (x0​−h,x0​+h).

The solution may blow up in finite time. Example: y′=y2, y(0)=1 gives y=1−x1​ — which blows up at x=1. Linearity is what prevents this from happening in the linear case.

§ 3 — Boundary Value Problems: Where Things Get Interesting

3.1 The Setup

Consider the second order linear BVP on [a,b]:

y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x)

with boundary conditions (BCs) of one of these types:

TypeConditionName
Dirichlety(a)=α, y(b)=βPrescribed values
Neumanny′(a)=α, y′(b)=βPrescribed derivatives (flux)
Robin (Mixed)y′(a)−h1​y(a)=α, y′(b)+h2​y(b)=βMixed value and flux

The most common in practice (and in Stewart) are Dirichlet conditions.

3.2 The Shocking Difference from IVPs: Three Cases

Unlike IVPs, a BVP may have no solution, a unique solution, or infinitely many solutions. This is not a theorem — it is a fact you must check case by case. Here are three examples that make this vivid.

Example A: Unique Solution

y′′+y=x,y(0)=0,y(π/2)=0

Homogeneous solution: yh​=c1​cosx+c2​sinx. Particular solution: yp​=x.

General: y=c1​cosx+c2​sinx+x.

Apply BCs:

  • y(0)=0: c1​=0.
  • y(π/2)=0: c2​+π/2=0⟹c2​=−π/2.

Unique solution: y=−2π​sinx+x. ✓

Example B: No Solution

y′′+y=0,y(0)=0,y(π)=1

Homogeneous solution: y=c1​cosx+c2​sinx.

Apply BCs:

  • y(0)=0: c1​=0. So y=c2​sinx.
  • y(π)=1: c2​sinπ=0=1. Contradiction.

No solution exists. ✗

Example C: Infinitely Many Solutions

y′′+y=0,y(0)=0,y(π)=0

Apply BCs:

  • y(0)=0: c1​=0. So y=c2​sinx.
  • y(π)=0: c2​sinπ=0, satisfied for any c2​.

Solution: y=c2​sinx for any c2​∈R. ✗ (infinitely many)

What just happened? The boundary condition y(π)=0 with y=c2​sinx is automatically satisfied because sinπ=0 regardless of c2​. The boundary “accidentally” coincides with the structure of the homogeneous solution. This is not a coincidence — it is the phenomenon of eigenvalues, which is the heart of the BVP theory.

3.3 The Algebraic Reason: When Does Uniqueness Fail?

After applying the general solution y=c1​y1​+c2​y2​+yp​ to Dirichlet BCs y(a)=α, y(b)=β, we get the linear system:

(y1​(a)​y2​(a) y1​(b)​y2​(b)​)(c1​ c2​​)=(α−yp​(a) β−yp​(b)​)

Let D=y1​(a)y2​(b)−y1​(b)y2​(a) (the determinant of the boundary matrix).

  • D=0: Unique solution always exists (for any α,β,g).
  • D=0: Either no solution or infinitely many — depending on whether the right-hand side is consistent.

Note: D is not the Wronskian W(x) (which is never zero). D is the Wronskian-like expression evaluated at the two different boundary points a and b. It can be zero even when W=0.

This is the fundamental obstacle in BVP theory, absent in IVP theory.

§ 4 — Eigenvalue Problems and Sturm–Liouville Theory

4.1 The Eigenvalue Problem

Consider the parameterized homogeneous BVP:

y′′+λy=0,y(0)=0,y(L)=0

For which values of λ does a nontrivial solution exist?

Case 1: λ<0. Write λ=−μ2, μ>0. General solution: y=c1​eμx+c2​e−μx.

  • y(0)=0: c1​+c2​=0.
  • y(L)=0: c1​(eμL−e−μL)=0⟹c1​=0 (since eμL=e−μL).
  • Only trivial solution y=0. No eigenvalue here.

Case 2: λ=0. y′′=0⇒y=c1​+c2​x. BCs force c1​=c2​=0. No eigenvalue.

Case 3: λ>0. Write λ=μ2, μ>0. General solution: y=c1​cosμx+c2​sinμx.

  • y(0)=0: c1​=0. So y=c2​sinμx.
  • y(L)=0: c2​sinμL=0. For nontrivial solution: sinμL=0⇒μL=nπ for n=1,2,3,…

Eigenvalues:

λn​=L2n2π2​,n=1,2,3,…

Eigenfunctions:

yn​(x)=sin(Lnπx​)

These eigenfunctions are the natural modes of the system. The BVP has nontrivial solutions if and only if λ is one of these eigenvalues.

4.2 Orthogonality of Eigenfunctions

The eigenfunctions yn​ satisfy a remarkable property:

∫0L​sinLmπx​sinLnπx​,dx={0​m=n L/2​m=n​

The eigenfunctions are orthogonal in L2([0,L]). This is not a coincidence — it is a consequence of the Sturm–Liouville theorem.

4.3 Sturm–Liouville Theory

The general Sturm–Liouville problem is:

−dxd​[p(x)y′]+q(x)y=λw(x)y,a<x<b

with boundary conditions, where p>0, w>0 (weight function).

Theorem (Sturm–Liouville). Under appropriate conditions:

  1. All eigenvalues λn​ are real.
  2. The eigenvalues form an increasing sequence λ1​<λ2​<λ3​<⋯→+∞.
  3. Each eigenvalue has exactly one linearly independent eigenfunction yn​.
  4. The eigenfunctions yn​ are orthogonal with respect to the weight w: ∫ab​ym​(x)yn​(x)w(x),dx=0,m=n
  5. The eigenfunctions form a complete orthogonal basis of Lw2​([a,b]): any square-integrable function can be expanded as f(x)=∑n=1∞​cn​yn​(x).

This is why Fourier series exist. The sine and cosine functions are eigenfunctions of −d2/dx2 with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions respectively. Fourier series is Sturm–Liouville theory for the simplest operator.

§ 5 — Shooting Method: Solving BVPs Numerically

The shooting method converts a BVP into a sequence of IVPs — exploiting the clean existence/uniqueness theory of IVPs to handle the messy BVP.

5.1 The Idea

For the BVP y′′=f(x,y,y′), y(a)=α, y(b)=β:

We know y(a)=α but don’t know y′(a).

Guess y′(a)=s (the “shooting parameter”). Solve the IVP: y′′=f(x,y,y′),y(a)=α,y′(a)=s

This gives a solution y(x;s). Define the residual: F(s)=y(b;s)−β

We want F(s)=0. Use root-finding (bisection, Newton’s method) to find s^_ such that F(s^_) = 0.

  y
  |        β ------- target
  |       /
  |      /  ← too low (s too small)
  |   α /
  |    / ← correct s*
  |   /\  ← too high (s too large)
  +---+----------> x
      a          b

For a linear BVP, F(s) is linear in s, so we only need two shots.

5.2 Linear Shooting Method

For the linear BVP y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x), y(a)=α, y(b)=β:

Solve two IVPs:

IVP 1: u′′+pu′+qu=g, u(a)=α, u′(a)=0.

IVP 2: v′′+pv′+qv=0, v(a)=0, v′(a)=1.

By linearity, y=u+cv solves the ODE for any constant c. Apply the boundary condition at b:

y(b)=u(b)+cv(b)=β⟹c=v(b)β−u(b)​

provided v(b)=0 (which is exactly the condition D=0 from §3.3 — the unique solution condition).

The solution is y(x)=u(x)+v(b)β−u(b)​v(x).

§ 6 — Green’s Functions: The BVP Analogue of Variation of Parameters

For the BVP: L[y]=y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x),y(a)=0,y(b)=0

(homogeneous Dirichlet BCs), assuming unique solvability, there exists a Green’s function G(x,t) such that:

y(x)=∫ab​G(x,t)g(t),dt

6.1 Construction of G(x,t)

Let y1​,y2​ be the fundamental set of L[y]=0 with:

  • y1​ satisfying the left BC: y1​(a)=0.
  • y2​ satisfying the right BC: y2​(b)=0.

Then:

G(x,t)=W(t)1​{y1​(x)y2​(t)​a≤x≤t y1​(t)y2​(x)​t≤x≤b​

where W(t)=W(y1​,y2​)(t) is the Wronskian.

6.2 Properties of the Green’s Function

  • G(x,t) is continuous at x=t.
  • Gx​(x,t) has a jump discontinuity at x=t: Gx​(t+,t)−Gx​(t−,t)=p(t)1​ (from the jump condition).
  • G(x,t)=G(t,x) when L is self-adjoint (Sturm–Liouville type) — symmetry of the Green’s function.
  • Lx​[G(x,t)]=δ(x−t), where δ is the Dirac delta — G is the “impulse response” of the operator L.

The physical interpretation: G(x,t) is the displacement at position x of a string (or beam) due to a unit point load at position t. The total displacement under a distributed load g(t) is the superposition integral ∫G(x,t)g(t),dt.

This is the deflection of a string under load g(x).

Homogeneous equation −y′′=0⇒y′′=0: general solution y=Ax+B.

  • y1​ satisfies y1​(0)=0: take y1​=x.
  • y2​ satisfies y2​(1)=0: take y2​=1−x.
  • Wronskian: W=y1​y2′​−y1′​y2​=x(−1)−1(1−x)=−1.

G(x,t)=−11​{x(1−t)​0≤x≤t t(1−x)​t≤x≤1​={−x(1−t)​x≤t −t(1−x)​x≥t​

Wait — let me recheck the sign. The equation is −y′′=g⇒L[y]=−y′′, so p=0, q=0, a=−1 in L[y]=ay′′. The standard form y′′=−g gives the Green’s function for y′′=δ:

G(x,t)={−x(1−t)​x≤t −t(1−x)​x≥t​

Note G≤0 — deflection is downward under a downward load. Note also G(x,t)=G(t,x) — the symmetry of the self-adjoint operator. The solution:

y(x)=∫01​G(x,t)(−g(t)),dt

For g=1 (uniform load): y(x)=2x(1−x)​ — a parabola, as expected for a uniformly loaded string.

§ 7 — Finite Difference Methods for BVPs

When analytic methods fail, we discretize. Replace the continuous BVP by a system of algebraic equations on a grid.

7.1 The Discretization

Divide [a,b] into N+1 equal subintervals: xi​=a+ih, h=(b−a)/(N+1), i=0,1,…,N+1.

The second-order finite difference approximations:

y′′(xi​)≈h2yi−1​−2yi​+yi+1​​,y′(xi​)≈2hyi+1​−yi−1​​

7.2 The Linear System

For the BVP y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=g(x), y(a)=α, y(b)=β, substituting the approximations at each interior node xi​ (i=1,…,N):

h2yi−1​−2yi​+yi+1​​+pi​2hyi+1​−yi−1​​+qi​yi​=gi​

Multiplying through by h2:

(1−2h​pi​)yi−1​+(−2+h2qi​)yi​+(1+2h​pi​)yi+1​=h2gi​

With y0​=α and yN+1​=β absorbed into the right-hand side, this is a tridiagonal linear system Ay=b of size N×N:

A=(−2+h2q1​​1+2h​p1​​​ 1−2h​p2​​−2+h2q2​​1+2h​p2​​ ​⋱​⋱​⋱ ​​1−2h​pN​​−2+h2qN​​)

Tridiagonal systems are solved in O(N) operations by the Thomas algorithm (tridiagonal LU factorization).

§ 9 — Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

9.1 Neural Networks as BVP Solvers: PINNs

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) are trained to solve BVPs:

L[y]=g on (a,b),y(a)=α,y(b)=β

The network yθ​(x) minimizes:

L(θ)=interior residualN1​i=1∑N​∣L[yθ​](https://claude.ai/chat/xi​)−g(xi​)∣2​​+boundary residual∣yθ​(a)−α∣2+∣yθ​(b)−β∣2​​

The IVP vs BVP distinction matters critically here:

  • IVP: Only the left boundary appears in the loss. Integration marches from left to right. This is numerically stable (Runge–Kutta).
  • BVP: Both boundaries constrain simultaneously. The optimizer must satisfy conditions on two sides at once. This is inherently harder — the loss landscape has ridges and saddle points that gradient descent struggles with near eigenvalues. When λ is close to an eigenvalue of L, the problem is nearly singular, and the neural network must “learn” the shooting method implicitly.

9.2 Boundary Conditions in PDE-solving Networks

For higher-dimensional PDEs (the natural extension of BVPs), enforcing boundary conditions is an active research problem:

  • Soft enforcement (penalty): Add boundary residual to the loss. Simple but the boundary may not be exactly satisfied.
  • Hard enforcement: Parameterize the network as yθ​(x)=A(x)+B(x)Nθ​(x), where A(x) satisfies the BCs exactly and B(x) vanishes on the boundary. Then Nθ​ is unconstrained. This is analogous to the shooting method’s linear decomposition y=u+cv.

9.3 IVPs in Sequence Models: Autoregressive Generation

Autoregressive language models (GPT, etc.) solve a discrete IVP: given the “initial condition” (the prompt = y0​,y0′​,…), march forward in time to generate the sequence. The existence and uniqueness theorem for IVPs is mirrored in the fact that, given a prompt, the model deterministically (at temperature 0) generates a unique continuation — the discrete analog of the unique IVP solution.

The contrast with BVPs appears in constrained generation:

  • Generate text that starts with A (left BC) and ends with B (right BC).
  • This is a discrete BVP, and it may have no solution, one solution, or many.
  • Controlled generation methods (like FUDGE, PPLM, classifier guidance in diffusion) are essentially numerical BVP solvers operating in the space of text.

9.4 Diffusion Models as IVPs Running Backwards

Score-based diffusion models define a forward SDE (adding noise):

dx=f(x,t),dt+g(t),dW

with initial condition x(0)∼pdata​. This is an IVP in distribution space.

The reverse (denoising) process is also an IVP, running backwards from t=T (pure noise as initial condition) to t=0 (generated sample):

dx=[f(x,t)−g(t)2∇x​logpt​(x)]dt

The learned score ∇x​logpt​(x) plays the role of p(x) in the ODE. The IVP structure guarantees that, given an initial noise vector, there is a unique denoising trajectory — which is why diffusion models are deterministic at inference given a seed.

9.5 Green’s Functions and Attention

The output of self-attention in a transformer can be written as:

Attention(Q,K,V)i​=∑j​Aij​Vj​

where Aij​=softmax(Qi​⋅Kj​/d​).

This is a discrete integral operator with kernel Aij​ — precisely the structure of a Green’s function integral y(x)=∫G(x,t)g(t),dt. The attention kernel Aij​ plays the role of the Green’s function G(xi​,tj​), and the values Vj​ play the role of the forcing g(tj​).

More precisely: attention learns a data-dependent Green’s function for the implicit operator that maps inputs to outputs. This connection is made rigorous in recent work on attention as integral operators and neural operators (Li et al., 2020 — Fourier Neural Operator), where the network directly parameterizes the Green’s function of a PDE operator.

9.6 Eigenvalues, Stability, and the Spectrum of Neural Networks

The Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue theory has a direct analogue in deep learning:

  • The Hessian ∇2L of the loss function plays the role of L in L[y]=λy.
  • The eigenvalues of the Hessian determine convergence rate: directions with large λ (sharp curvature) are hard to optimize; directions with small λ are flat.
  • Batch normalization implicitly shifts the eigenvalue spectrum of layer-wise Jacobians — it is an attempt to solve a “spectral BVP” at each layer: keep the singular values of the weight Jacobian near 1.
  • Residual connections (ResNets) modify the spectrum: hl+1​=hl​+F(hl​) shifts all eigenvalues by 1, preventing eigenvalues from being too close to 0 (vanishing gradients = small eigenvalues of the chain product Jacobian).

The S4 / Mamba state space models explicitly parameterize their recurrent matrices to have eigenvalues with negative real part — mirroring the Sturm–Liouville condition that eigenvalues are real and bounded below. This guarantees the IVP stability of the recurrence, preventing the exponential blow-up (exploding gradients) that plagues vanilla RNNs.

§ 10 — Summary

IVP: y(x0​)=y0​, y′(x0​)=y0′​

Always has a unique solution on I if p,q,g∈C(I)​

  • Two conditions at one point.
  • Solve: find general solution, apply ICs to get unique c1​,c2​.
  • Nonlinear IVPs: may blow up in finite time.

BVP: y(a)=α, y(b)=β

May have 0, 1, or ∞ solutions — check the boundary matrix determinant D​

  • Solve: shooting method, finite differences, eigenfunction expansion.
  • Eigenvalue problems: nontrivial solutions exist only for special λ — the eigenvalues.
  • Eigenfunctions form a complete orthogonal basis (Sturm–Liouville) — the foundation of Fourier series and spectral methods.
