“The velocity problem and the tangent problem appear to be different. But they turn out to be the same problem in disguise — and that disguise is the derivative.”

§1. The Physical Motivation

Suppose a ball is dropped from a tower. Physics tells us its height (in metres) at time t (in seconds) is approximately:

s(t)=4.9t2

Question: How fast is the ball falling at exactly t=2 seconds?

The phrase “at exactly t=2” is the crux. Speed is ordinarily computed as:

speed=timedistance​

But this ratio requires two moments in time — a start and an end. At a single instant, both numerator and denominator collapse to zero. We have a 00​ problem.

This is not a computational inconvenience. It is a genuine conceptual difficulty that Newton and Leibniz spent their lives resolving. The resolution is the limit.

§2. Average Velocity vs. Instantaneous Velocity

Definition: Average Velocity

Over the time interval [t1​,t2​], the average velocity is:

vˉ=t2​−t1​s(t2​)−s(t1​)​=ΔtΔs​

This is just slope: the slope of the secant line on the position-time graph between (t1​,s(t1​)) and (t2​,s(t2​)).

Definition: Instantaneous Velocity

Fix a moment t=a. Let h=Δt→0. The instantaneous velocity is:

v(a)=limh→0​hs(a+h)−s(a)​

This is the slope of the tangent line on the position-time graph at (a,s(a)).

The velocity problem is the tangent problem, with time on the horizontal axis.

See also: The Tangent Problem

The derivative of position is velocity. The derivative of velocity is acceleration. Differentiation is the engine of kinematics.

§5. The Full Kinematic Chain

Let s(t) be position. Then:

v(t)=s′(t)=limh→0​hs(t+h)−s(t)​(velocity)

a(t)=v′(t)=s′′(t)(acceleration)

For our falling ball s(t)=4.9t2=21​gt2:

QuantityFunctionValue at t=2
Positions(t)=4.9t219.6 m
Velocityv(t)=9.8t19.6 m/s
Accelerationa(t)=9.89.8 m/s²

The acceleration is constant — this is the defining property of free fall near Earth’s surface. Newton’s second law F=ma with F=mg gives a=g.

§6. Example 2 — Oscillatory Motion

Let s(t)=sint (a particle on a spring, say). Find the velocity at t=0.

v(0)=limh→0​hsin(0+h)−sin0​=limh→0​hsinh​

This is the fundamental trigonometric limit:

limh→0​hsinh​=1

So v(0)=1 m/s (in appropriate units). And indeed dtd​sint=cost, which gives cos0=1. ✓

This limit is not obvious. It requires the geometric squeeze theorem — the area of a circular sector squeezed between two triangles. See Stewart §3.3.

§7. Example 3 — Negative Velocity (Motion in Reverse)

Let s(t)=−t2+4t (a projectile thrown upward).

v(t)=limh→0​h[−(t+h)2+4(t+h)]−[−t2+4t]​

=limh→0​h−t2−2th−h2+4t+4h+t2−4t​

=limh→0​h−2th−h2+4h​=limh→0​(−2t−h+4)=−2t+4

Analysis:

  • v(t)=0⇒t=2: the ball is momentarily at rest (peak of trajectory).
  • v(t)>0 for t<2: ball moving upward (positive direction).
  • v(t)<0 for t>2: ball moving downward (negative velocity).
s(t) = -t² + 4t

  s
  4|    *   ← peak at t=2, s=4; velocity = 0 here
  |   / \
  |  /   \
  | /     \
  |/       \
  *_________*_________ t
  0         4

v(t) = -2t + 4: positive before t=2, zero at t=2, negative after.

The sign of the derivative encodes the direction of motion. Zero derivative = stationary point.

§8. Formal Definitions and Theorems

Definition. Let s:[0,T]→R be a position function. The instantaneous velocity at time a∈(0,T) is:

v(a)=s′(a)=limh→0​hs(a+h)−s(a)​

if the limit exists.

Definition. The speed at time a is ∣v(a)∣ — velocity is a signed scalar (or in higher dimensions, a vector); speed is its magnitude.

Theorem (Mean Value Theorem for velocity). If s is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), then there exists c∈(a,b) such that:

v(c)=s′(c)=b−as(b)−s(a)​

In words: at some moment, the instantaneous velocity equals the average velocity over the interval. This is the MVT — one of the deepest theorems in calculus.

Practical consequence: if your average speed on a highway is 120 km/h, then at some moment your speedometer read exactly 120 km/h. The MVT is also why radar speed traps work.

§9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

The velocity problem is not just a physics curiosity. It is the conceptual prototype for how neural networks learn — and how time-series models reason about change.

9.1 Learning as Motion Through Parameter Space

Think of training a neural network as a particle moving through parameter space Rn. The weights w(t) evolve over “time” (iterations t), following:

dtdw​=−∇w​L(w)

This is gradient flow — the continuous-time limit of gradient descent. The velocity of the parameter particle at time t is −∇L. Just as v(t)=s′(t) tells us how position changes, the gradient tells us how loss changes as we move through weight space.

The discrete update rule w(t+1)=w(t)−η∇L is the Euler method for this ODE — the most primitive numerical integration of the velocity problem.

9.2 Momentum Optimizers

In physics, a particle with momentum p=mv resists sudden changes in direction. The SGD with momentum optimizer is exactly this:

p(t+1)=βp(t)−η∇L(w(t)) w(t+1)=w(t)+p(t+1)

The parameter β∈(0,1) is the momentum coefficient — a “friction” term that decays past velocities. The Adam optimizer goes further: it tracks both the first moment (velocity) and second moment (variance of velocity) of the gradient, adaptively scaling each parameter’s learning rate.

9.3 Recurrent Neural Networks and Temporal Derivatives

An RNN processes sequences x1​,x2​,…,xT​ and maintains a hidden state ht​ that evolves over time. The continuous-time limit is the Neural ODE:

dtdh​=fθ​(h(t),x(t))

Here dtdh​ is literally the instantaneous velocity of the hidden state — exactly the velocity problem. Training Neural ODEs requires differentiating through an ODE solver (using the adjoint method, which is itself a calculus result).

9.4 Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs solve differential equations (which are velocity equations — equations involving s′(t), s′′(t), etc.) by training neural networks whose loss includes a penalty for violating the ODE:

L=data termi∑​(uθ​(ti​)−ui​)2​​+physics term (velocity residual)j∑​(dtduθ​​​tj​​−f(uθ​(tj​)))2​​

The physics term is zero only when the network’s instantaneous rate of change matches the prescribed ODE. This is a direct application of the velocity problem inside a neural network loss function.

9.5 Finite Differences in Time-Series

The difference quotient approximation:

v(t)≈hs(t+h)−s(t)​

appears directly as a feature engineering step in time-series ML. First differences Δxt​=xt​−xt−1​ are used to stationarize time series before feeding them to models — removing trends by working with velocities (rates of change) rather than raw levels.

Raw series (non-stationary):         First differences (stationary):
   x                                    Δx
   |    /                               |  *  *     *
   |   /                                | * *   * *
   |  /                                 |*         *
   | /                                  |
   |/_____ t                            |___________ t
   trend present                        trend removed

ARIMA models explicitly include an “integrated” component d — the number of times the series must be differenced to become stationary — because d-fold differencing approximates the d-th order derivative.

§11. What the Velocity Problem Teaches Us

  1. Instantaneous quantities are limits of average quantities. There is no shortcut. The real number v(a) exists only because the difference quotient converges.

  2. The sign of v encodes direction. Zero velocity = critical point (maximum, minimum, or inflection in time).

  3. Acceleration is the second derivative. One differentiation gives velocity; a second gives acceleration. Higher derivatives encode higher-order structure of motion — and of functions.

  4. Differential equations are velocity equations. Newton’s law F=ma is s′′(t)=F/m — an equation constraining the second derivative of position. Virtually all laws of physics are written this way.

  5. In ML, the gradient is velocity through loss landscape. Training dynamics, optimizer design, and convergence theory are all kinematics in disguise.
