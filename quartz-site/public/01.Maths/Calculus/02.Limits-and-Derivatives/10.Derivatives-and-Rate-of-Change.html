10.Derivatives and Rate of Change

10.Derivatives and Rate of Change

Apr 16, 202616 min read

“The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change — a limit that collapses an interval to a point, and in doing so, extracts the geometry of the function at that point.”

§1. Motivation — The Two Problems That Built Calculus

Newton and Leibniz arrived at the derivative from two directions that turn out to be the same thing.

The tangent problem. Given a curve y=f(x) and a point P=(a,f(a)), find the slope of the line tangent to the curve at P. A tangent line touches the curve at one point and captures its instantaneous direction.

The velocity problem. Given a position function s(t), find the instantaneous velocity at time t=a. Average velocity over [a,a+h] is Δs/Δt; but what happens as the time interval collapses?

Both reduce to the same limiting process. The derivative is their common answer.

§2. The Definition

2.1 The Difference Quotient

The average rate of change of f over [a,a+h] is the slope of the secant line through (a,f(a)) and (a+h,f(a+h)):

ΔxΔf​=hf(a+h)−f(a)​

 y
 |               ● (a+h, f(a+h))
 |             /
 |           /  ← secant line, slope = Δf/Δx
 |         /
 |       ● (a, f(a))
 |
 +---+---+---+------- x
     a   a+h

As h→0, the second point slides toward (a,f(a)), and the secant line rotates toward the tangent line.

2.2 The Derivative

Definition. The derivative of f at a is: f′(a)=limh→0​hf(a+h)−f(a)​ provided this limit exists. If it does, f is differentiable at a.

Equivalent formulation (substituting x=a+h):

f′(a)=limx→a​x−af(x)−f(a)​

Both forms appear constantly. Know both.

2.3 The Derivative as a Function

If f is differentiable at every point in its domain, we get the derivative function:

f′(x)=limh→0​hf(x+h)−f(x)​

This is itself a function — it assigns to each x the slope of the tangent to f at x.

2.4 Notation

All of the following mean the same thing:

f′(x)=y′=dxdy​=dxdf​=Df(x)=y˙​ (physics)

Leibniz notation dy/dx is not a fraction — it is a single symbol, the limit of Δy/Δx. But it behaves like a fraction in chain rule and in differentials, which is one of its great virtues.

§3. Differentiability and Continuity

Theorem. If f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a.

Proof. We want limx→a​f(x)=f(a). Write:

f(x)−f(a)=x−af(x)−f(a)​⋅(x−a)

Taking limits:

limx→a​[f(x)−f(a)]=limx→a​x−af(x)−f(a)​⋅limx→a​(x−a)=f′(a)⋅0=0

So limx→a​f(x)=f(a). ■

The converse is false. Continuity does not imply differentiability.

Canonical counterexample: f(x)=∣x∣ at x=0.

f is continuous at 0, but:

limh→0+​h∣h∣−0​=1,limh→0−​h∣h∣−0​=−1

Left and right derivatives disagree. The limit does not exist. f′(0) does not exist — there is a corner.

Geometric Picture of Non-Differentiability

Corner (|x|)         Cusp (x^{2/3})       Vertical Tangent (x^{1/3})
 y                    y                     y
  \  /                  |                   |/
   \/                   |                   |
   0      x           --+---  x           --+--  x
                                            |
slope: +1/-1 disagree   slope → ∞          slope = ∞

Three ways differentiability fails: corners, cusps, vertical tangents (and discontinuities).

§4. The Differentiation Rules

Computing derivatives from the definition every time is untenable. The differentiation rules are theorems — each provable from the limit definition.

4.2 The Product Rule — Intuition

Why is (fg)′=f′g+fg′ and not f′g′? Think of f and g as side lengths of a rectangle with area A=f(x)g(x).

        g + Δg
   +----------+----+
   |          |    |
 f |  f·g     |f·Δg|
   |          |    |
   +----------+----+
   |    g·Δf  | ε  |  ← second-order small, vanishes
   +----------+----+
      g           Δg

ΔA=f⋅Δg+g⋅Δf+Δf⋅Δg

Dividing by Δx and taking Δx→0: the last term is (Δf/Δx)⋅Δg→f′⋅0=0.

So dxdA​=f⋅g′+g⋅f′. ■

4.3 The Chain Rule — The Most Important Rule

If y=f(u) and u=g(x), then: dxdy​=dudy​⋅dxdu​

In function notation: (f∘g)′(x)=f′(g(x))⋅g′(x).

Reading: differentiate the outside (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

Example. y=sin(x2).

  • Outside: sin(□), derivative cos(□)
  • Inside: x2, derivative 2x

y′=cos(x2)⋅2x

Example. y=e−x2/2 (the Gaussian).

y′=e−x2/2⋅(−x)=−xe−x2/2

The chain rule chains: y=f(g(h(x))) gives y′=f′(g(h(x)))⋅g′(h(x))⋅h′(x).

dxd​xn=nxn−1dxd​ex=exdxd​lnx=x1​

The derivative of ex equals itself — this is the defining property of the exponential. It is why exponential functions appear everywhere dynamics are modeled.

§5. Higher-Order Derivatives

The derivative f′ is itself a function; we can differentiate it again.

f′′(x)=(f′)′(x)=dx2d2y​f(n)(x)=dxndny​

Interpretations:

  • f′(x): instantaneous rate of change, slope of tangent
  • f′′(x): rate of change of slope — concavity
  • f′′′(x): rate of change of concavity — jerk in physics

Physics chain: s(t);dtd​;​v(t);dtd​;​a(t);dtd​;​j(t) position → velocity → acceleration → jerk

Newton’s second law F=ma is a statement about the second derivative of position.

Concavity:

  • f′′>0 on (a,b): f is concave up (bowl facing up, slope increasing)
  • f′′<0 on (a,b): f is concave down (bowl facing down, slope decreasing)

§6. Implicit Differentiation

Not every curve is expressible as y=f(x). When x and y are related implicitly (e.g., x2+y2=r2), differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y as a function of x and applying the chain rule.

Example. Differentiate x2+y2=25:

2x+2ydxdy​=0⟹dxdy​=−yx​

At (3,4): slope =−3/4.

Example. Differentiate x3+y3=6xy (folium of Descartes):

3x2+3y2y′=6y+6xy′⟹y′(3y2−6x)=6y−3x2⟹y′=3y2−6x6y−3x2​=y2−2x2y−x2​

Logarithmic Differentiation

For products, quotients, and powers of functions, take ln first.

Example. y=(2x−1)4x3x+1​​

lny=3lnx+21​ln(x+1)−4ln(2x−1)

yy′​=x3​+2(x+1)1​−2x−18​

y′=y⋅[x3​+2(x+1)1​−2x−18​]

This is far cleaner than the quotient and product rules applied directly.

§7. The Mean Value Theorem

The MVT is the central theorem of differential calculus. Everything about the connection between derivatives and function behavior flows from it.

Theorem (MVT). If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), then there exists c∈(a,b) such that: f′(c)=b−af(b)−f(a)​

Geometric reading: There is a point where the instantaneous slope equals the average slope over the interval. The tangent at c is parallel to the secant from a to b.

 y
 |                    ● (b, f(b))
 |              ·  ·/
 |           · /· /   ← tangent at c parallel to secant
 |          /●/
 |        /  c
 |  (a, f(a))●
 +---+-------+------- x
     a       b

Consequences:

  • f′=0 everywhere on (a,b) ⟹ f is constant
  • f′>0 everywhere ⟹ f is strictly increasing
  • f′<0 everywhere ⟹ f is strictly decreasing

These seem obvious but require proof — and the proof uses MVT.

Rolle’s Theorem (special case): if f(a)=f(b), then f′(c)=0 for some c∈(a,b). Between two equal values, the derivative vanishes somewhere.

§8. Linear Approximation and Differentials

Near x=a, a differentiable function looks like a line:

f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)for x near a

This is the tangent line approximation (or linearization). It is the first-order Taylor polynomial.

Example. Approximate 4.02​.

Let f(x)=x​, a=4, f′(x)=1/(2x​), f′(4)=1/4.

4.02​≈4​+41​(4.02−4)=2+40.02​=2.005

(Exact: 2.00499...)

Differentials

Write dy=f′(x),dx where dx=Δx is a small change in x and dy is the corresponding change in the tangent line (approximating Δy=f(x+Δx)−f(x)).

This notation makes the chain rule transparently a cancellation of differentials:

dxdy​=dudy​⋅dxdu​

and justifies the substitution rule in integration.

§9. Rates of Change — Applications

9.1 Related Rates

When two quantities both depend on time t, differentiating an equation relating them gives a relationship between their rates.

Example. A ladder 10 m long leans against a wall. The base slides away at 2 m/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the base is 6 m from the wall?

Let x = distance of base from wall, y = height of top. x2+y2=100.

Differentiate with respect to t:

2xdtdx​+2ydtdy​=0

When x=6: y=100−36​=8. Given dx/dt=2:

2(6)(2)+2(8)dtdy​=0⟹dtdy​=−1624​=−23​ m/s

The top slides down at 3/2 m/s.

9.2 Optimization

At a local extremum, f′(c)=0 (critical point) or f′(c) does not exist.

First Derivative Test: f′ changes sign at c.

  • +→−: local maximum
  • −→+: local minimum

Second Derivative Test: At critical point c with f′(c)=0:

  • f′′(c)>0: local minimum (concave up)
  • f′′(c)<0: local maximum (concave down)
  • f′′(c)=0: inconclusive

§10. Partial Derivatives (18.02 Perspective)

For f:Rn→R, the partial derivative with respect to xi​ holds all other variables fixed:

∂xi​∂f​(a)=limh→0​hf(a+hei​)−f(a)​

where ei​ is the i-th standard basis vector.

Geometric reading: ∂f/∂x is the slope of the slice of the surface z=f(x,y) cut by a plane of constant y.

The Gradient

∇f=(∂x1​∂f​,∂x2​∂f​,…,∂xn​∂f​)

The gradient is the vector of all partial derivatives. Its geometric meaning: it points in the direction of steepest ascent of f, and its magnitude is the rate of steepest ascent.

∇f(a) is perpendicular to the level set f=f(a) at a.

This is the most important geometric fact in multivariable calculus.

Differentiability in Rn

In higher dimensions, existence of partial derivatives does not imply differentiability (unlike in R). The function must be well-approximated by a linear map near a:

f(a+h)=f(a)+∇f(a)⋅h+o(∣h∣)

If partial derivatives exist and are continuous near a, then f is differentiable at a. (Sufficient, not necessary.)

§11. The Jacobian

For f:Rn→Rm, the derivative generalizes to the Jacobian matrix:

Jf​(x)=(∂f1​/∂x1​​⋯​∂f1​/∂xn​ ⋮​⋱​⋮ ∂fm​/∂x1​​⋯​∂fm​/∂xn​​)

This is the best linear approximation to f near x:

f(x+h)≈f(x)+Jf​(x),h

The chain rule in multiple dimensions: if h=g∘f, then:

Jh​=Jg​⋅Jf​

Matrix multiplication of Jacobians.

§12. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is not analogy — derivatives are the literal computational object underlying all of modern machine learning.

12.1 Gradient Descent is Differentiation Applied

To minimize a loss L(θ), gradient descent takes steps in the direction of steepest descent:

θ←θ−η∇θ​L(θ)

The gradient ∇θ​L is a vector of partial derivatives — one per parameter. A neural network with 109 parameters has a gradient in R109.

The fundamental insight: the gradient tells you, for each parameter θi​, how much a small increase in θi​ increases the loss. Move opposite that direction.

12.2 Backpropagation is the Chain Rule

Training a neural network requires computing ∇θ​L where L is a composition of many functions (layers). This is precisely the chain rule applied to the computational graph.

For a network y^​=fL​∘fL−1​∘⋯∘f1​(x):

∂θ(k)∂L​=∂y^​∂L​⋅∂fL−1​∂y^​​⋅∂fL−2​∂fL−1​​⋯∂θ(k)∂fk​​

Backpropagation is the chain rule evaluated efficiently via dynamic programming — computing the product from right to left, reusing intermediate values.

In matrix form, the chain rule for Jacobians is:

JL,θ(k)​=JL,fL​​⋅JfL​,fL−1​​⋯Jfk​,θ(k)​

Every backward pass through a layer is a Jacobian-vector product.

12.3 Automatic Differentiation

Modern frameworks (PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow) implement automatic differentiation (autograd) — not numerical differentiation (finite differences) nor symbolic differentiation, but exact differentiation via the chain rule on the computational graph.

Two modes:

  • Forward mode: propagate x˙ (tangent vector) forward through the graph. Efficient when n≪m (few inputs, many outputs).
  • Reverse mode (backprop): propagate yˉ​ (adjoint/gradient) backward through the graph. Efficient when m≪n (few outputs — as in scalar loss — many inputs). This is why backprop is used.

The derivative you write in calculus class — the limit of a difference quotient — is what autograd computes, exactly, for every parameter simultaneously.

12.4 Second Derivatives: Curvature and Optimization

The Hessian H=∇2L is the matrix of second partial derivatives:

Hij​=∂θi​∂θj​∂2L​

Newton’s method uses the Hessian to take curvature-aware steps:

θ←θ−H−1∇L

This converges much faster than gradient descent near a minimum (quadratic vs. linear convergence). But computing and inverting an n×n Hessian for n=109 is catastrophically expensive.

Quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS, Adam) approximate H−1 cheaply:

  • Adam uses running estimates of the gradient mean (first moment) and gradient squared (second moment — diagonal Hessian approximation) to adapt step sizes per parameter.

The second derivative test from single-variable calculus — f′′(c)>0 at a critical point means local min — generalizes to: H is positive definite at a critical point means local min. This is what determines whether gradient descent has found a true minimum.

12.5 Directional Derivative and the Gradient

The directional derivative in direction u (unit vector):

Du​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅u=∣∇f∣cosθ

This is maximized when u=∇f/∣∇f∣ — the gradient direction. Gradient descent moves in this direction (with a sign flip for minimization).

In deep learning, understanding which direction to move in parameter space is exactly this computation.

12.6 The Jacobian in Neural Networks

For a layer z=Wa+b followed by activation h=σ(z):

Jh,a​=diag(σ′(z))⋅W

During backprop, the gradient is pre-multiplied by this Jacobian. If σ′(z) is near zero (sigmoid saturation), the Jacobian has small entries and the gradient is crushed — the vanishing gradient problem, now expressed as a Jacobian becoming nearly zero.

Batch normalization, residual connections (ResNets), and careful weight initialization all address this by keeping Jacobians well-conditioned through the depth of the network.

12.7 Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

PINNs encode physical laws (ODEs/PDEs) as constraints on the neural network’s derivatives. The loss function penalizes violations of the differential equation:

L=fit dataLdata​​​+derivatives must satisfy PDEλ,LPDE​​​

where LPDE​ involves the actual partial derivatives of the network output (computed via autograd). The derivative — the calculus object — appears inside the loss being minimized.

§14. Things Worth Losing Sleep Over

  • A function with f′(c)=0 need not have a local extremum there. (f(x)=x3 at x=0: inflection point, not extremum.)
  • Partial derivatives can exist everywhere while the function is not differentiable. (The partial derivatives exist but the linear approximation fails.)
  • ∂2f/∂x∂y=∂2f/∂y∂x (Clairaut’s theorem) requires continuity of the mixed partials — it is not automatic.
  • The Jacobian of a composition is the product of Jacobians — this is the chain rule, the product rule, and matrix multiplication unified into one theorem.
  • Weierstrass constructed a function that is continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere. It looks like noise at every scale. This shattered the 19th-century intuition that continuity implied differentiability “almost everywhere.”
