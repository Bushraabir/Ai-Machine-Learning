The Tangent Problem

“The problem of finding the tangent line to a curve is the fundamental problem of differential calculus — and secretly, the fundamental problem of intelligence itself.”

§1. The Classical Geometric Question

Let C be a curve in the plane, and let P be a point on C.

Naïve attempt: A tangent line at P is a line that “touches” C at P without “crossing” it. This Euclidean intuition (inherited from the tangent to a circle) is wrong in general.

Why it fails: The parabola y=x2 at the origin. The x-axis touches the curve at exactly one point and doesn’t “cross” it — yet every introductory student correctly names y=0 as the tangent. But the cubic y=x3 at the origin: the x-axis does cross the curve, yet it is also the tangent line. The geometric intuition of “not crossing” is not the right criterion.

The correct approach is limiting. We must escape statics and enter dynamics.

§2. The Secant-to-Tangent Limit

Setup

Let f:R→R be a function. Fix a point P=(a,f(a)) on the graph.

Take a second point Q=(a+h,f(a+h)) on the graph, with h=0.

The line through P and Q is called a secant line. Its slope is:

mPQ​=hf(a+h)−f(a)​

This is the difference quotient — the single most important formula in all of calculus.

The Tangent Line

mtan​=h→0lim​hf(a+h)−f(a)​​

The tangent line at P=(a,f(a)) is the line through P with slope mtan​:

y−f(a)=mtan​(x−a)

This limit, when it exists, is defined to be the derivative f′(a).

§3. Worked Examples

Example 1 — The Parabola f(x)=x2

Find the tangent line at P=(1,1).

m=limh→0​h(1+h)2−12​=limh→0​h1+2h+h2−1​=limh→0​h2h+h2​

=limh→0​(2+h)=2

Tangent line: y−1=2(x−1), i.e., y=2x−1.

Notice: we cannot simply substitute h=0 into (2h+h2)/h — it’s 0/0. The algebraic cancellation of h is not a trick; it is the rigorous resolution of the indeterminate form. This is why limits are necessary.

Example 2 — The Square Root f(x)=x​

Find f′(a) for a>0.

m=limh→0​ha+h​−a​​

Rationalize by multiplying by a+h​+a​a+h​+a​​:

=limh→0​h(a+h​+a​)(a+h)−a​=limh→0​a+h​+a​1​=2a​1​

So f′(a)=2a​1​. The tangent slope decreases as a grows — the curve flattens.

Example 3 — Where the Limit Fails: f(x)=∣x∣ at x=0

limh→0+​h∣h∣−0​=limh→0+​hh​=1

limh→0−​h∣h∣−0​=limh→0−​h−h​=−1

The one-sided limits disagree. The derivative does not exist at x=0. The graph has a corner, and no unique tangent line can be assigned.

This is a canonical example of a continuous-but-not-differentiable point. Continuity is necessary but not sufficient for differentiability.

Slope as a Function

Once we compute f′(a) for every a, we obtain a new function — the derivative function f′(x). This is the profound insight: differentiating a function produces another function encoding the entire landscape of slopes.

f(x)f′(x)Geometric meaning
x22xSlope grows linearly; steeper as x increases
x​2x​1​Slope always positive but decreasing
sinxcosxSlope oscillates; zero at peaks/troughs
exexSlope equals height — exponential’s hallmark

§5. The Formal Definition (Rigorous Statement)

Definition (Stewart §2.7; MIT 18.02). Let f be defined on an open interval containing a. The derivative of f at a is

f′(a)=limh→0​hf(a+h)−f(a)​

provided the limit exists. If it does, f is said to be differentiable at a.

Alternative form (using x→a instead of h→0):

f′(a)=limx→a​x−af(x)−f(a)​

These are identical; the substitution x=a+h converts one to the other.

Theorem. Differentiability implies continuity. If f′(a) exists, then f is continuous at a.

Proof sketch: limh→0​[f(a+h)−f(a)]=limh→0​hf(a+h)−f(a)​⋅h=f′(a)⋅0=0

Hence limh→0​f(a+h)=f(a). ■

The converse fails: ∣x∣ is continuous at 0 but not differentiable.

§6. Velocity as the Same Limit

The tangent problem is isomorphic to the velocity problem. If s(t) is the position of a particle at time t, then:

v(a)=limh→0​hs(a+h)−s(a)​=s′(a)

The difference quotient hs(a+h)−s(a)​ is the average velocity over [a,a+h]. The derivative is the instantaneous velocity — the limit of average velocities over shrinking intervals.

This dual interpretation (geometric slope / physical rate of change) is why the derivative is the central object of calculus. It encodes how fast things change.

§7. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

Everything that follows is a direct descendant of the difference quotient.

7.1 The Loss Landscape

In supervised learning, we have a model parameterized by weights w∈Rn and a loss function L(w) measuring prediction error. Training means finding:

w∗=argminw​L(w)

To minimize L, we need to know its slope — that is, we need its gradient ∇w​L. Each partial derivative ∂wi​∂L​ is exactly the tangent problem applied to L in the wi​-direction.

7.2 Gradient Descent

The update rule for gradient descent is:

w(t+1)=w(t)−η∇w​L(w(t))

where η>0 is the learning rate. The gradient ∇w​L tells us the direction of steepest ascent; we step in the opposite direction to descend.

This is the tangent problem, iterated over millions of parameters.

Loss landscape (1D illustration):

  L(w)

  | *      *        *        *     *

           local min           global min

  At each w, the tangent slope = ∂L/∂w tells us 
  which direction to move. Gradient descent follows

7.3 Backpropagation is the Chain Rule

A deep neural network is a composition of functions:

y^​=fL​∘fL−1​∘⋯∘f1​(x)

To compute ∂wk​∂L​ for layer k, we apply the chain rule (itself a theorem about tangent lines of composite functions):

∂wk​∂L​=∂y^​∂L​⋅∂fL−1​∂y^​​⋯∂fk​∂fk+1​​⋅∂wk​∂fk​​

Backpropagation is just the chain rule applied systematically from output to input. Every tensor flowing backwards in a neural network is a derivative — a generalized tangent slope.

7.4 Numerical Differentiation (Finite Differences)

Before automatic differentiation existed, ML practitioners would approximate derivatives using the definition directly:

f′(a)≈hf(a+h)−f(a)​(forward difference)

f′(a)≈2hf(a+h)−f(a−h)​(central difference, more accurate)

The central difference is O(h2) error vs O(h) for forward difference — this comes from Taylor expansion. This is still used today to gradient-check neural network implementations: verify that autodiff gradients match finite difference approximations.

7.5 Activation Functions and Non-Differentiability

The ReLU activation σ(x)=max(0,x) is not differentiable at x=0 — exactly like ∣x∣ at 0. In practice, training proceeds anyway because:

  • The set x=0 has measure zero (the network almost never lands exactly there).
  • A subgradient (a generalization of derivative) is used: ∂σ(0)=[0,1].

The Leaky ReLU and smooth approximations (Swish, GELU) were invented precisely to fix this non-differentiability while preserving the computational advantages of ReLU.

§8. A Note on Notation

Various notations you will encounter, all meaning the same thing:

f′(a)=dxdf​​x=a​=dxd​f(x)​x=a​=Df(a)=f˙​(a) (physics, time derivative)

Leibniz notation dxdf​ is suggestive: it “looks like” the ratio ΔxΔf​ in the limit. This is not coincidence — it is why Leibniz notation is so powerful for the chain rule:

dxdy​=dudy​⋅dxdu​

The du‘s “cancel” (not rigorously, but the notation encodes the chain rule’s content).

§9. What Comes Next

The tangent problem motivates everything that follows:

  1. Differentiation rules — product, quotient, chain rule: so we never have to return to the limit definition.
  2. Linear approximation — f(x)≈f(a)+f′(a)(x−a): the tangent line as the best local approximation.
  3. Taylor series — approximation by polynomials to arbitrary order.
  4. Partial derivatives and gradients — tangent lines in higher-dimensional spaces.
  5. Optimization — critical points where f′(a)=0; second derivative test.
  6. Differential equations — equations involving f′, encoding physical and biological laws.

Every one of these threads pulls on the same knot: the limit of the difference quotient. Learn this well and the rest of calculus is commentary.
