04.One-Sided Limits

Apr 16, 202611 min read

“The limit does not care where you are — only where you are heading.”

Tags

#calculus #real-analysis #limits #continuity #deep-learning #machine-learning

0. Why Bother? (Motivation Before the Machine)

Before we write a single ε, let’s feel the problem.

Consider the function f(x)=x∣x∣​. Near x=0, the function does not oscillate wildly. It is perfectly calm on either side. To the left, f(x)=−1. To the right, f(x)=+1. And yet, no two-sided limit exists at x=0.

The classical (two-sided) limit asks: does the function approach a single value no matter how you approach a? One-sided limits ask a more refined question: what does the function approach if I restrict the direction of approach?

This is not a technicality. It is a fundamental distinction that separates differentiable functions from merely continuous ones, smooth activation functions from hard thresholds, and integrable functions from unruly ones.

The function approaches different values depending on the direction. Neither is wrong — they are simply different one-sided facts.

2. Formal Definitions

2.1 Left-Hand Limit (LHL)

Definition (Stewart §2.2; MIT 18.02 spirit). We say limx→a−​f(x)=L if for every ε>0, there exists δ>0 such that 0<a−x<δ⟹∣f(x)−L∣<ε.

Unpacking the notation.

  • x→a− means x<a and x→a. The superscript "−" denotes approach from the left (below).
  • The condition 0<a−x<δ encodes: x is strictly less than a, and within distance δ of a.
  • We require ∣f(x)−L∣<ε: the output is arbitrarily close to L.

2.2 Right-Hand Limit (RHL)

Definition. We say limx→a+​f(x)=L if for every ε>0, there exists δ>0 such that 0<x−a<δ⟹∣f(x)−L∣<ε.

  • x→a+ means x>a and x→a. The superscript "+" denotes approach from the right (above).
  • The condition 0<x−a<δ encodes: x is strictly greater than a, and within distance δ of a.

3. The Bridge Theorem

This theorem is the central link between one-sided and two-sided limits.

Theorem. The two-sided limit limx→a​f(x)=L exists if and only if both one-sided limits exist and are equal: limx→a−​f(x)=Landlimx→a+​f(x)=L.

Proof.

(⇒) Suppose limx→a​f(x)=L. Let ε>0. Then ∃δ>0 such that 0<∣x−a∣<δ⟹∣f(x)−L∣<ε. In particular, this holds when x∈(a−δ,a) (giving the LHL) and when x∈(a,a+δ) (giving the RHL). Hence both one-sided limits equal L.

(⇐) Suppose both one-sided limits equal L. Let ε>0. By hypothesis, ∃δ1​,δ2​>0 such that:

  • 0<a−x<δ1​⟹∣f(x)−L∣<ε
  • 0<x−a<δ2​⟹∣f(x)−L∣<ε

Set δ=min(δ1​,δ2​). Then for 0<∣x−a∣<δ, either x<a or x>a, and in both cases ∣f(x)−L∣<ε. Hence the two-sided limit exists. ■

Corollary. If limx→a−​f(x)=limx→a+​f(x), the two-sided limit does not exist.

Example 4.2 — The Heaviside Step Function

H(x)={0​x<0 1​x≥0​

limx→0−​H(x)=0,limx→0+​H(x)=1.

The two-sided limit does not exist. Note: H(0)=1 is defined, but it does not affect the limit analysis.

Remark. H(x) is the “hard threshold” activation function. It is not differentiable at 0, precisely because the one-sided limits disagree. This is why sigmoid and ReLU were introduced — to fix differentiability for gradient-based learning.

Example 4.3 — A Piecewise Function with Matching Limits

g(x)={x2+1​x<2 3x−1​x≥2​

limx→2−​g(x)=(2)2+1=5,limx→2+​g(x)=3(2)−1=5.

Since both agree, limx→2​g(x)=5. Moreover, g(2)=3(2)−1=5, so g is continuous at x=2.

Example 4.4 — An ε-δ Proof of a One-Sided Limit

Claim: limx→0+​x​=0.

Proof. Let ε>0. We need to find δ>0 such that 0<x−0<δ⟹∣x​−0∣<ε. Note ∣x​∣=x​ for x>0. We want x​<ε, i.e., x<ε2. Choose δ=ε2. Then 0<x<δ=ε2⟹x​<ε2​=ε.■

The function x​ only makes sense for x≥0, so a right-hand limit is the only sensible notion at x=0. One-sided limits are not just a technicality — sometimes they are the only thing that exists.

5. One-Sided Limits and Continuity

5.1 One-Sided Continuity

Definition. f is right-continuous at a if limx→a+​f(x)=f(a). f is left-continuous at a if limx→a−​f(x)=f(a).

f is (two-sided) continuous at a ⟺ f is both left- and right-continuous at a.

5.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus Connection

The Riemann integral is defined via step functions. The floor function ⌊x⌋ (greatest integer ≤x) satisfies: limx→n−​⌊x⌋=n−1,limx→n+​⌊x⌋=n,⌊n⌋=n.

So ⌊x⌋ is right-continuous but not left-continuous at every integer. This matters when defining integrals of piecewise functions.

7. Connection to Derivatives

The derivative at a point is a two-sided limit: f′(a)=limh→0​hf(a+h)−f(a)​.

We can define one-sided derivatives: f+′​(a)=limh→0+​hf(a+h)−f(a)​,f−′​(a)=limh→0−​hf(a+h)−f(a)​.

f is differentiable at a ⟺ f+′​(a)=f−′​(a).

Example: ReLU

The ReLU function σ(x)=max(0,x) satisfies: σ−′​(0)=0,σ+′​(0)=1. Since these disagree, ReLU is not differentiable at 0. This is precisely the “kink.” In practice, one chooses a subgradient (often 0 or 1) at this point for backpropagation.

8. Connection to AI / Machine Learning / Deep Learning

This is not decoration — one-sided limits sit at the mathematical heart of several core concepts.

8.1 Activation Functions and the Differentiability Problem

The transition from Heaviside → sigmoid → ReLU → GELU is precisely the story of trying to find activations that are (a) nonlinear, (b) computationally simple, and (c) differentiable or at least subdifferentiable everywhere. One-sided limits diagnose exactly where and why differentiability fails.

8.2 Backpropagation and the Chain Rule

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied layer by layer. The chain rule requires derivatives to exist. When activations have kinks (like ReLU), we use subgradients, which are defined via one-sided derivatives: ∂σ(x)={0​x<0 [0,1]​x=0 1​x>0​ The value chosen at x=0 is a convention — the mathematics says the one-sided derivatives disagree there.

8.3 Loss Functions and Piecewise Structure

The Hinge loss used in Support Vector Machines: ℓ(y,y^​)=max(0,1−yy^​) has a kink at yy^​=1. Computing its gradient requires understanding left- and right-hand derivatives at the kink point. One-sided limits are the language for this.

The Huber loss was designed to be smooth everywhere by matching the one-sided derivatives at the transition point: Lδ​(r)={21​r2​∣r∣≤δ δ(∣r∣−21​δ)​∣r∣>δ​ One can verify: limr→δ−​Lδ′​(r)=δ=limr→δ+​Lδ′​(r). The function is C1 by design.

8.4 The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) in Probabilistic Models

In probabilistic deep learning and Bayesian inference, the CDF FX​(t)=P(X≤t) is always right-continuous: limx→t+​FX​(x)=FX​(t). The left-hand limit limx→t−​FX​(x)=P(X<t) may differ at atoms of the distribution. This distinction matters in variational autoencoders, normalizing flows, and any model that works with explicit probability distributions.

8.5 Learning Rate Schedules

Cosine annealing and step decay schedules for learning rates are piecewise-defined functions. The one-sided continuity at “restart” points determines whether gradient updates are stable across restarts.

9. Common Pitfalls

⚠️ The value f(a) is irrelevant to the limit. One-sided limits depend only on the behavior of f near a, not at a.

⚠️ “Does not exist” is not the same as “equals infinity.” limx→0+​x1​=+∞ is a precise statement. The one-sided limit exists as an extended real number, even though it is not finite.

⚠️ Notation discipline. limx→a−​ and limx→a−​ (without the superscript) are different things in sloppy notation. Always write the superscript clearly: a−, a+.

⚠️ Domain matters. For f(x)=x​, asking for limx→0−​x​ is asking about a region outside the domain. The left-hand limit does not exist (not just equals zero).

11. Practice Problems

  1. (Computational) Let f(x)={x2−1​x<1 2x​x>1​. Compute limx→1−​f(x) and limx→1+​f(x). Does the two-sided limit exist?

  2. (Epsilon-delta) Prove rigorously that limx→3−​(2x+1)=7 using the ε-δ definition.

  3. (Conceptual) The function f(x)=sin(1/x) for x=0. Show that neither one-sided limit at x=0 exists.

  4. (Applied — ML) For the Leaky ReLU f(x)=max(0.01x,x), compute f−′​(0) and f+′​(0). Is the function differentiable at 0? What subgradient is typically used?

  5. (Proof) Let f be monotonically increasing and bounded above on (a,b). Prove that limx→b−​f(x) exists. (Hint: use the completeness of R and the supremum.)
