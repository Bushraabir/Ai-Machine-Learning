“The integral does not care whether you are weighing a lamina, locating a centroid, or training a neural network. It is always the same operation — accumulate, then normalize.”

§ 0 — The Unifying Idea

Every application in this note follows the same template:

Global quantity=∬D​(local density of that quantity),dA

The “local density” changes — mass density, charge density, probability density, squared-distance density — but the machinery is identical. Master the pattern once; apply it everywhere.

§ 1 — Mass and Density

Setup

A lamina is a thin flat plate occupying region D⊂R2. Its surface mass density ρ(x,y) (units: kg/m²) describes how mass is distributed: a small patch dA near (x,y) has mass ≈ρ(x,y),dA.

Definition

M=∬D​ρ(x,y),dA

If ρ is constant (uniform lamina), then M=ρ⋅A(D) — consistent with the elementary formula.

Example 1 — Variable density lamina

A triangular lamina occupies D=(x,y):0≤x≤1,,0≤y≤x with density ρ(x,y)=x+y.

Sketch of D: the region below the line y=x in the unit square; a right triangle with legs along the axes.

Mass:

M=∫01​∫0x​(x+y),dy,dx

Inner integral:

∫0x​(x+y),dy=[xy+2y2​]0x​=x2+2x2​=23x2​

Outer integral:

M=∫01​23x2​,dx=23​⋅31​=21​

§ 2 — Moments and Center of Mass

Motivation

The center of mass (xˉ,yˉ​) is the point at which the lamina would balance on a pin. It is the density-weighted average position.

Definitions

The first moments about the coordinate axes:

My​=∬D​x,ρ(x,y),dA(moment about the y-axis)

Mx​=∬D​y,ρ(x,y),dA(moment about the x-axis)

The center of mass:

xˉ=MMy​​=∬D​ρ,dA∬D​x,ρ,dA​,yˉ​=MMx​​=∬D​ρ,dA∬D​y,ρ,dA​

Why My​ uses x? The moment about the y-axis measures tendency to rotate around the y-axis — driven by perpendicular distance, which is the x-coordinate.

Special case: uniform density

When ρ= const, it cancels:

xˉ=A(D)∬D​x,dA​,yˉ​=A(D)∬D​y,dA​

This is the centroid — purely geometric, depending only on the shape of D.

Example 2 — Center of mass of Example 1’s lamina

Continuing with D=0≤x≤1,0≤y≤x, ρ=x+y, M=1/2.

Compute My​:

My​=∫01​∫0x​x(x+y),dy,dx

Inner: ∫0x​(x2+xy),dy=x2y+2xy2​​0x​=x3+2x3​=23x3​

Outer: ∫01​23x3​,dx=23​⋅41​=83​

Compute Mx​:

Mx​=∫01​∫0x​y(x+y),dy,dx

Inner: ∫0x​(xy+y2),dy=2xy2​+3y3​​0x​=2x3​+3x3​=65x3​

Outer: ∫01​65x3​,dx=65​⋅41​=245​

Center of mass:

xˉ=MMy​​=1/23/8​=43​,yˉ​=MMx​​=1/25/24​=125​

Sanity check: The lamina lives in 0≤y≤x≤1. The density ρ=x+y is heavier toward the corner (1,1). So xˉ should be above 1/2, and yˉ​ should be above the geometric centroid y=1/3 of the region. Both are: xˉ=3/4 ✓, yˉ​=5/12>1/3 ✓.

§ 3 — Moments of Inertia

Motivation

While the center of mass governs translational balance, the moment of inertia governs rotational inertia — resistance to angular acceleration. It is the rotational analogue of mass.

For a point mass m at distance r from the axis: I=mr2. For a distributed mass, integrate.

Definitions

Second moments (moments of inertia):

Ix​=∬D​y2,ρ(x,y),dA(about the x-axis, distance = ∣y∣)

Iy​=∬D​x2,ρ(x,y),dA(about the y-axis, distance = ∣x∣)

I0​=∬D​(x2+y2),ρ(x,y),dA(about the origin/z-axis)

Note: I0​=Ix​+Iy​ (the perpendicular axis theorem).

Radius of gyration x~ about the y-axis:

x~=MIy​​​

x~ is the distance from the axis at which all the mass, if concentrated, would give the same Iy​.

Example 3 — Disk with uniform density

Disk D:x2+y2≤R2, uniform density ρ.

By symmetry Ix​=Iy​, so compute I0​=∬D​r2ρ,dA in polar:

I0​=ρ∫02π​∫0R​r2⋅r,dr,dθ=ρ⋅2π⋅4R4​=2πρR4​

Since M=ρπR2:

I0​=2MR2​,Ix​=Iy​=4MR2​

These are the standard formulas used throughout engineering mechanics.

Example 4 — Rectangle, uniform density

Rectangle [0,a]×[0,b], uniform ρ. Mass M=ρab.

Ix​=ρ∫0a​∫0b​y2,dy,dx=ρ⋅a⋅3b3​=3ρab3​=3Mb2​

Iy​=3Ma2​

Why engineers care: I appears in τ=Iα (torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration) and in the deflection of beams under load. A wide, flat beam resists bending better — that is Ix​∝b3: doubling height multiplies bending resistance by 8.

§ 4 — Probability

Setup

A function f(x,y)≥0 is a joint probability density function (PDF) for continuous random variables X,Y if:

∬R2​f(x,y),dA=1

The probability that (X,Y) falls in a region D:

P((X,Y)∈D)=∬D​f(x,y),dA

Marginal Densities

Integrate out one variable to get the marginal:

fX​(x)=∫−∞∞​f(x,y),dy,fY​(y)=∫−∞∞​f(x,y),dx

Independence

X and Y are independent if and only if f(x,y)=fX​(x)⋅fY​(y) — the joint PDF factors.

Example 5 — Verifying a PDF and computing probability

Let f(x,y)=6xy2 on D=0≤x≤1,,0≤y≤1, zero elsewhere.

Verify: ∫01​∫01​6xy2,dy,dx=6∫01​x,dx∫01​y2,dy=6⋅21​⋅31​=1 ✓

Compute P(X>Y): the region is 0≤y≤x≤1.

P(X>Y)=∫01​∫0x​6xy2,dy,dx=∫01​6x⋅3x3​,dx=∫01​2x4,dx=52​

Example 6 — Bivariate normal (the fundamental distribution in ML)

The bivariate normal with independent components σX​=σY​=σ, means μX​=μY​=0:

f(x,y)=2πσ21​exp!(−2σ2x2+y2​)

This factors as fX​(x)⋅fY​(y) — confirming independence. To verify ∬f=1, switch to polar:

∫02π​∫0∞​2πσ21​e−r2/2σ2r,dr,dθ=σ21​∫0∞​r,e−r2/2σ2,dr

Substitute u=r2/2σ2: integral =σ2∫0∞​e−u,du=σ2. So σ21​⋅σ2=1 ✓

§ 5 — Expected Values

Definitions

For random variables (X,Y) with joint PDF f:

E[X]=∬R2​x,f(x,y),dA=∫−∞∞​x,fX​(x),dx

E[Y]=∬R2​y,f(x,y),dA

E[g(X,Y)]=∬R2​g(x,y),f(x,y),dA

Variance:

Var(X)=E[(X−E[X])2]=∬(x−μX​)2f(x,y),dA

Covariance:

Cov(X,Y)=E[(X−μX​)(Y−μY​)]=∬(x−μX​)(y−μY​),f(x,y),dA

Structural observation: Compare with § 2.

PhysicsProbability
Mass density ρ(x,y)Probability density f(x,y)
Total mass M=∬ρ,dATotal probability =1
First moment My​=∬xρ,dAE[X]=∬xf,dA
Moment of inertia Iy​=∬x2ρ,dAE[X2]=∬x2f,dA
Iy​/M=x~2E[X2]−μX2​=Var(X)

The physics and probability are the same mathematical structure with different interpretations of the weight function.

Example 7 — Expected value and variance

Using f(x,y)=6xy2 from Example 5:

E[X]: (use marginal fX​(x)=∫01​6xy2,dy=2x)

E[X]=∫01​x⋅2x,dx=2∫01​x2,dx=32​

E[Y]: (marginal fY​(y)=∫01​6xy2,dx=3y2)

E[Y]=∫01​y⋅3y2,dy=3∫01​y3,dy=43​

Var(X): E[X2]=∫01​x2⋅2x,dx=21​, so Var(X)=21​−(32​)2=21​−94​=181​

§ 7 — Applications in AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

7.1 — The Loss Function is a Moment Integral

The population risk (true loss) is:

R(θ)=E(x,y)∼p​[ℓ(fθ​(x),y)]=∬ℓ(fθ​(x),y),p(x,y),dx,dy

This is the first moment of the loss function under the data distribution. Minimizing R(θ) is the theoretical objective; empirical risk minimization (ERM) approximates this integral by a sample mean — a Monte Carlo estimate of the double integral.

7.2 — Variance and Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The bias-variance decomposition involves:

E[(fθ​(x)−y)2]=Bias2+Variance+σnoise2​

Each term is an integral of the form ∬(⋅)2,p(x,y),dx,dy — a second moment, exactly as Iy​=∬x2ρ,dA. The parallel is not a metaphor; the mathematics is identical.

7.3 — Covariance and Feature Correlation

The covariance matrix Σ of a feature vector (X1​,…,Xd​) has entries:

Σij​=Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=∬(xi​−μi​)(xj​−μj​),p(x),dx

In 2D: Cov(X,Y)=∬(x−μX​)(y−μY​),f(x,y),dA.

PCA finds the eigenvectors of Σ, which requires computing these double (or higher-dimensional) integrals from data. The empirical covariance matrix Σ^=N1​∑i​(xi​−xˉ)(xi​−xˉ)T is the Monte Carlo estimate of this integral.

7.4 — Gaussian Distributions are Everywhere

The multivariate Gaussian N(μ,Σ) with density:

f(x)=(2π)d/2∣Σ∣1/21​exp!(−21​(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ))

is defined by the condition ∬⋯∫f,dx=1. Verifying this uses the polar-coordinates trick from Example 6, generalized to d dimensions. Every forward pass through a Bayesian neural network, every Gaussian Process prediction, and every VAE latent space manipulation involves this integral implicitly.

7.5 — Center of Mass in Attention Mechanisms

The attention mechanism in Transformers computes:

Attn(Q,K,V)=softmax!(dk​​QKT​)V

The softmax produces a probability distribution (the weights), and the output is their weighted average of the values V. In the continuous limit (over a sequence modeled as a density), this is:

output(q)=∫α(q,k),v(k),dk,∫α(q,k),dk=1

This is precisely a center of mass computation: the output is the α-weighted average of the values v — the continuous analogue of xˉ=M1​∬xρ,dA.

7.6 — Moment of Inertia and Regularization

Weight regularization (L2/ridge) adds λ∣θ∣2 to the loss:

L(θ)+λ∑i​θi2​

The sum ∑θi2​ is a discrete second moment — the analogue of moment of inertia I=∑mi​ri2​. Regularization is the assertion: keep the parameter “mass” close to the origin. The continuous limit is λ∫θ2,p(θ),dθ — a 1D (or higher) moment of inertia.

7.7 — Probability Density Estimation

Kernel Density Estimation (KDE):

f^​(x,y)=Nh21​∑i=1N​K!(hx−xi​​,hy−yi​​)

estimates f(x,y) from data. The kernel K must satisfy ∬K=1 — a double integral normalization condition. Evaluating probabilities under f^​ over regions requires ∬D​f^​,dA, switching the double integral and the sum via Fubini.

7.8 — Score-Based Diffusion Models

Modern diffusion models (DDPM, score matching) learn ∇x​logp(x), the score function of the data density. The training objective involves:

Ex∼p,ϵ∼N​[∣sθ​(x+σϵ)−∇x​logpσ​(x)∣2]

Every expectation here is a double (or higher-dimensional) integral. The entire theoretical foundation — existence of scores, denoising score matching equivalence, probability flow ODEs — rests on properties of these integrals: their existence, Fubini-type interchanges, and moments of the Gaussian kernel.
