“The art of integration is choosing coordinates that respect the symmetry of the problem. Fight the geometry and you drown in radicals.”

0. Motivation — Why Bother?

Consider the integral:

∭E​x2+y2​,dV,E:x2+y2≤4,;0≤z≤3

In Cartesian coordinates this becomes:

∫−22​∫−4−x2​4−x2​​∫03​x2+y2​,dz,dy,dx

The y-limits are messy square roots, and x2+y2​ has no clean antiderivative in x or y.

In cylindrical coordinates, the same integral becomes:

∫02π​∫02​∫03​r⋅r,dz,dr,dθ=2π∫02​r2⋅3,dr=6π⋅38​=16π

Done in three lines. This is the entire point of cylindrical coordinates.

1. The Coordinate System

1.1 Definition

Cylindrical coordinates (,r,,θ,,z,) are simply polar coordinates in the xy-plane, extended by z:

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ,z=z​

with inverse relations:

r=x2+y2​,θ=arctan!(xy​),z=z

Domain conventions:

r≥0,0≤θ≤2π,z∈R

1.2 Geometric Picture

        z
        │
        │         P = (r, θ, z)
        │- - - - -●
        │         │
        │         │ z
        │         │
        └─────────┼──────── y
       /    θ    /
      /     ↙   / r
     /     /   /
    x─────●───/
          (r, θ, 0)

  r  = distance from z-axis (NOT origin)
  θ  = angle in xy-plane from +x axis
  z  = same height as Cartesian z

Key surfaces in cylindrical coordinates:

EquationSurface
r=c (constant)Cylinder of radius c about z-axis
θ=c (constant)Half-plane through z-axis
z=c (constant)Horizontal plane

2. The Volume Element — Deriving dV=r,dr,dθ,dz

This is the result students most often memorize without understanding. Let’s derive it properly via the Jacobian.

2.1 Jacobian Derivation

The transformation T:(r,θ,z)↦(x,y,z) has Jacobian matrix:

J=∂(r,θ,z)∂(x,y,z)​=(∂r∂x​​∂θ∂x​​∂z∂x​\[8pt]∂r∂y​​∂θ∂y​​∂z∂y​\[8pt]∂r∂z​​∂θ∂z​​∂z∂z​​)=(cosθ​−rsinθ​0 sinθ​rcosθ​0 0​0​1​)

Expanding along the third row:

detJ=1⋅det(cosθsinθ​−rsinθrcosθ​)=rcos2θ+rsin2θ=r

Since r≥0, we have ∣detJ∣=r. Therefore:

dV=r,dr,dθ,dz​

2.2 Geometric Derivation

The same result follows from geometry. Consider a small “cylindrical box” with increments dr, dθ, dz:

          dz
         ┌──────────────────┐
         │                  │
         │                  │  Height = dz
         │                  │
         └──────────────────┘
              ↑
         Base area = r·dθ · dr
         (arc length) × (radial thickness)

   ─────────────────────────────
       r·dθ is the arc length
       at radius r sweeping angle dθ
   ─────────────────────────────

   dV = (dr) × (r dθ) × (dz) = r dr dθ dz

The factor of r is not optional arithmetic — it reflects that at larger radius, the same angular increment dθ sweeps a longer arc r,dθ.

Warning. A common error is writing dV=dr,dθ,dz, forgetting the r. This would be dimensionally inconsistent: dr,dθ,dz has units length2, not length3.

3. The Integration Formula

Theorem. If E is a solid region described in cylindrical coordinates by:

E=(r,θ,z)∣α≤θ≤β,;g1​(θ)≤r≤g2​(θ),;u1​(r,θ)≤z≤u2​(r,θ)

and f is continuous on E, then:

∭E​f(x,y,z),dV=∫αβ​∫g1​(θ)g2​(θ)​∫u1​(r,θ)u2​(r,θ)​f(rcosθ,,rsinθ,,z)⋅r,dz,dr,dθ​

Mnemonic for limit order: Think of the integration as peeling from outside in:

  • θ: sweep the angle (full revolution = 0 to 2π)
  • r: at each angle, how far radially?
  • z: at each (r,θ), what is the vertical extent?

4. Strategy — When to Switch to Cylindrical

Switch to cylindrical coordinates when any of the following appear:

Signal in the ProblemReason
x2+y2 in integrand or limits=r2 in cylindrical; eliminates square roots
Cylindrical or conical boundariesr=c or z=cr are clean in cylindrical
Region of integration has circular cross-section in xyPolar limits for r and θ replace messy y-limits
Integrand is f(x2+y2​,z)Direct substitution r=x2+y2​

Do NOT use cylindrical when the region is box-shaped, tetrahedron, or bounded purely by planes — Cartesian is simpler.

5. Worked Examples

Example 5.1 — Volume of a Cylinder

Problem. Find the volume enclosed by x2+y2=9, z=0, z=5.

Setup. In cylindrical: r=3 is the boundary, z runs from 0 to 5.

V=∫02π​∫03​∫05​r,dz,dr,dθ

Inner (z): ∫05​dz=5

Middle (r): ∫03​5r,dr=5⋅2r2​​03​=245​

Outer (θ): ∫02π​245​,dθ=45π

V=45π​

Check: V=πr2h=π(9)(5)=45π ✓

Example 5.2 — Integrating Over a Solid Cylinder

Problem. Evaluate ∭E​(x2+y2),dV where E is the solid cylinder x2+y2≤4, −1≤z≤2.

Setup. x2+y2=r2. Limits: 0≤θ≤2π, 0≤r≤2, −1≤z≤2.

I=∫02π​∫02​∫−12​r2⋅r,dz,dr,dθ=∫02π​∫02​∫−12​r3,dz,dr,dθ

Inner (z): ∫−12​dz=3

Middle (r): ∫02​3r3,dr=3⋅4r4​​02​=3⋅4=12

Outer (θ): ∫02π​12,dθ=24π

I=24π​

Example 5.3 — Region Between a Paraboloid and a Plane

Problem. Find the volume of the solid E bounded above by z=4 and below by z=x2+y2 (a paraboloid).

Setup. In cylindrical: paraboloid becomes z=r2. The surfaces intersect when r2=4, i.e., r=2.

E:0≤θ≤2π,0≤r≤2,r2≤z≤4

V=∫02π​∫02​∫r24​r,dz,dr,dθ

Inner (z): ∫r24​dz=4−r2

Middle (r): ∫02​r(4−r2),dr=∫02​(4r−r3),dr=[2r2−4r4​]02​=8−4=4

Outer (θ): ∫02π​4,dθ=8π

V=8π​

Example 5.4 — Cone Inside a Sphere (Classic)

Problem. Find the volume of the solid above the cone z=x2+y2​ and inside the sphere x2+y2+z2=2.

Setup. In cylindrical: cone is z=r; sphere is r2+z2=2⇒z=2−r2​.

Intersection: r=2−r2​⇒r2=2−r2⇒r=1.

For 0≤r≤1: z runs from cone (z=r) up to sphere (z=2−r2​).

V=∫02π​∫01​∫r2−r2​​r,dz,dr,dθ

Inner (z): ∫r2−r2​​dz=2−r2​−r

Middle (r): ∫01​r(2−r2​−r)dr=∫01​r2−r2​,dr−∫01​r2,dr

For ∫01​r2−r2​,dr: let u=2−r2, du=−2r,dr: =−21​∫21​u​,du=21​∫12​u​,du=21​⋅32​[23/2−1]=322​−1​

For ∫01​r2,dr=31​.

Middle result: 322​−1​−31​=322​−2​

Outer (θ): V=2π⋅322​−2​=34π(2​−1)​​

Example 5.5 — Mass with Variable Density

Problem. A solid cylinder E:x2+y2≤1, 0≤z≤2 has density δ(x,y,z)=x2+y2​. Find total mass.

Setup. δ=r in cylindrical.

M=∫02π​∫01​∫02​r⋅r,dz,dr,dθ=∫02π​∫01​∫02​r2,dz,dr,dθ

Inner (z): ∫02​dz=2

Middle (r): ∫01​2r2,dr=32​

Outer (θ): ∫02π​32​,dθ=34π​

M=34π​​

Notice: density δ=r means the material is denser toward the outer edge (zero at center, maximum at r=1). The answer reflects this distribution.

Example 5.6 — Non-Full Revolution (Wedge Region)

Problem. Evaluate ∭E​z,dV where E is the region inside x2+y2=4, above z=0, below z=3, and in the first octant (x≥0, y≥0).

Setup. First octant means 0≤θ≤π/2.

I=∫0π/2​∫02​∫03​z⋅r,dz,dr,dθ

Inner (z): ∫03​z,dz=29​

Middle (r): ∫02​29​r,dr=29​⋅2=9

Outer (θ): ∫0π/2​9,dθ=29π​

I=29π​​

6. Setting Up Limits — A Systematic Procedure

This is where students struggle most. Here is a clean procedure:

Step 1: Identify the boundary surfaces

Write every bounding surface. Translate to cylindrical immediately.

Cartesian SurfaceCylindrical Form
x2+y2=R2r=R
x2+y2+z2=R2r2+z2=R2
z=x2+y2z=r2
z=x2+y2​z=r
z=cx2+y2​z=cr

Step 2: Find the projection onto the xy-plane (= r-θ plane)

Where does the solid project? This gives the range of r and θ.

  • Full revolution: 0≤θ≤2π
  • First octant only: 0≤θ≤π/2
  • Half-space y≥0: 0≤θ≤π

Step 3: Determine z-limits as functions of r (and possibly θ)

For a given (r,θ), what is the lowest and highest z in the solid?

  • zmin​=u1​(r,θ): lower bounding surface
  • zmax​=u2​(r,θ): upper bounding surface

Step 4: Determine r-limits

For a given θ, what is the radial extent?

  • r ranges from 0 (if region includes the z-axis) to g(θ)

Step 5: Write the integral

∫θmin​θmax​​∫rmin​(θ)rmax​(θ)​∫u1​(r,θ)u2​(r,θ)​f(rcosθ,rsinθ,z)⋅r,dz,dr,dθ

The small "brick" has:
  - radial thickness:   dr
  - arc length:         r·dθ   ← THIS is why the Jacobian is r

  Volume = dr × r·dθ × dz = r·dr·dθ·dz  ✓

MistakeWhy It’s WrongFix
Writing dV=dr,dθ,dzMissing the Jacobian factorAlways dV=r,dr,dθ,dz
r-limits from −R to Rr≥0 by definitionr runs from 0 to R (angle θ handles sign)
Forgetting r in the integrand toox2+y2​=r, not 1Substitute x,y,z entirely

9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

9.1 Radial Basis Functions and Cylindrical Symmetry

In kernel methods (Support Vector Machines, Gaussian Processes), the most common kernel is the Radial Basis Function (RBF):

k(x,x′)=exp!(−2σ2∣x−x′∣2​)

The kernel depends only on ∣x−x′∣ — it has radial symmetry, the same structure exploited by cylindrical coordinates. When computing integrals over kernel functions in R2 (e.g., marginalizing over a feature), polar/cylindrical coordinates are exactly what you use.

9.2 Monte Carlo in Cylindrical Domains

Training data for 3D computer vision (point clouds, LiDAR data) naturally lives in cylindrical domains — sensors sweep radially. Integrating a learned density p(r,θ,z) over a cylindrical region uses exactly this formula.

In 3D generative models (e.g., NeRF — Neural Radiance Fields), the expected color along a camera ray is computed as:

C(r)=∫0∞​T(t),σ(r(t)),c(r(t),d),dt

where T(t)=exp!(−∫0t​σ,ds). The underlying volume integration over the scene uses cylindrical and spherical parameterizations.

9.3 Moment of Inertia → Attention Heads

The moment of inertia about the z-axis:

Iz​=∭E​(x2+y2)ρ,dV=∫02π​∫r3ρ,dr,dz,dθ

is structurally identical to computing the variance of a radial distribution — which appears in attention mechanisms when the query-key similarity has rotational symmetry in the embedding space (as in RoPE — Rotary Position Embeddings).

9.4 Coordinate Systems in Equivariant Neural Networks

Equivariant neural networks (SE(3)-equivariant networks for molecular modeling, e.g., SE(3)-Transformers, DiffDock) explicitly work in spherical/cylindrical harmonic bases. The core insight: if a physical law is rotationally symmetric, the neural network architecture should respect that symmetry — and the natural basis for rotationally symmetric functions is exactly the one defined by the cylindrical/spherical coordinate decomposition.

The integration of features over these bases uses cylindrical volume elements r,dr,dθ,dz as the fundamental measure.

9.5 Practical Example — 3D Convolution in Cylindrical Coordinates

Standard 3D CNNs operate in Cartesian grids. For data with cylindrical symmetry (medical CT scans, radar, sonar), cylindrical CNNs discretize the volume in (r,θ,z) space. The sampling density must account for the Jacobian:

Output:

Numerical: 0.785297
Exact:     0.785398

The discrepancy is only numerical discretization error — and notice that R * dr * dtheta * dz is exactly the cylindrical volume element. Forgetting the R factor gives a wrong answer by an order of magnitude.

10. Summary

∭E​f(x,y,z),dV=∫αβ​∫r1​(θ)r2​(θ)​∫z1​(r,θ)z2​(r,θ)​f(rcosθ,,rsinθ,,z)⋅Jacobianr​​,dz,dr,dθ​

When to use: Region has circular/cylindrical cross-section, or integrand contains x2+y2.

The factor r: Comes from the Jacobian ∣∂(x,y,z)/∂(r,θ,z)∣=r; geometrically, it’s the arc length r,dθ at radius r.

Order of limits:

  1. z: lower surface to upper surface (may depend on r)
  2. r: inner radius to outer radius (may depend on θ)
  3. θ: starting angle to ending angle (constants)
