04.Fubini's Theorem

“The ability to switch the order of integration is one of the most quietly powerful facts in all of analysis. Use it without ceremony — but never without understanding.”

§ 0 — Why This Theorem Exists

Before we state anything, let us ask: what problem does Fubini solve?

Suppose you want to compute

∬R​f(x,y),dA

over some rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d]. The double integral is defined as a limit of Riemann sums over 2D partitions — conceptually clean, computationally brutal. What we want to do is reduce this to two ordinary 1D integrals, nested inside each other:

∫ab​(∫cd​f(x,y),dy)dxor∫cd​(∫ab​f(x,y),dx)dy

These are called iterated integrals. But why should they equal the double integral? And why should the two orderings give the same answer?

That is precisely what Fubini’s Theorem guarantees — under the right conditions.

§ 1 — Setup and Notation

Definition (Double Integral over a Rectangle). Let R=[a,b]×[c,d]⊂R2. Partition [a,b] into m subintervals and [c,d] into n subintervals, forming mn subrectangles Rij​ each of area ΔAij​=Δxi​⋅Δyj​. Pick sample points (x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_) in each. The double integral is:

\iint_R f(x,y), dA = \lim_{|P| \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} f(x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_), \Delta A_{ij}

where ∣P∣ is the norm of the partition (diameter of the largest subrectangle).

Definition (Iterated Integral). For fixed x, define g(x)=∫cd​f(x,y),dy (integrate in y first). The iterated integral integrating y first is:

∫ab​∫cd​f(x,y),dy,dx=∫ab​g(x),dx

Similarly, integrating x first:

∫cd​∫ab​f(x,y),dx,dy

§ 2 — The Theorem

Theorem (Fubini, rectangle version)

Let f be continuous on the rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d]. Then:

∬R​f(x,y),dA=∫ab​∫cd​f(x,y),dy,dx=∫cd​∫ab​f(x,y),dx,dy

Two things are being asserted simultaneously:

  1. The double integral equals each iterated integral (reducing 2D to nested 1D)
  2. The two orderings are equal (order of integration can be swapped freely)

Remark (continuity is sufficient, not necessary). Continuity is the condition Stewart emphasizes, and it is the right condition for a first encounter. The theorem holds under weaker hypotheses (Fubini’s full theorem in measure theory requires only that f be integrable in the Lebesgue sense — see § 6 for the general statement). Continuity on a closed rectangle is more than enough.

§ 3 — Proof Sketch (the honest version)

A full measure-theoretic proof belongs to a real analysis course. But let us trace the argument to see why it is true.

Step 1: Continuity implies uniform continuity. Since f is continuous on the compact set R, it is uniformly continuous. This ensures that for any ε>0, the iterated integrals are well-approximated by Riemann sums simultaneously in both variables.

Step 2: The inner integral defines a continuous function. Define

A(x)=∫cd​f(x,y),dy

Since f is continuous jointly in (x,y), for fixed x the function y↦f(x,y) is continuous (hence integrable), and A(x) is well-defined. Moreover, uniform continuity of f implies A(x) is continuous in x.

Step 3: Both equal the double integral. We use the uniform continuity to show that the Riemann sum approximations for ∫ab​A(x),dx and for ∬R​f,dA can be made to agree within ε. The key identity is:

\sum_i \sum_j f(x_i^_, y_j^_), \Delta x_i, \Delta y_j \approx \sum_i \left( \int_c^d f(x_i^*, y), dy \right) \Delta x_i \approx \int_a^b A(x), dx

Both approximation steps use uniform continuity. Taking ∣P∣→0 gives the result. ■

§ 4 — Examples

Example 1 — Polynomial integrand (warm-up)

∬R​(x2+2xy),dA,R=[0,1]×[1,2]

Integrating y first:

∫01​∫12​(x2+2xy),dy,dx

Inner integral (treat x as constant):

∫12​(x2+2xy),dy=[x2y+xy2]12​=(2x2+4x)−(x2+x)=x2+3x

Outer integral:

∫01​(x2+3x),dx=[3x3​+23x2​]01​=31​+23​=611​

Verify by switching order:

∫12​∫01​(x2+2xy),dx,dy

Inner integral:

∫01​(x2+2xy),dx=[3x3​+x2y]01​=31​+y

Outer integral:

∫12​(31​+y)dy=[3y​+2y2​]12​=(32​+2)−(31​+21​)=31​+23​=611​✓

Example 2 — Separable functions (the cleanest case)

Suppose f(x,y)=g(x)⋅h(y) on R=[a,b]×[c,d]. Then:

∬R​g(x)h(y),dA=(∫ab​g(x),dx)(∫cd​h(y),dy)

Proof:

∫ab​∫cd​g(x)h(y),dy,dx=∫ab​g(x)(∫cd​h(y),dy)dx=(∫cd​h(y),dy)∫ab​g(x),dx

The inner integral is a constant with respect to x, so it factors out. This is an extremely useful pattern — memorize it.

Concrete instance: ∬[0,π]×[0,1]​sin(x)⋅ey,dA=∫0π​sinx,dx⋅∫01​ey,dy=2⋅(e−1).

Example 3 — Switching order rescues a computation

Sometimes one iterated order is tractable and the other is not.

∫01​∫x1​ey2,dy,dx

Integrating y first (as written) requires evaluating ∫ey2,dy — a non-elementary integral. Stuck.

Switch the order. The region of integration is (x,y):0≤x≤1,,x≤y≤1, i.e., the triangle below the diagonal y=x in [0,1]2.

Describing this region with y as the outer variable: 0≤y≤1, 0≤x≤y.

∫01​∫0y​ey2,dx,dy=∫01​ey2⋅y,dy

Now substitute u=y2, du=2y,dy:

=21​∫01​eu,du=21​(e−1)

Lesson: Fubini does not just tell you that you can switch order — it gives you the freedom to switch when one order makes the problem tractable.

§ 5 — Extension to General Regions

The theorem extends beyond rectangles. For a Type I region D=(x,y):a≤x≤b,,g1​(x)≤y≤g2​(x):

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∫ab​∫g1​(x)g2​(x)​f(x,y),dy,dx

For a Type II region D=(x,y):c≤y≤d,,h1​(y)≤x≤h2​(y):

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∫cd​∫h1​(y)h2​(y)​f(x,y),dx,dy

The strategy is always: describe the region, identify which variable has fixed limits (the outer integral), and determine how the other variable’s bounds depend on the outer.

§ 6 — The Full Theorem (Measure-Theoretic Statement)

Fubini’s Theorem (general). Let (X,M,μ) and (Y,N,ν) be σ-finite measure spaces, and let f:X×Y→R be measurable. If

∫X×Y​∣f∣,d(μ×ν)<∞

(i.e., f∈L1(μ×ν)), then:

∫X×Y​f,d(μ×ν)=∫X​(∫Y​f(x,y),dν(y))dμ(x)=∫Y​(∫X​f(x,y),dμ(x))dν(y)

The Tonelli variant (for non-negative measurable f, no integrability hypothesis needed — equality holds though both sides may be +∞) is often used to check the integrability hypothesis before invoking Fubini.

The Classic Counterexample

Without the absolute integrability condition, Fubini fails. Consider [0,1]2 with:

f(x,y)=(x2+y2)2x2−y2​

One can verify: ∫01​∫01​f,dy,dx=4π​ but ∫01​∫01​f,dx,dy=−4π​.

The two iterated integrals disagree because ∫∫∣f∣,dA=∞. Fubini’s hypothesis is violated, and it shows.

The double integral computes the volume under the surface z=f(x,y) above the region R. Fubini says: slice the volume with planes x=const to get cross-sectional area A(y) (or B(x)), then integrate those areas.

7.2 — Cross-Section Mental Model

Imagine the solid (x,y,z):(x,y)∈R,,0≤z≤f(x,y).

  • Fix x=x0​. The cross-section is a 2D region in the yz-plane with area ∫cd​f(x0​,y),dy.
  • Slide x0​ from a to b. Integrating those cross-sectional areas gives the volume.

This is Cavalieri’s Principle in disguise. Fubini is the analytic rigorous version of: the volume is the integral of the cross-sectional areas.

§ 8 — Fubini in AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

This is not a digression. Fubini is everywhere in modern ML.

8.1 — Expectation and Joint Distributions

The expected value of a function ϕ(X,Y) under a joint distribution p(x,y) is:

E[ϕ(X,Y)]=∬ϕ(x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy

Fubini lets us marginalize in any order:

=∫(∫ϕ(x,y),p(x∣y),dx)p(y),dy=∫(∫ϕ(x,y),p(y∣x),dy)p(x),dx

Every time you condition on a variable and integrate out another, you are invoking Fubini.

8.2 — The ELBO in Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) involves:

L=Eq(z∣x)​[logp(x∣z)]−KL(q(z∣x)∣p(z))

The KL divergence term requires switching integration and differentiation in a way that is only valid because the integrands are sufficiently well-behaved — Fubini-type arguments underlie the interchange.

8.3 — Gradient of Expectations (REINFORCE, Score Function Estimator)

In reinforcement learning and variational inference, one needs:

∇θ​Epθ​(x)​[f(x)]=∇θ​∫f(x),pθ​(x),dx=∫f(x),∇θ​pθ​(x),dx

Interchanging the gradient and the integral is justified by the Leibniz integral rule — a direct corollary of Fubini applied to a product measure (treating θ as one coordinate, x as another). The score function trick ∇θ​logpθ​(x)=pθ​(x)∇θ​pθ​(x)​ is the payoff.

8.4 — Kernel Methods and Integral Operators

In kernel machines and Gaussian Processes, the kernel matrix entry is:

K(x,x′)=∫ϕ(x,ω),ϕ(x′,ω),p(ω),dω

(Random Fourier Features, Bochner’s theorem). Manipulating such integrals — bounding generalization error, computing posterior means — routinely requires switching integration order.

8.5 — Convolutional Neural Networks

The convolution operation at layer l:

(f∗g)(x)=∫f(x−y),g(y),dy

is itself an integral transform. Properties like associativity of convolution — essential to understanding deep network composition — follow from Fubini:

∫∫f(x−y),g(y−z),h(z),dy,dz=∫h(z)(∫f(x−y),g(y−z),dy)dz

The inner integral is a convolution; rearranging the order is Fubini.

8.6 — Normalizing Flows and Change of Variables

In normalizing flows, one models a complex distribution by a sequence of invertible transformations. The change of variables formula in multiple dimensions:

pX​(x)=pZ​(f−1(x))​detJf−1​(x)​

requires that the Jacobian determinant integrates correctly under the joint distribution — which is again a Fubini-type argument ensuring the integral of pX​ over Rn equals 1.

§ 9 — Summary and Working Rules

SituationWhat to do
Rectangle, f continuousApply Fubini freely in either order
Non-rectangular regionSketch the region; decide Type I or Type II; set up limits carefully
One order leads to non-elementary integralSwitch the order of integration
Verifying integrability (general f)Check ∬∥f∥<∞ first (Tonelli), then invoke Fubini
Counterexample riskRemember: switching order without absolute integrability can give wrong answers

The working habit: Always sketch the region before setting up any iterated integral. Getting the limits wrong is far more common than misapplying the theorem itself.
