01.Volumes and Double Integrals

“The single integral measures length swept into area. The double integral measures area swept into volume. The pattern continues — and so does the wonder.”

0. Preamble — Motivation

The definite integral ∫ab​f(x),dx computes the signed area between y=f(x) and the x-axis. Now we have a function f(x,y) of two variables — its graph is a surface z=f(x,y) floating above the xy-plane.

The question: What is the volume of the solid between this surface and the xy-plane, over a region D?

The answer is the double integral ∬D​f(x,y),dA, and building it from scratch — through the same limiting process as the single integral — is what this note is about.

1. The Riemann Sum Construction

1.1 Setting Up the Approximation

Let f:D→R be defined on a closed rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d]. Divide:

  • [a,b] into m subintervals of width Δx=mb−a​, with partition points x0​=a<x1​<⋯<xm​=b
  • [c,d] into n subintervals of width Δy=nd−c​, with partition points y0​=c<y1​<⋯<yn​=d

This creates mn sub-rectangles Rij​=[xi−1​,xi​]×[yj−1​,yj​], each with area: ΔA=Δx⋅Δy

1.2 The Sample Points

In each Rij​, choose a sample point (x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_). The value f(x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_) is the height of the surface at that point. The volume of the thin box (column) over Rij​ is approximately: \text{Volume of box}_{ij} \approx f(x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_) \cdot \Delta A

1.3 The Double Riemann Sum

Summing over all sub-rectangles: V \approx \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} f(x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_),\Delta A

This is the double Riemann sum. As m,n→∞ (boxes get thinner), the approximation becomes exact.

1.4 The Double Integral — Definition

Definition. If the limit exists and is the same regardless of choice of sample points, then f is integrable over R and: \iint_R f(x,y),dA = \lim_{m,n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(x_{ij}^_, y_{ij}^_),\Delta A

Theorem. If f is continuous on R, then f is integrable over R.

Geometric meaning when f≥0: ∬R​f(x,y),dA is the volume of the solid between the surface z=f(x,y) and the rectangle R in the xy-plane.

When f takes negative values: Like the single integral, the double integral computes signed volume — regions where f<0 contribute negatively.

2. Fubini’s Theorem — How to Actually Compute

The definition via limits is impractical. Fubini’s theorem converts a double integral into two successive single integrals.

2.1 The Theorem

Theorem (Fubini). If f is continuous on the rectangle R=[a,b]×[c,d], then: ∬R​f(x,y),dA=∫ab​∫cd​f(x,y),dy,dx=∫cd​∫ab​f(x,y),dx,dy

Both iterated integrals give the same answer. The order of integration can be swapped freely (when f is continuous).

2.2 The Idea Behind Fubini — Cavalieri’s Principle

Fix x∈[a,b]. The cross-sectional area of the solid at position x is: A(x)=∫cd​f(x,y),dy (treat x as a constant, integrate over y).

The volume is then: V=∫ab​A(x),dx=∫ab​[∫cd​f(x,y),dy]dx

This is Cavalieri’s principle: volume = integral of cross-sectional areas. Fubini’s theorem is the rigorous version.

2.3 Notation

The iterated integral is written: ∫ab​∫cd​f(x,y),dy,dx

Read from the inside out:

  1. Inner integral: ∫cd​f(x,y),dy — integrate with respect to y, treating x as a constant. Result is a function of x.
  2. Outer integral: ∫ab​[⋯],dx — integrate the result with respect to x.

2.4 Worked Example — Rectangle

Compute ∬R​(x2+2xy),dA where R=[0,2]×[1,3].

Method (integrate y first, then x):

∫02​∫13​(x2+2xy),dy,dx

Inner integral (x fixed): ∫13​(x2+2xy),dy=[x2y+xy2]13​=(3x2+9x)−(x2+x)=2x2+8x

Outer integral: ∫02​(2x2+8x),dx=[32x3​+4x2]02​=316​+16=364​

Verify by reversing order (integrate x first, then y):

∫13​∫02​(x2+2xy),dx,dy

Inner integral (y fixed): ∫02​(x2+2xy),dx=[3x3​+x2y]02​=38​+4y

Outer integral: ∫13​(38​+4y)dy=[38y​+2y2]13​=(8+18)−(38​+2)=26−314​=364​✓

3. Double Integrals Over General Regions

Most domains of interest are not rectangles. We integrate over general regions by describing the boundary carefully.

3.1 Type I Regions (vertical slices)

Definition. D is a Type I region if: D=(x,y):a≤x≤b,;g1​(x)≤y≤g2​(x)

The y-limits depend on x — the region lies between two curves y=g1​(x) (bottom) and y=g2​(x) (top).

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∫ab​∫g1​(x)g2​(x)​f(x,y),dy,dx

Example. The region bounded by y=x2 and y=2x is Type I with a=0, b=2 (intersection points: x2=2x⟹x=0,2), g1​(x)=x2, g2​(x)=2x.

∬D​f,dA=∫02​∫x22x​f(x,y),dy,dx

3.2 Type II Regions (horizontal slices)

Definition. D is a Type II region if: D=(x,y):c≤y≤d,;h1​(y)≤x≤h2​(y)

The x-limits depend on y — the region lies between x=h1​(y) (left) and x=h2​(y) (right).

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∫cd​∫h1​(y)h2​(y)​f(x,y),dx,dy

3.3 Choosing the Order of Integration

Some integrals are impossible (or very hard) in one order but elementary in the other. The strategy is:

Sketch the region D. Identify which description (Type I or Type II) produces integrable limits. Try the other order if the inner integral cannot be evaluated in closed form.

Example of switching order. Consider ∫01​∫x1​ey2,dy,dx.

ey2 has no elementary antiderivative in y — we cannot evaluate the inner integral directly.

Switch order. The region is 0≤x≤y,;0≤y≤1. As a Type II region: 0≤y≤1, 0≤x≤y.

∫01​∫0y​ey2,dx,dy=∫01​[xey2]0y​dy=∫01​yey2,dy=[2ey2​]01​=2e−1​

4. Volume as a Double Integral

The volume of the solid bounded above by z=f(x,y)≥0 and below by the region D in the xy-plane is:

V=∬D​f(x,y),dA

This is the defining geometric interpretation.

4.1 Volume Between Two Surfaces

If f(x,y)≥g(x,y) on D, the volume between the two surfaces z=f(x,y) (top) and z=g(x,y) (bottom) is: V=∬D​[f(x,y)−g(x,y)],dA

4.2 Full Worked Example — Volume Under a Paraboloid

Problem. Find the volume of the solid bounded above by z=4−x2−y2 and below by the xy-plane, over the region where z≥0.

The surface meets the xy-plane where 4−x2−y2=0, i.e., on the circle x2+y2=4. So D=x2+y2≤4.

V=∬D​(4−x2−y2),dA

Describe D as Type I: −2≤x≤2, −4−x2​≤y≤4−x2​.

V=∫−22​∫−4−x2​4−x2​​(4−x2−y2),dy,dx

Inner integral: ∫−4−x2​4−x2​​(4−x2−y2),dy=[(4−x2)y−3y3​]−4−x2​4−x2​​

Let r2=4−x2. By symmetry in y: =2[(4−x2)4−x2​−3(4−x2)3/2​]=2(4−x2)3/2[1−31​]=34​(4−x2)3/2

Outer integral: V=∫−22​34​(4−x2)3/2,dx

Substitute x=2sinθ, dx=2cosθ,dθ, 4−x2​=2cosθ, limits θ∈[−π/2,π/2]:

V=∫−π/2π/2​34​(2cosθ)3⋅2cosθ,dθ=34​⋅16∫−π/2π/2​cos4θ,dθ

Using ∫−π/2π/2​cos4θ,dθ=83π​:

V=364​⋅83π​=8π

(This result is more elegantly obtained using polar coordinates — see §6.)

5. Properties of the Double Integral

These mirror the single-variable properties exactly:

Linearity: ∬D​[f+g],dA=∬D​f,dA+∬D​g,dA,∬D​cf,dA=c∬D​f,dA

Additivity over regions: If D=D1​∪D2​ with D1​∩D2​ having zero area: ∬D​f,dA=∬D1​​f,dA+∬D2​​f,dA

Monotonicity: If f≥g on D, then ∬D​f,dA≥∬D​g,dA.

Area formula: If f≡1: ∬D​1,dA=Area(D)

Average value: fˉ​=Area(D)1​∬D​f(x,y),dA

6. Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

When D is a disk, annulus, or sector — or when f involves x2+y2 — polar coordinates simplify the integral dramatically.

Transformation: x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, with the crucial factor: dA=r,dr,dθ

⚠️ The factor r is not optional. It arises from the Jacobian of the transformation: ∂(r,θ)∂(x,y)​=r. A polar area element is a thin wedge of area r,dr,dθ, not dr,dθ.

Formula: ∬D​f(x,y),dA=∫αβ​∫r1​(θ)r2​(θ)​f(rcosθ,rsinθ)⋅r,dr,dθ

Sector, angle α to β0≤r≤a, α≤θ≤β

6.2 Revisiting the Paraboloid Volume

V=∬x2+y2≤4​(4−x2−y2),dA=∫02π​∫02​(4−r2),r,dr,dθ

=∫02π​dθ⋅∫02​(4r−r3),dr=2π[2r2−4r4​]02​=2π(8−4)=8π✓

Far cleaner than Cartesian.

6.3 The Gaussian Integral — A Famous Application

Double integrals in polar coordinates give one of the most celebrated results in all of mathematics:

(∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx)2=∬R2​e−(x2+y2),dA=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2r,dr,dθ=2π⋅21​=π

Therefore: ∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π​

This integral is the backbone of probability theory (it normalizes the Gaussian distribution) and appears throughout physics and ML.

8. Changing the Order of Integration — Strategy Guide

Step 1. Identify the region D from the given limits:

  • Inner limits of y: y goes from ϕ1​(x) to ϕ2​(x)
  • Outer limits of x: x goes from a to b
  • Sketch the region D.

Step 2. Re-describe D with the roles of x and y swapped:

  • New y range (outer): read off the global y extent from the sketch
  • New x range (inner): express x bounds as functions of y

Step 3. Write the new iterated integral and evaluate.

Example. Reverse the order: ∫01​∫y1​xsinx​,dx,dy.

Region: 0≤y≤x,;0≤x≤1 (triangle below y=x).

As Type I: 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤x.

∫01​∫0x​xsinx​,dy,dx=∫01​xsinx​⋅x,dx=∫01​sinx,dx=[−cosx]01​=1−cos1

9. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

9.1 Probability Distributions — The Core Application

In ML, every probabilistic model involves a probability density function (pdf) p(x), which satisfies p≥0 and: ∬R2​p(x,y),dx,dy=1

This normalization condition is a double integral. The Gaussian (normal) distribution in 2D is: p(x,y)=2πσ1​σ2​1​exp!(−2σ12​x2​−2σ22​y2​)

That this integrates to 1 is precisely a consequence of the Gaussian integral ∫e−x2,dx=π​ proved via polar coordinates in §6.3.

Expected values. If X,Y are continuous random variables with joint density p(x,y): E[f(X,Y)]=∬f(x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy

Every expectation in probabilistic ML — expected loss, expected reward in RL, variational lower bound in VAEs — is a (high-dimensional) integral of this form.

9.2 The Loss Function as an Expected Double Integral

In supervised learning, the population risk (expected loss over the true data distribution p(x,y)) is: R(f)=E(x,y)∼p​,[ℓ(f(x),y)]=∬ℓ(f(x),y),p(x,y),dx,dy

Training minimizes the empirical version (a Riemann sum approximation!): R^(f)=n1​∑i=1n​ℓ(f(xi​),yi​)≈R(f)

The law of large numbers says the empirical risk converges to the true integral as n→∞. The double Riemann sum is not just an approximation scheme for volumes — it is the mathematical model for how a training set approximates the underlying data distribution.

9.3 Monte Carlo Integration — The ML Workhorse

In high dimensions (n≫2), iterated integrals are computationally infeasible — the cost grows as Nn for a grid of N points per dimension. ML uses Monte Carlo integration instead:

∬f(x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy≈N1​∑i=1N​f(xi​,yi​),(xi​,yi​)∼p

This is a stochastic Riemann sum: sample points from p, evaluate f, average. Error scales as 1/N​ regardless of dimension — this dimension-independence is why Monte Carlo is used universally in ML (MCMC, variational inference, policy gradient in RL).

The whole theory — why MC works, its variance, importance sampling — rests on the definition of the integral as a limit of Riemann sums.

9.4 Convolutional Neural Networks — Convolution as an Integral

The convolution operation (which gives CNNs their name) is, in continuous form: (f∗g)(x)=∫−∞∞​f(x−t),g(t),dt

In 2D (image processing): (f∗g)(x,y)=∬f(x−s,,y−t),g(s,t),ds,dt

A convolutional layer computes a discrete approximation of this — a double Riemann sum. Every convolution in deep learning is a double integral in disguise.

9.5 The Marginal Distribution — Integrating Out Variables

Given a joint distribution p(x,y), the marginal over x is: p(x)=∫p(x,y),dy

This is the fundamental operation in latent variable models (VAEs, GMMs, hidden Markov models): to compute the probability of observed data x, integrate out the latent variables y. The integral is usually intractable in closed form — approximating it is the central challenge of variational inference, which replaces the integral with an optimization problem.
