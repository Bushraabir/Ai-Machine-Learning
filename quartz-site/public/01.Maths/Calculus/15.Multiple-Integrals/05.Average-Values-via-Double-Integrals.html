“The average is the single number that, spread uniformly over the region, produces the same total. Everything else is bookkeeping.”

§ 0 — Motivation: From 1D to 2D

Recall the average value of a function f(x) on [a,b] from single-variable calculus:

favg​=b−a1​∫ab​f(x),dx

The denominator b−a is the measure (length) of the domain. The integral is the total accumulation. Their ratio is the average.

In two dimensions, nothing changes philosophically. The domain now has area instead of length, and we integrate over a region D⊂R2 instead of an interval. The formula becomes:

favg​=A(D)1​∬D​f(x,y),dA​

where A(D)=∬D​dA is the area of D.

Intuition: Imagine pouring water whose depth at (x,y) is f(x,y) into a flat-bottomed container of the same footprint D. The water redistributes to a uniform depth favg​. The total volume is conserved:

favg​⋅A(D)=∬D​f(x,y),dA

§ 1 — Formal Definition

Definition. Let f be integrable on a bounded region D⊂R2 with area A(D)>0. The average value of f over D is:

favg​=A(D)1​∬D​f(x,y),dA

Remarks:

  • A(D) is itself a double integral: A(D)=∬D​1,dA.
  • If f is continuous and D is connected, the Mean Value Theorem for Double Integrals guarantees existence of a point (x0​,y0​)∈D with f(x0​,y0​)=favg​ (see § 4).
  • The definition extends immediately to Rn: replace area with n-dimensional volume.

§ 2 — Mean Value Theorem for Double Integrals

Theorem

Let f be continuous on a closed, bounded, connected region D. Then there exists at least one point (x0​,y0​)∈D such that:

f(x0​,y0​)=A(D)1​∬D​f(x,y),dA=favg​

Proof

Since f is continuous on compact D, it attains its minimum m and maximum M:

m≤f(x,y)≤M∀,(x,y)∈D

Integrating over D:

m⋅A(D)≤∬D​f,dA≤M⋅A(D)

Dividing by A(D):

m≤favg​A(D)1​∬D​f,dA​​≤M

So favg​∈[m,M]. Since f is continuous on connected D, by the Intermediate Value Theorem, f takes every value between m and M. In particular, there exists (x0​,y0​)∈D with f(x0​,y0​)=favg​. ■

Note: The MVT guarantees existence of such a point, but not uniqueness. There may be many such points, or (for non-connected D) none at all.

§ 3 — Computing Average Values: Full Examples

Example 1 — Average of a polynomial over a rectangle

Problem. Find the average value of f(x,y)=x2y+3y2 over R=[0,2]×[0,1].

Step 1: Area.

A(R)=(2−0)(1−0)=2

Step 2: Double integral.

∬R​(x2y+3y2),dA=∫02​∫01​(x2y+3y2),dy,dx

Inner integral (x fixed):

∫01​(x2y+3y2),dy=[2x2y2​+y3]01​=2x2​+1

Outer integral:

∫02​(2x2​+1)dx=[6x3​+x]02​=68​+2=34​+2=310​

Step 3: Average.

favg​=21​⋅310​=35​

Example 2 — Average temperature over a triangular region

Problem. The temperature at (x,y) is T(x,y)=4x+2y (in °C). Find the average temperature over the triangle D with vertices (0,0), (2,0), (0,4).

Step 1: Describe D.

The hypotenuse has equation 2x​+4y​=1, i.e., y=4−2x. So:

D=(x,y):0≤x≤2,;0≤y≤4−2x

Step 2: Area.

A(D)=21​⋅base⋅height=21​(2)(4)=4

(Or via integration: ∫02​(4−2x),dx=[4x−x2]02​=4.)

Step 3: Double integral.

∬D​(4x+2y),dA=∫02​∫04−2x​(4x+2y),dy,dx

Inner integral:

∫04−2x​(4x+2y),dy=[4xy+y2]04−2x​=4x(4−2x)+(4−2x)2

=(4−2x)[4x+(4−2x)]=(4−2x)(4+2x)=16−4x2

Outer integral:

∫02​(16−4x2),dx=[16x−34x3​]02​=32−332​=364​

Step 4: Average.

Tavg​=41​⋅364​=316​≈5.33∘C

Example 3 — Average over a disk (polar coordinates)

Problem. Find the average value of f(x,y)=x2+y2 over the disk D:x2+y2≤R2.

Step 1: Area.

A(D)=πR2

Step 2: Switch to polar. x2+y2=r2, dA=r,dr,dθ.

∬D​(x2+y2),dA=∫02π​∫0R​r2⋅r,dr,dθ=∫02π​dθ∫0R​r3,dr

=2π⋅4R4​=2πR4​

Step 3: Average.

favg​=πR21​⋅2πR4​=2R2​

Sanity check: f=r2 ranges from 0 (center) to R2 (boundary). The average R2/2 lies strictly between them. ✓

Geometric insight: The average is NOT R2/2 because of a naive “midpoint” argument — it is R2/2 because points near the boundary carry more area weight (circumference 2πr grows with r), pulling the average above the naive midpoint R2/2… wait, here the naive midpoint is R2/2. But in general, the area-weighted average shifts the answer. For f=r over the disk: favg​=2R/3=R/2 — the boundary-heavy weighting pulls the average toward the rim.

4.2 — Locating the Mean Value Point

For Example 2 (T=4x+2y over the triangle), the average was 16/3. The MVT guarantees a point with 4x0​+2y0​=16/3 inside D. This is a line segment; any point on it inside D works. The theorem asserts existence — it does not pinpoint a unique location.

4.3 — Effect of Region Shape on the Average

For f(x,y)=x (temperature increasing left-to-right):

Region DA(D)∬x,dAfavg​
Square [0,1]211/21/2
Triangle: vertices (0,0),(1,0),(0,1)1/21/61/3
Triangle: vertices (0,0),(1,0),(1,1)1/25/125/6

The shape of D shifts the average by changing how much area lies near large vs. small values of f — the geometric analogue of a weighted average.

§ 5 — Weighted Average and Probability Density

When the region carries a weight function w(x,y)≥0 (a probability density, mass density, etc.), the weighted average is:

⟨f⟩w​=∬D​w(x,y),dA∬D​f(x,y),w(x,y),dA​

The standard average is the special case w≡1.

Center of mass is a weighted average of position:

xˉ=∬D​ρ(x,y),dA∬D​x,ρ(x,y),dA​,yˉ​=∬D​ρ(x,y),dA∬D​y,ρ(x,y),dA​

where ρ is the surface mass density.

§ 6 — Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

6.1 — Expected Value IS a Weighted Average Integral

The expected value of a function ϕ(x,y) under a joint density p(x,y):

E(X,Y)∼p​[ϕ(X,Y)]=∬R2​ϕ(x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy

is precisely a weighted average with weight w=p. The denominator ∬p,dA=1 (probability axiom). Every time you compute an expectation in ML, you are computing the weighted average value of a function over its domain.

6.2 — Loss Function as Average Loss

The empirical risk (training loss) in ML is a discrete average:

L(θ)=N1​∑i=1N​ℓ(y^​i​,yi​)

Its continuous population analogue is exactly the average value integral:

L(θ)=∬ℓ(fθ​(x),y),p(x,y),dx,dy

The difference between training loss (sample average) and population loss (integral average) is generalization error — and bounding it requires understanding how well discrete sums approximate double integrals (uniform laws of large numbers, VC theory).

6.3 — Batch Normalization

Batch normalization computes the mean and variance of activations over the spatial dimensions of a feature map. For a H×W feature map F:

μ=HW1​∑i=1H​∑j=1W​Fij​

This is a discrete double average — the exact discretization of A1​∬F(x,y),dA over the spatial domain. The continuous formulation is used when deriving the theory of spatial statistics and analyzing convolutional architectures.

6.4 — Gaussian Process Posterior Mean

In a Gaussian Process, the posterior mean function is:

μ∗(x)=∫k(x,x′),α(x′),dx′

where k is the kernel and α encodes the training data. Over a 2D input space (x1​,x2​), this is a weighted average value of α with weight kernel k — a double integral of exactly the form § 5 above.

6.5 — Spatial Pooling in CNNs

Global Average Pooling (GAP) — common in modern architectures (ResNet, GoogLeNet) — replaces the final fully-connected layer with:

GAP(F(c))=H⋅W1​∑i,j​Fij(c)​

for channel c. This is literally the discrete average value of the feature map over the spatial domain. The motivation: compute the average activation of each feature detector across the entire image. The continuous limit is favg​=A1​∬F(c)(x,y),dA.

GAP reduces a 3D tensor to a vector while being translation-invariant — a property that follows from the invariance of the average value under rearrangement of the domain (Fubini + change of variables).

6.6 — Monte Carlo Integration

The average value formula is the basis of Monte Carlo integration. To compute ∬D​f,dA:

  1. Sample N points (xi​,yi​) uniformly from D.
  2. Estimate: ∬D​f,dA≈A(D)⋅N1​∑i=1N​f(xi​,yi​)

Because the sample mean N1​∑f(xi​,yi​) is an unbiased estimator of favg​=A(D)1​∬f,dA, by the Law of Large Numbers.

Monte Carlo integration is the workhorse of:

  • Bayesian posterior approximation (MCMC sampling)
  • Policy gradient methods in RL (estimating Eπ​[R])
  • Training diffusion models (DDPM, score matching objectives)
  • Dropout (averaging over random subnetworks at test time)

The error scales as O(1/N​) regardless of dimension — this dimension-independence is why MC dominates for high-dimensional integrals, while deterministic quadrature degrades exponentially (the curse of dimensionality).
