“When the exact integral is out of reach, the midpoint rule gives you the best possible rectangular approximation — and tells you precisely how wrong you are.”

0. Preamble — Why Approximate?

The machinery of Fubini’s theorem computes ∬D​f(x,y),dA exactly — but only when f has a closed-form antiderivative. In practice:

  • f(x,y) may be given only as tabular data (sensor readings, simulation outputs)
  • The antiderivative of f may not exist in closed form (e.g., f=e−x2−y2 over a square)
  • The domain D may be geometrically irregular

In all these cases, we need a numerical method. The midpoint rule is the simplest and most natural — it is the double Riemann sum with a specific, optimal choice of sample points.

1. Recall — The Single-Variable Midpoint Rule

For ∫ab​f(x),dx, divide [a,b] into n subintervals of width Δx=nb−a​, with midpoints xˉi​=a+(i−21​)Δx:

Mn​=∑i=1n​f(xˉi​),Δx≈∫ab​f(x),dx

The midpoint is the optimal sample point for a rectangle because it cancels the leading error term (the linear part of f over the subinterval integrates to zero when sampled at the center).

2. The Midpoint Rule for Double Integrals — Setup

2.1 Partition the Rectangle

Let R=[a,b]×[c,d]. Divide:

  • [a,b] into m equal subintervals of width Δx=mb−a​
  • [c,d] into n equal subintervals of width Δy=nd−c​

This produces mn sub-rectangles Rij​=[xi−1​,xi​]×[yj−1​,yj​], each with area: ΔA=Δx⋅Δy

2.2 The Midpoints

The midpoint of the subinterval [xi−1​,xi​] is: xˉi​=2xi−1​+xi​​=a+(i−21​)Δx,i=1,…,m

The midpoint of the subinterval [yj−1​,yj​] is: yˉ​j​=2yj−1​+yj​​=c+(j−21​)Δy,j=1,…,n

The center of the sub-rectangle Rij​ is (xˉi​,yˉ​j​).

2.3 The Midpoint Rule

Definition (Midpoint Rule for Double Integrals). The midpoint rule approximation is:

Mmn​=i=1∑m​j=1∑n​f(xˉi​,yˉ​j​),ΔA​

where ΔA=Δx⋅Δy, and (xˉi​,yˉ​j​) is the center of the sub-rectangle Rij​.

Geometric meaning. Over each sub-rectangle Rij​, we build a rectangular column (box) whose height is the value of f at the center of the base. The midpoint rule sums the volumes of all these columns.

  z
  │       ┌─────┐   ← height = f(x̄ᵢ, ȳⱼ)
  │       │     │
  │       │     │
  └───────┴─────┴────── xy-plane
          R_{ij}
          ↑ center = (x̄ᵢ, ȳⱼ)

3. Why Midpoints? — The Error Analysis

3.1 Intuition from 1D

Over a single subinterval [xi−1​,xi​], expand f in a Taylor series about the midpoint xˉi​: f(x)=f(xˉi​)+f′(xˉi​)(x−xˉi​)+2f′′(xˉi​)​(x−xˉi​)2+⋯

Integrating over [xi−1​,xi​]: ∫xi−1​xi​​f(x),dx=f(xˉi​)Δx+f′(xˉi​)=,0 (odd, symmetric)∫xi−1​xi​​(x−xˉi​),dx​​+O((Δx)3)

The linear error term vanishes because (x−xˉi​) is an odd function on [xi−1​,xi​] centered at xˉi​. This is why the midpoint is better than an endpoint: it kills the O((Δx)2) error per interval, leaving only O((Δx)3) per interval.

The same cancellation happens in 2D over each Rij​.

3.2 Error Bound for the Midpoint Rule

Theorem. If ∣fxx​∣,∣fyy​∣≤K on R=[a,b]×[c,d], then the error in the midpoint rule satisfies: ∣EM​∣=​∬R​f,dA−Mmn​​≤24m2K(b−a)3(d−c)​+24n2K(b−a)(d−c)3​

Key observations:

  • Error ∼O!(m21​)+O!(n21​) — doubling m and n reduces error by factor of 4.
  • Error →0 as m,n→∞ — the rule is consistent.
  • The bound depends on second derivatives of f, not first — confirming the cancellation of linear error.

Compare with the naive left/right endpoint rule, whose error is O(1/m)+O(1/n) — the midpoint rule converges an order faster.

4. Worked Example I — Direct Computation

Problem. Use the midpoint rule with m=2, n=2 to approximate ∬R​x+2y​,dA where R=[0,2]×[0,1].

Step 1 — Partition

Δx=22−0​=1,Δy=21−0​=0.5,ΔA=1×0.5=0.5

Sub-rectangles (2×2 = 4 total):

(i,j)x-intervaly-interval
(1,1)[0,1][0,0.5]
(1,2)[0,1][0.5,1]
(2,1)[1,2][0,0.5]
(2,2)[1,2][0.5,1]

Step 2 — Midpoints

xˉ1​=0.5,xˉ2​=1.5 yˉ​1​=0.25,yˉ​2​=0.75

Step 3 — Evaluate f at Centers

(i,j)(xˉi​,yˉ​j​)f=x+2y​
(1,1)(0.5,;0.25)0.5+0.5​=1​=1
(1,2)(0.5,;0.75)0.5+1.5​=2​≈1.4142
(2,1)(1.5,;0.25)1.5+0.5​=2​≈1.4142
(2,2)(1.5,;0.75)1.5+1.5​=3​≈1.7321

Step 4 — Sum

M22​=(1+2​+2​+3​)×0.5 =(1+22​+3​)×0.5 =(1+2(1.4142)+1.7321)×0.5 =(1+2.8284+1.7321)×0.5=5.5605×0.5≈2.780

Step 5 — Compare with Exact Value

∬R​x+2y​,dA=∫02​∫01​x+2y​,dy,dx

Inner: ∫01​x+2y​,dy=[3(x+2y)3/2​]01​=3(x+2)3/2−x3/2​

Outer: ∫02​3(x+2)3/2−x3/2​,dx=31​[52(x+2)5/2​−52x5/2​]02​

=152​[(45/2−25/2)−(25/2−0)]=152​[32−2⋅25/2]=152​(32−82​)

=1564−162​​≈1564−22.627​≈1541.373​≈2.758

Error: ∣2.780−2.758∣=0.022 with only a 2×2 grid. Already less than 1 error.

5. Worked Example II — Function Given as a Table

This is the scenario where the midpoint rule is indispensable: f is known only at discrete points.

Problem. The temperature T(x,y) (in °C) at the center of each sub-rectangle in the grid [0,4]×[0,2] (with m=2, n=2, so Δx=2, Δy=1, ΔA=2) is measured as:

yˉ​1​=0.5yˉ​2​=1.5
xˉ1​=1T=12T=18
xˉ2​=3T=20T=25

Estimate the total heat content ∬R​T(x,y),dA.

M22​=(12+18+20+25)×2=75×2=150

The average temperature over R is Tˉ=8150​=18.75,∘C (area of R is 4×2=8).

6.2 Why Midpoint Beats Endpoints (1D Analogy Extended to 2D)

  Over one sub-rectangle R_{ij}:

  Exact integral:        f   f + f_y·Δy + ...
                        ┌───────────────────┐
                    f + │   (curved surface) │
                  f_y·Δ │                   │
                        └───────────────────┘

  Left corner approx:   f(x_{i-1}, y_{j-1}) ← systematically low if f increasing
  Right corner approx:  f(x_i, y_j)         ← systematically high if f increasing
  Midpoint approx:      f(x̄_i, ȳ_j)        ← linear errors cancel (symmetric)

7. The Midpoint Rule on General Regions

If D is not a rectangle, a standard approach is:

  1. Enclose D in a rectangle R.
  2. Extend f to R by setting f=0 outside D.
  3. Apply the midpoint rule to R.

The sub-rectangles Rij​ that lie entirely outside D contribute zero. Sub-rectangles that straddle the boundary contribute an approximation error, but this boundary error →0 as the grid refines (assuming D has a reasonable boundary).

Alternative: Use the midpoint rule over each boundary-aligned sub-region after decomposing D into Type I or Type II strips.

8. Comparison with Other Rules

RuleSample point in Rij​Error order (per dimension)Notes
Left endpoint(xi−1​,yj−1​)O(1/m)+O(1/n)Simplest; systematic error
Right endpoint(xi​,yj​)O(1/m)+O(1/n)Systematic error (opposite sign)
Midpoint(xˉi​,yˉ​j​)O(1/m2)+O(1/n2)Best among 1-point rules
Trapezoidal (2D)Average of 4 cornersO(1/m2)+O(1/n2)Same order as midpoint
Simpson’s (2D)Weighted comboO(1/m4)+O(1/n4)Higher order; needs even subdivisions

Key takeaway. The midpoint rule achieves the same asymptotic error order as the trapezoidal rule (both O(h2)), but with half the function evaluations — one per sub-rectangle instead of four corner evaluations.

9. Connection to Machine Learning and Deep Learning

9.1 Mini-Batch SGD as a Midpoint Rule

In machine learning, the true objective is the expected loss: R(θ)=∬ℓ(θ;x,y),p(x,y),dx,dy

This integral is approximated by sampling. When we use a mini-batch of B samples drawn uniformly from the dataset: R^B​(θ)=B1​∑b=1B​ℓ(θ;xb​,yb​)

If the data space is partitioned into B equal cells and one sample is drawn from near the center of each cell, this is structurally identical to the midpoint rule. The “midpoint” philosophy — sample the center of each region — is why stratified sampling (a structured variant of this) reduces variance in Monte Carlo integration.

The key analogy:

Midpoint RuleMini-batch SGD
Partition R into mn sub-rectanglesPartition dataset into B mini-batches
Sample f at center (xˉi​,yˉ​j​)Evaluate loss at batch samples
mn1​∑ij​f(xˉi​,yˉ​j​)B1​∑b​ℓ(θ;xb​,yb​)
Error →0 as mn→∞Gradient estimate improves as B→∞

9.2 Numerical Integration in Bayesian ML

Bayesian inference requires computing integrals of the form: p(y∣x)=∫p(y∣x,θ),p(θ∣D),dθ

For low-dimensional θ, the midpoint rule (or more sophisticated quadrature) is applied directly. For high-dimensional θ, Monte Carlo methods (MCMC, variational inference) take over — but they are conceptually grid-free versions of the same Riemann sum philosophy.

9.3 Quadrature in Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

Physics-Informed Neural Networks minimize residuals of PDEs over a domain: L=∬D​[N[u](https://claude.ai/chat/x,y)]2dA

where N[u] is a PDE operator applied to the network output u. This integral is approximated by the midpoint rule (or Gaussian quadrature) over a set of collocation points — the midpoints of a partition of D. The quality of the approximation directly controls training quality.

9.4 Gaussian Quadrature — The “Optimal Midpoint” Generalization

The midpoint rule uses one evaluation at the center of each sub-rectangle. Gaussian quadrature asks: what are the optimal evaluation points and weights for a given number of function evaluations? The answer (Gauss–Legendre points) generalizes the midpoint idea — place sample points where they eliminate the most error. This is used in high-performance numerical integration throughout scientific ML and finite element methods.

10. Full Procedure Summary

Given ∬R​f(x,y),dA over R=[a,b]×[c,d], approximate with m×n grid:

Δx=mb−a​,Δy=nd−c​,ΔA=Δx⋅Δy

xˉi​=a+(i−21​)Δx(i=1,…,m)

yˉ​j​=c+(j−21​)Δy(j=1,…,n)

Mmn​=ΔA∑i=1m​∑j=1n​f(xˉi​,yˉ​j​)

To improve accuracy: increase m and n. Error decreases as O(1/m2)+O(1/n2).
