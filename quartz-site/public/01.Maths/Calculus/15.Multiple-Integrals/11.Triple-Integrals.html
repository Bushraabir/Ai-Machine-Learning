0. Why Triple Integrals Exist

Before jumping to formalism, let’s be honest about what’s happening.

A double integral ∬D​f(x,y),dA sweeps a function over a 2-dimensional region — it accumulates area-weighted mass, flux, probability, etc.

A triple integral ∭E​f(x,y,z),dV does the same over a 3-dimensional solid region E⊂R3. The natural physical pictures are:

f(x,y,z)∭E​f,dV means
1Volume of E
ρ(x,y,z) (density)Total mass
ρ(x,y,z)⋅xMoment about the yz-plane
Joint probability densityProbability of event in E
Temperature fieldAverage temperature (after dividing by volume)

In machine learning, every one of these shows up — just in dimensions n≫3.

1. The Definition — Building From First Principles

1.1 Riemann Sum Construction

Let E⊂R3 be a bounded solid and f:E→R be bounded. Enclose E in a box:

B=[a1​,b1​]×[a2​,b2​]×[a3​,b3​]

Partition each interval: Δxi​,Δyj​,Δzk​ with l,m,n subintervals respectively. This creates l⋅m⋅n sub-boxes Bijk​ of volume:

ΔVijk​=Δxi​,Δyj​,Δzk​

Pick sample points (x_{ijk}^_, y_{ijk}^_, z_{ijk}^*) in each Bijk​. The Riemann sum is:

S_{lmn} = \sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_{ijk}^_, y_{ijk}^_, z_{ijk}^*),\Delta V_{ijk}

Definition. The triple integral of f over E is:

∭E​f(x,y,z),dV=l,m,n→∞lim​Slmn​​

provided the limit exists and is the same for all choices of sample points.

Existence. If f is continuous on a bounded solid E whose boundary has volume zero (a measurable set), then the triple integral exists. (Stewart, §15.6; this follows from the general theory of Riemann integration on Rn.)

2. Fubini’s Theorem — The Computational Engine

Computing triple integrals directly from the Riemann sum definition is impractical. Fubini’s theorem reduces the problem to three iterated single-variable integrals.

2.1 Statement (Box Case)

Theorem (Fubini). Let B=[a,b]×[c,d]×[r,s] and f continuous on B. Then:

∭B​f(x,y,z),dV=∫rs​∫cd​∫ab​f(x,y,z),dx,dy,dz

and all 3!=6 orderings of integration give the same value.

Key insight. The iterated integral is evaluated inside-out: fix y,z and integrate over x first (treating y,z as constants), then integrate the result over y (treating z as constant), then over z.

2.2 The General Case — Type I, II, III Regions

Most interesting domains are not boxes. We classify them:

Type I (Project onto xy-plane):

E=(x,y,z)∣(x,y)∈D,;u1​(x,y)≤z≤u2​(x,y)

∭E​f,dV=∬D​[∫u1​(x,y)u2​(x,y)​f(x,y,z),dz]dA

Type II (Project onto xz-plane):

E=(x,y,z)∣(x,z)∈D,;u1​(x,z)≤y≤u2​(x,z)

Type III (Project onto yz-plane):

E=(x,y,z)∣(y,z)∈D,;u1​(y,z)≤x≤u2​(y,z)

Heuristic for choosing type. Draw the solid, look at it from three directions, and choose the projection that gives the simplest boundary functions u1​,u2​.

3. Worked Examples

Example 3.1 — Volume of a Tetrahedron

Problem. Find the volume of the tetrahedron E with vertices at (0,0,0), (1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1).

Solution. The bounding plane through the non-origin vertices has equation:

x+y+z=1⟹z≤1−x−y

The region is:

E=(x,y,z)∣0≤x≤1,;0≤y≤1−x,;0≤z≤1−x−y

V=∭E​1,dV=∫01​∫01−x​∫01−x−y​dz,dy,dx

Inner integral (over z):

∫01−x−y​dz=(1−x−y)

Middle integral (over y):

∫01−x​(1−x−y),dy=[(1−x)y−2y2​]01−x​=(1−x)2−2(1−x)2​=2(1−x)2​

Outer integral (over x):

∫01​2(1−x)2​,dx=21​⋅[−3(1−x)3​]01​=21​⋅31​=61​​

Check: The standard formula for a tetrahedron with edge vertices at unit distance from origin gives V=61​(1)(1)(1)=61​. ✓

Example 3.2 — Mass with Variable Density

Problem. Find the mass of the rectangular box B=[0,2]×[0,3]×[0,1] with density ρ(x,y,z)=xy2z.

M=∭B​xy2z,dV=∫01​∫03​∫02​xy2z,dx,dy,dz

Inner: ∫02​xy2z,dx=y2z⋅2x2​​02​=2y2z

Middle: ∫03​2y2z,dy=2z⋅3y3​​03​=2z⋅9=18z

Outer: ∫01​18z,dz=18⋅21​=9​

Example 3.3 — Changing Order of Integration

Problem. Evaluate ∫01​∫01​∫y1​cos(z2),dz,dy,dx by changing the order.

Observation. ∫cos(z2),dz has no closed form (Fresnel integral). Must change order.

Original limits: 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤1, y≤z≤1.

The region: 0≤y≤z≤1, 0≤x≤1.

New order (dx,dy,dz):

∫01​∫0z​∫01​cos(z2),dx,dy,dz=∫01​∫0z​cos(z2),dy,dz=∫01​zcos(z2),dz

=21​sin(z2)​01​=2sin1​​

4. Change of Variables — The Jacobian

For complex regions, the key tool is a coordinate substitution. Suppose (x,y,z)=T(u,v,w) is a smooth, invertible transformation. Define the Jacobian determinant:

∂(u,v,w)∂(x,y,z)​=​∂u∂x​​∂v∂x​​∂w∂x​ ∂u∂y​​∂v∂y​​∂w∂y​ ∂u∂z​​∂v∂z​​∂w∂z​​​

Change of Variables Theorem:

∭E​f(x,y,z),dV=∭E∗​f(T(u,v,w))​∂(u,v,w)∂(x,y,z)​​du,dv,dw

The Jacobian ​∂(u,v,w)∂(x,y,z)​​ measures how the transformation stretches or compresses volume locally. It’s the 3D generalization of the substitution factor ∣dx/du∣ in single-variable calculus.

5. Cylindrical Coordinates

5.1 Definition

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ,z=z r≥0,0≤θ≤2π,z∈R

Jacobian:

∂(r,θ,z)∂(x,y,z)​=​cosθ​−rsinθ​0 sinθ​rcosθ​0 0​0​1​​=r

Therefore:

∭E​f,dV=∭E∗​f(rcosθ,rsinθ,z)⋅r,dr,dθ,dz

When to use. Any time the region E or the integrand involves x2+y2 (i.e., circular cross-sections).

5.3 Example — Volume of a Cone Inside a Sphere

Problem. Find the volume of the solid E that lies inside the sphere x2+y2+z2=4 and above the cone z=x2+y2​.

In cylindrical: sphere becomes r2+z2=4⇒z=4−r2​; cone becomes z=r.

Intersection: r=4−r2​⇒r2=2⇒r=2​.

V=∫02π​∫02​​∫r4−r2​​r,dz,dr,dθ

6. Spherical Coordinates

6.1 Definition

x=ρsinϕcosθ,y=ρsinϕsinθ,z=ρcosϕ

ρ≥0,0≤ϕ≤π(polar angle from +z),0≤θ≤2π

Note: ρ2=x2+y2+z2, and r=ρsinϕ (cylindrical r).

Jacobian: (computed by expanding the 3×3 determinant)

∂(ρ,ϕ,θ)∂(x,y,z)​=ρ2sinϕ

∭E​f,dV=∭E∗​f(ρsinϕcosθ,,ρsinϕsinθ,,ρcosϕ)⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ​

Derivation of Jacobian. The volume element dV=ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ can be understood geometrically: dρ is the radial thickness; ρ,dϕ is the arc in the meridional direction; ρsinϕ,dθ is the arc in the azimuthal direction. Their product gives the volume of the infinitesimal “orange slice” wedge.

8. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

This is not a digression — it is the point.

8.1 High-Dimensional Integration is the Core Problem of ML

In machine learning, we almost never have 3 variables. We have n variables where n can be in the hundreds of thousands. But every concept below is a direct generalization of triple integrals.

Bayesian inference. The posterior distribution requires computing:

p(θ∣D)=∫Θ​p(D∣θ),p(θ),dθp(D∣θ),p(θ)​

The denominator is an integral over the entire parameter space Θ⊂Rn. For a neural network with 107 parameters, this is a 107-dimensional integral — a triple integral is the toy case.

Expected loss. The theoretical risk is:

R(f)=E(x,y)∼p​[ℓ(f(x),y)]=∫X×Y​ℓ(f(x),y),p(x,y),dx,dy

This is a multidimensional integral over the data distribution.

8.2 Probability Distributions Over Rn

The triple integral ∭E​f,dV with f a density function and ∭R3​f,dV=1 gives a 3D probability distribution — a joint distribution p(x1​,x2​,x3​).

The multivariate Gaussian in n dimensions:

p(x)=(2π)n/2∣Σ∣1/21​exp(−21​(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ))

is the n-dimensional analog. The normalization constant is verified by a generalization of the Gaussian integral, which itself relies on the Jacobian of spherical coordinates in n dimensions.

8.3 Monte Carlo Integration — Approximating the Triple Integral

Direct numerical integration (using a Riemann sum grid) becomes infeasible for high dimensions — this is the curse of dimensionality: the number of grid points grows as Nn.

Monte Carlo integration sidesteps this. To estimate ∭E​f,dV:

  1. Sample x1​,…,xN​∼Uniform(E)
  2. Approximate:

∭E​f,dV≈V(E)⋅N1​∑i=1N​f(xi​)

The error scales as O(1/N​) regardless of dimension — a dramatic improvement over quadrature.

This is the engine behind:

  • MCMC sampling (Metropolis-Hastings, HMC)
  • Variational inference
  • Reinforcement learning (policy gradient methods)
  • Generative models (VAE, diffusion models)

8.4 The Jacobian in Deep Learning — Backpropagation

The Jacobian matrix J=∂(x1​,…,xn​)∂(y1​,…,ym​)​ of a change-of-variables transformation generalizes the scalar Jacobian determinant in integration theory.

In neural networks:

  • Backpropagation computes Jacobian-vector products JTv via the chain rule
  • Normalizing flows in generative modeling use the exact formula:

pX​(x)=pZ​(f(x))​det∂x∂f​​

This is precisely the change-of-variables formula for probability densities — the n-dimensional generalization of pX​(x)=pZ​(g(x))∣g′(x)∣ and of the Jacobian determinant in §4.

Punchline. Every time a normalizing flow pushes a Gaussian through a sequence of invertible transformations to model a complex data distribution, it is computing Jacobians and change-of-variables in high-dimensional space. The theory you’re building here is foundational.

8.5 Gaussian Processes and Kernel Methods

The mean and covariance of a Gaussian process are defined as integrals over the input space:

μ(x)=∫f(x),p(f),δf,k(x,x′)=∫(f(x)−μ(x))(f(x′)−μ(x′)),p(f),δf

These are functional integrals — the infinite-dimensional limit of multidimensional integrals.

10. A Personal Note on Intuition

The transition from double to triple integrals is philosophically straightforward: one more variable, one more nested integration. The real technical content lives in:

  1. Correctly setting up limits — this requires genuinely understanding the geometry of the region E in 3D.
  2. Choosing coordinates wisely — a naive Cartesian setup for a sphere leads to nightmarish square roots; spherical coordinates make it trivial.
  3. The Jacobian is non-optional — it is not a bookkeeping device. It is a theorem about how volume transforms under smooth maps, and it has deep consequences in differential geometry, physics (general relativity), and machine learning (normalizing flows).

When in doubt, draw the region. Slice it. Label the bounding surfaces. The limits will follow from the geometry.
