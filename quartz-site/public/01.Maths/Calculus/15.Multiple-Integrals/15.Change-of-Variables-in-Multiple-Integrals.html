15.Change of Variables in Multiple Integrals

“A good coordinate system is half the solution. The Jacobian is the price you pay for the privilege of working in comfortable coordinates.”

0. The Core Problem

Suppose you need to evaluate:

∬R​ex+yx−y​,dA

where R is the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), (0,1).

In Cartesian coordinates this is a nightmare — the exponent is a rational function of x and y, and the triangular region produces y-limits that depend on x. There is no clean antiderivative.

But watch what happens if we introduce u=x−y, v=x+y. Then:

ex+yx−y​=eu/v

The triangular region in xy maps to a triangular region in uv with simple, constant limits. The integral collapses.

This is the essence of change of variables: trade a hard region/integrand pair for an easy one, paying a precise price (the Jacobian) to keep the integral equal.

1. Review — The 1D Case

Before generalizing, recall the substitution rule from single-variable calculus:

∫ab​f(x),dx=∫cd​f(g(u)),g′(u),du

where x=g(u), g(c)=a, g(d)=b.

The factor g′(u)=dx/du accounts for how the transformation stretches or compresses the interval. If g′(u)>1, a small du corresponds to a larger dx; the integral must be scaled accordingly.

The absolute value ∣g′(u)∣ is what matters for area/volume — not the sign. The sign of g′ determines orientation, not magnitude.

The Jacobian in higher dimensions is the exact generalization of ∣g′(u)∣.

2. The 2D Change of Variables Formula

2.1 Setup

Let T:(u,v)↦(x,y) be a transformation defined by:

x=x(u,v),y=y(u,v)

Suppose:

  • T is a C1 (continuously differentiable) bijection from region S in uv-space onto region R in xy-space
  • T is one-to-one on the interior of S (it may fail on the boundary)

2.2 The Jacobian

Definition. The Jacobian determinant of the transformation T is:

∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=​∂u∂x​​∂v∂x​\[10pt]∂u∂y​​∂v∂y​​​=∂u∂x​∂v∂y​−∂v∂x​∂u∂y​

2.3 The Change of Variables Theorem

Theorem. Under the conditions above:

∬R​f(x,y),dA=∬S​f(x(u,v),,y(u,v))​∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​​du,dv​

What the Jacobian does. The area element dA=dx,dy in xy-space corresponds to ​∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​​du,dv in uv-space. The Jacobian measures the local area magnification factor of the transformation at each point.

3. Geometric Meaning of the Jacobian

3.1 Linear Approximation

Near a point (u0​,v0​), the transformation T is approximately linear:

(x\y​)≈(x0​\y0​​)+J(∂x/∂upartialy/∂u​∂x/∂v∂y/∂v​)​​(u−u0​−ˇ​v0​​)

A small rectangle [u0​,u0​+du]×[v0​,v0​+dv] in S maps (approximately) to a parallelogram in R spanned by the columns of J:

a=(∂x/∂upartialy/∂u​)du,b=(∂x/∂vpartialy/∂v​)dv

The area of this parallelogram is ∣a×b∣=∣detJ∣,du,dv.

3.2 Visualization

uv-space                           xy-space
                                   
  v                                y
  │                                │
  │  ┌──────┐                      │    ╱──────╲
  │  │      │  ──── T ────>        │   ╱        ╲
  │  │  S   │  du×dv               │  ╱    R     ╲
  │  └──────┘                      │ ╱             ╲
  │   du  dv                       │╱ |det J|·du·dv ╲
  └──────────── u                  └────────────────── x

  Rectangle of area du·dv   →   Parallelogram of area |det J|·du·dv

  If |det J| > 1: transformation EXPANDS area locally
  If |det J| < 1: transformation COMPRESSES area locally
  If |det J| = 0: transformation is DEGENERATE (collapses dimension)

3.3 Why the Absolute Value?

The determinant detJ can be negative — this means T reverses orientation (like a reflection). For computing areas or integrals of positive quantities, orientation doesn’t matter; only the magnitude of the area scaling does. Hence ∣detJ∣.

4. The Inverse Jacobian

A useful identity: if T:(u,v)↦(x,y) has Jacobian ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v), then the inverse transformation T−1:(x,y)↦(u,v) satisfies:

∂(x,y)∂(u,v)​=(∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​)−1

Proof. By the chain rule:

∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​⋅∂(x,y)∂(u,v)​=∂(x,y)∂(x,y)​=det(I)=1

Practical use. Sometimes it’s easier to define T−1 (i.e., express u,v in terms of x,y) and compute ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y), then take its reciprocal to get ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v).

5. The 3D Change of Variables Formula

The extension to three dimensions is direct. Let T:(u,v,w)↦(x,y,z).

Jacobian:

∂(u,v,w)∂(x,y,z)​=​∂x/∂u​∂x/∂v​∂x/∂w ∂y/∂u​∂y/∂v​∂y/∂w ∂z/∂u​∂z/∂v​∂z/∂w​​

Formula:

∭E​f(x,y,z),dV=∭E∗​f(T(u,v,w))​∂(u,v,w)∂(x,y,z)​​du,dv,dw​

The Jacobian now measures the local volume magnification factor of the transformation.

6. Standard Coordinate Systems as Change of Variables

Every special coordinate system is a change of variables with a known Jacobian. Let’s re-derive them.

6.1 Polar Coordinates (R2)

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ

J=(cosθsinθ​−rsinθrcosθ​)

detJ=rcos2θ+rsin2θ=r

dA=r,dr,dθ

6.2 Cylindrical Coordinates (R3)

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ,z=z

J=(cosθsinθ​−rsinθrcosθ​00\0​0​1​)

detJ=r(same as polar, z-row is trivial)

dV=r,dr,dθ,dz

6.3 Spherical Coordinates (R3)

x=ρsinϕcosθ,y=ρsinϕsinθ,z=ρcosϕ

J=(sinϕcosθ​ρcosϕcosθ​−ρsinϕsinθ sinϕsinθ​ρcosϕsinθ​ρsinϕcosθ cosϕ​−ρsinϕ​0​)

Expanding along the third row:

detJ=ρ2sinϕ

dV=ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

Pattern. All three standard coordinate Jacobians are non-negative (for r≥0, 0≤ϕ≤π), so the absolute value is automatic.

7. Worked Examples

Example 7.1 — Linear Transformation (Parallelogram Region)

Problem. Evaluate ∬R​(x+y),dA where R is the parallelogram with vertices (0,0), (2,1), (3,3), (1,2).

Step 1: Identify the transformation. The sides of the parallelogram are parallel to a=(2,1) and b=(1,2). Set:

x=2u+v,y=u+2v,(u,v)∈[0,1]×[0,1]

Verify corners: (u,v)=(0,0)→(0,0); (1,0)→(2,1); (0,1)→(1,2); (1,1)→(3,3). ✓

Step 2: Jacobian.

∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=​2​1\1​2​​=4−1=3

Step 3: Transform integrand. x+y=(2u+v)+(u+2v)=3u+3v

Step 4: Integrate.

∬R​(x+y),dA=∫01​∫01​(3u+3v)⋅3,du,dv=9∫01​∫01​(u+v),du,dv

=9∫01​[21​+v]dv=9[2v​+2v2​]01​=9⋅1=9​

Example 7.2 — The Opening Problem

Problem. Evaluate ∬R​ex+yx−y​,dA where R is the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), (0,1).

Step 1: Choose substitution. Let u=x−y, v=x+y.

Step 2: Find the inverse Jacobian (easier to compute ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y) first):

∂(x,y)∂(u,v)​=​1​−1\1​1​​=1+1=2

Therefore:

∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=21​

Step 3: Map the region. In terms of x,y:

  • Edge y=0, 0≤x≤1: u=x, v=x⇒u=v
  • Edge x=0, 0≤y≤1: u=−y, v=y⇒u=−v
  • Edge x+y=1: v=1 (constant!)

So S in uv-space: bounded by u=v, u=−v, v=1, i.e., 0≤v≤1, −v≤u≤v.

Step 4: Integrate.

∬R​eu/v⋅21​,du,dv=21​∫01​∫−vv​eu/v,du,dv

Inner (u):

∫−vv​eu/v,du=v⋅eu/v​−vv​=v(e1−e−1)=v(e−e−1)

Outer (v):

21​∫01​v(e−e−1),dv=2e−e−1​⋅21​=4e−e−1​​

Example 7.3 — Elliptic Region

Problem. Find the area of the ellipse a2x2​+b2y2​≤1.

Substitution. x=au, y=bv. Then the ellipse maps to the unit disk u2+v2≤1.

Jacobian:

∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=​a​0\0​b​​=ab

Integrate:

A=∬ellipse​dA=∬u2+v2≤1​ab,du,dv=ab⋅π(1)2=πab​

This is a one-line derivation of the ellipse area formula. No elliptic integrals required.

Example 7.4 — General Ellipsoid Volume (3D)

Problem. Find the volume of a2x2​+b2y2​+c2z2​≤1.

Substitution. x=au, y=bv, z=cw. Maps ellipsoid to unit ball u2+v2+w2≤1.

Jacobian:

∂(u,v,w)∂(x,y,z)​=​a​0​0\0​b​0\0​0​c​​=abc

Integrate:

V=∭ellipsoid​dV=abc⋅∭u2+v2+w2≤1​du,dv,dw=abc⋅34π​=34πabc​​

Example 7.5 — Non-Obvious Substitution

Problem. Evaluate ∬R​(x2−xy+y2),dA where R:1≤x2−xy+y2≤4.

Observation. The region boundaries are level curves of the integrand. Let:

u=x2−xy+y2(this defines level curves)

But we need a two-variable substitution. Notice x2−xy+y2 is a quadratic form. Complete the square-style rotation: let x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ… actually, let’s use the substitution:

x=u​⋅f(θ),y=u​⋅g(θ)

for the right f,g. Instead, a cleaner approach: introduce u,θ via:

x=u​,43/4​cosθ−sinθ/3​​,y=u​,43/4​2sinθ/3​​

This is getting complex — let’s use the clean version:

The form x2−xy+y2=u with the substitution x=u1/2⋅p, y=u1/2⋅q where p2−pq+q2=1 (the unit level curve). By the theory of quadratic forms, x2−xy+y2 can be diagonalized via rotation by θ=π/4:

x=λ1​​rcosθ−ssinθ​,y=λ2​​rsinθ+scosθ​

Simpler direct approach: use x2−xy+y2=u and y/x=t, then x2(1−t+t2)=u⇒x=u/(1−t+t2)​.

Jacobian computation is involved; the key result via polar-type rescaling gives ∣J∣=3​1​ (constant!).

∬R​u⋅3​1​,du,dθ=3​1​∫02π​∫14​u,du,dθ=3​2π​⋅215​=3​15π​=5π3​​

Example 7.6 — Spherical Coordinates Derived from Scratch

Rather than memorizing the spherical Jacobian, derive it:

x=ρsinϕcosθ,y=ρsinϕsinθ,z=ρcosϕ

J=(sinϕcosθ​ρcosϕcosθ​−ρsinϕsinθ sinϕsinθ​ρcosϕsinθ​ρsinϕcosθ cosϕ​−ρsinϕ​0​)

Expand along row 3:

detJ=cosϕ​ρcosϕcosθrhocosϕsinθ​−ρsinϕsinθρsinϕcosθ​​−(−ρsinϕ)​sinϕcosθsinϕsinθ​−ρsinϕsinθρsinϕcosθ​​

First minor: =ρ2cosϕcosθsinϕcosθ+ρ2sinϕsinθcosϕsinθ=ρ2cosϕsinϕ(cos2θ+sin2θ)=ρ2cosϕsinϕ

Second minor: =ρsin2ϕcos2θ+ρsin2ϕsin2θ=ρsin2ϕ

Therefore: detJ=cosϕ⋅ρ2cosϕsinϕ+ρsinϕ⋅ρsin2ϕ=ρ2sinϕcos2ϕ+ρ2sin3ϕ=ρ2sinϕ

dV=ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ​

8. How to Choose a Substitution

There is no algorithm, but there are heuristics:

8.1 The Region Is the Guide

Shape of RTry
ParallelogramLinear map aligning sides with coordinate axes
Ellipse a2x2​+b2y2​≤1x=au, y=bv (maps to disk)
Region bounded by xy=c1​, xy=c2​, y/x=c3​, y/x=c4​u=xy, v=y/x
Region bounded by x+y=cu=x+y, v=x−y or v=x
Circular or radial symmetryPolar or cylindrical
Spherical symmetrySpherical coordinates

8.2 The Integrand Is the Guide

If the integrand contains an expression like g(x,y), setting u=g(x,y) is often productive. Then choose v to make the Jacobian nonzero.

8.3 Match Boundaries to Coordinate Lines

The ideal substitution makes each boundary of R correspond to u=const or v=const. This gives rectangular limits [u1​,u2​]×[v1​,v2​] in uv-space — the simplest possible.

9. The Jacobian in Full Generality — A Linear Algebra View

9.1 The Jacobian as the Derivative

For a differentiable map T:Rn→Rn, the total derivative at a point p is the n×n matrix DT(p) of all partial derivatives — precisely the Jacobian matrix J.

Locally, T acts like the linear map J. A unit cube in the domain maps to a parallelepiped in the range with volume ∣detJ∣.

9.2 Singular Jacobians

If detJ=0 at some point, the transformation collapses a region of positive area/volume to zero. The change-of-variables formula requires detJ=0 on the interior of S (one-to-one condition).

Example: polar coordinates have detJ=r=0 at the origin. This is a single point (measure zero), so the formula still holds.

9.3 The Chain Rule for Jacobians

If T1​:(u,v)↦(s,t) and T2​:(s,t)↦(x,y), then T2​∘T1​ has:

∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​=∂(s,t)∂(x,y)​⋅∂(u,v)∂(s,t)​

This is the chain rule for Jacobians — it mirrors det(AB)=det(A)det(B).

10. Proof Sketch of the Change of Variables Theorem

For the rigorous mind — here’s why the theorem is true.

Step 1. For a linear transformation T(u)=Au (constant matrix A), a rectangle in u-space maps to a parallelogram in x-space. The area of the parallelogram equals ∣detA∣ times the area of the rectangle. This is the geometric content of the determinant.

Step 2. For a nonlinear T, partition S into small rectangles Sij​. On each Sij​, approximate T by its linearization DT(pij​) at the center point. By Step 1, each small piece maps to area ∣detDT(pij​)∣,Δu,Δv.

Step 3. The Riemann sum:

∑i,j​f(T(pij​)),∣detDT(pij​)∣,Δu,Δv→∬S​f(T(u)),∣detDT(u)∣,du,dv

as the partition is refined. This equals ∬R​f(x),dA by the definition of the integral (since T maps S to R bijectively).

Step 4. The error in the linear approximation (Step 2) is O(Δu2+Δv2) per rectangle, which vanishes in the limit because there are O(1/(Δu,Δv)) rectangles — the error is O(Δu+Δv)→0. □

11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

11.1 Normalizing Flows — The Jacobian as the Core Operation

Normalizing flows are a class of deep generative models that learn to transform a simple distribution (e.g., Gaussian) into a complex data distribution using a sequence of invertible transformations.

If z∼pZ​(z) (simple base distribution) and x=f(z) (invertible transformation), then the density of x is:

pX​(x)=pZ​(f−1(x))​det∂x∂f−1​​=pZ​(f−1(x))​det∂z∂f​​−1

This is precisely the change of variables formula applied to probability densities. The Jacobian determinant ​det∂z∂f​​ accounts for how f stretches or compresses probability mass.

Design challenge: Computing detJ for a general n×n matrix costs O(n3). For neural networks with millions of parameters, this is infeasible. The main research in normalizing flows is designing architectures where detJ is cheap to compute (e.g., triangular Jacobians → O(n); coupling layers in RealNVP, Glow).

Training a normalizing flow:

  z ~ N(0,I)  →  [f₁]  →  [f₂]  →  ... →  [fₖ]  →  x ≈ data

  Log-likelihood: log p(x) = log p_Z(z) - Σᵢ log|det J_fᵢ|
  
  Each |det J_fᵢ| IS the Jacobian determinant from change of variables.
  
  Triangular architecture → det J = product of diagonal entries → O(n)

11.2 The Reparameterization Trick — Gradient Through a Stochastic Node

In Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), the encoder outputs μ and σ, and we sample:

z=μ+σ⊙ϵ,ϵ∼N(0,I)

This is a change of variables: z=T(ϵ;μ,σ). The Jacobian of T with respect to ϵ is diagonal (since zi​=μi​+σi​ϵi​):

detJ=∏i​σi​

This allows gradients to flow through the sampling operation — which is why the reparameterization trick works mathematically: it replaces a stochastic node with a deterministic transformation of a fixed noise distribution.

11.3 Score Matching and Diffusion Models

Diffusion models (DDPM, score-based models) define a forward process that gradually adds noise:

xt​=αˉt​​,x0​+1−αˉt​​,ϵ

This is a change of variables (linear transformation) from (x0​,ϵ) to xt​. The Jacobian of this transformation is αˉt​​n (for n-dimensional data) — a scalar scaling of the volume element.

The probability flow ODE in continuous diffusion models is:

dx=[f(x,t)−21​g(t)2∇x​logpt​(x)]dt

The change in log-probability along trajectories involves the instantaneous change of variables formula (Liouville’s equation):

dtdlogp(x(t))​=−∇x​⋅v(x,t)

where ∇x​⋅v is the divergence — the infinitesimal Jacobian. This is the continuous-time generalization of the discrete change-of-variables theorem.

11.4 The Jacobian in Backpropagation

Every layer l in a neural network computes h(l)=f(l)(h(l−1)). The backpropagation algorithm computes:

∂h(l−1)∂L​=Jf(l)T​⋅∂h(l)∂L​

where Jf(l)​=∂h(l−1)∂h(l)​ is the Jacobian matrix of the layer. For an n-neuron layer, this is an n×n matrix — the same object as our change-of-variables Jacobian, now used not for area scaling but for gradient propagation.

Batch normalization, layer normalization, and spectral normalization all control the singular values of this Jacobian matrix — keeping them near 1 prevents vanishing/exploding gradients.

12. Summary

DimensionFormulaKey object
1D$\int_a^b f(x),dx = \int_c^d f(g(u))g’(u)
2D$\iint_R f,dA = \iint_S f\circ T\cdot\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)
3D$\iiint_E f,dV = \iiint_{E^*} f\circ T\cdot\partial(x,y,z)/\partial(u,v,w)
nDSame pattern, n×n det$

The Jacobian determinant ∣detJ∣ is the local volume scaling factor of the transformation — it is the single most important object in the theory of change of variables, and it reappears throughout modern machine learning as the fundamental bookkeeping device for how transformations distort probability mass.
