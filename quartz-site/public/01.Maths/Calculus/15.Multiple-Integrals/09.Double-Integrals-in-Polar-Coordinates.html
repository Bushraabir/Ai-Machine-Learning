09.Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

09.Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

“Cartesian coordinates are honest but often unkind. Polar coordinates reward those who see the geometry.”

§0 — Preamble

There is a class of double integrals that is technically legitimate in Cartesian form but computationally brutal — integrands involving x2+y2, domains that are disks or sectors or annuli. Forcing these into the dx,dy framework is like trying to describe a circle with a ruler: possible in principle, ugly in practice.

Polar coordinates exist precisely for this class of problems. The passage from (x,y) to (r,θ) is not merely a substitution — it is a change of measure, and the appearance of the Jacobian factor r is the theorem’s most important output. This note proves the transformation rigorously, develops the geometric intuition, catalogues the standard domain types, supplies worked examples (including the famous Gaussian integral), and traces polar integration into deep learning and probability.

References: Stewart §15.3; MIT 18.02 Sessions 55–56.

§1 — Motivation: When Cartesian Coordinates Fail

1.1 The Archetypal Hard Integral

∬D​e−(x2+y2),dA,D=x2+y2≤R2

In Cartesian form:

∫−RR​∫−R2−x2​R2−x2​​e−(x2+y2),dy,dx

The inner integral ∫e−(x2+y2),dy has no closed form in elementary functions. The limits involve square roots. This is a disaster.

In polar form, the same integral becomes ∫02π​∫0R​e−r2,r,dr,dθ — a product of two elementary integrals.

1.2 The Diagnostic Test

Use polar coordinates when:

  • The integrand contains x2+y2, x2+y2​, x2−y2, arctan(y/x), or xy/(x2+y2)
  • The domain is a disk, annulus, sector, or wedge
  • The domain boundary is described by r=f(θ)

§2 — The Polar Coordinate System

2.1 The Transformation

The polar coordinate map Φ:(r,θ)↦(x,y) is:

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ

with inverse (on R2∖(0,0)):

r=x2+y2​,θ=arctan!(xy​)(with quadrant correction)

Convention. We always take r≥0 and θ∈[0,2π) (or (−π,π] when convenient). Negative r is defined by (−r,θ)=(r,θ+π), but we never need it here.

2.2 Polar Grid Geometry

The two families of coordinate curves are:

  • Circles r=c: centred at the origin
  • Rays θ=α: emanating from the origin

A “polar rectangle” [r1​,r2​]×[θ1​,θ2​] is a curved wedge — an annular sector.

§3 — The Jacobian: The Central Theorem

This is the heart of the matter. The change-of-variables formula for double integrals says:

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∬D∗​f(rcosθ,,rsinθ),∣detJΦ​∣,dr,dθ

where D∗ is the pre-image of D in the (r,θ)-plane, and JΦ​ is the Jacobian matrix of Φ.

3.1 Computing the Jacobian

JΦ​=∂(r,θ)∂(x,y)​=(∂x/∂r​∂x/∂θ ∂y/∂r​∂y/∂θ​)=(cosθ​−rsinθ sinθ​rcosθ​)

detJΦ​=cosθ⋅rcosθ−(−rsinθ)⋅sinθ=rcos2θ+rsin2θ=r

Since r≥0: ∣detJΦ​∣=r.

3.2 The Main Formula

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∬D∗​f(rcosθ,,rsinθ);r;dr,dθ​

The r is not optional. It is the Jacobian determinant, encoding the geometric fact that a polar area element is not dr,dθ but r,dr,dθ.

3.3 Geometric Proof of the r Factor

Consider a small polar rectangle: r∈[r,r+Δr], θ∈[θ,θ+Δθ].

           ╭────╮  ← arc of radius r + Δr, length (r+Δr)Δθ
          ╱      ╲
    Δr   ╱  ΔA    ╲
        ╱    ≈ r   ╲
       ╰────╯        ← arc of radius r, length rΔθ
       ←  rΔθ  →

The area of this curved wedge is:

ΔA=21​(r+Δr)2Δθ−21​r2Δθ=r,Δr,Δθ+21​(Δr)2Δθ

In the limit Δr,Δθ→0:

dA=r,dr,dθ

The r factor is the ratio of arc length to radius — it compensates for the fact that arcs at larger r subtend more area per unit angle than arcs near the origin.

§4 — Standard Polar Domains

4.1 Type I Polar Region (Fixed r-bounds)

D∗=(θ,r):α≤θ≤β,;0≤r≤R

Examples: Full disk (α=0,β=2π), half-disk, sector.

∬D​f,dA=∫αβ​∫0R​f(rcosθ,rsinθ),r,dr,dθ

4.2 Type II Polar Region (r-bounds depend on θ)

D∗=(θ,r):α≤θ≤β,;r1​(θ)≤r≤r2​(θ)

Examples: Regions bounded by polar curves like r=1+cosθ (cardioid), r=cos(2θ) (rose).

∬D​f,dA=∫αβ​∫r1​(θ)r2​(θ)​f(rcosθ,rsinθ),r,dr,dθ

§5 — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Area of a Disk (Verification)

Compute Area(D) where D=x2+y2≤R2.

Area(D)=∬D​1,dA=∫02π​∫0R​r,dr,dθ=∫02π​2R2​,dθ=πR2✓

Example 2 — The Gaussian Integral (The Most Important Integral in Probability)

Compute I=∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx.

The trick. Consider I2:

I2=(∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx)!(∫−∞∞​e−y2,dy)=∬R2​e−(x2+y2),dA

The second equality uses separability (the domain is all of R2, a “rectangle” in the extended sense).

Switch to polar. Let DR​=r≤R. By exhaustion (DR​↗R2 as R→∞):

I2=limR→∞​∬DR​​e−r2,r,dr,dθ=limR→∞​∫02π​∫0R​e−r2,r,dr,dθ

Inner integral: let u=r2, du=2r,dr:

∫0R​e−r2,r,dr=21​∫0R2​e−u,du=21​(1−e−R2)

Therefore:

I2=limR→∞​∫02π​21​(1−e−R2),dθ=21​⋅2π⋅1=π

I=∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π​​

This is the most elegant application of polar integration. Every Gaussian in probability and deep learning traces back to this computation.

Example 3 — Integrand with x2+y2

Compute ∬D​(x2+y2)3/2,dA where D=1≤x2+y2≤4 (annulus).

In polar: x2+y2=r2, so (x2+y2)3/2=r3. Domain: r∈[1,2], θ∈[0,2π].

=∫02π​∫12​r3⋅r,dr,dθ=2π∫12​r4,dr=2π⋅5r5​​12​=2π⋅531​=562π​

Compute the area enclosed by one petal of the rose r=cos(2θ).

Area=∫−π/4π/4​∫0cos(2θ)​r,dr,dθ=∫−π/4π/4​2cos2(2θ)​,dθ

Example 6 — Converting a Cartesian Integral to Polar

Evaluate ∫01​∫01−x2​​ex2+y2,dy,dx.

Identify the region. This is the quarter-disk in the first quadrant: x2+y2≤1, x≥0, y≥0.

Polar translation: r∈[0,1], θ∈[0,π/2]. Integrand: er2.

=∫0π/2​∫01​er2,r,dr,dθ=2π​⋅21​[er2]01​=4π​(e−1)

§6 — The Change of Variables Theorem (Full Generality)

The polar substitution is a special case of the general Change of Variables theorem. This section states the general theorem so that polar is properly contextualised.

Theorem (Change of Variables). Let Φ:D∗→D be a C1 bijection (except possibly on a set of measure zero), where D∗,D⊂R2. Then:

∬D​f(x,y),dA=∬D∗​f(Φ(u,v)),​∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​​,du,dv

where ​∂(u,v)∂(x,y)​​=∣detJΦ​∣ is the absolute Jacobian determinant.

For polar coordinates: Φ(r,θ)=(rcosθ,rsinθ), ∣detJΦ​∣=r.

Geometric meaning of the Jacobian. ∣detJΦ​∣ measures the local area magnification of Φ: a small region near (u,v) of area δA in the (u,v)-plane maps to a region of area ≈∣detJΦ​∣⋅δA in the (x,y)-plane. The Jacobian is the correction factor ensuring areas are correctly accounted for.

(r,θ)-plane                    (x,y)-plane
                    Φ
  ┌──────┐   ─────────────→   ╭──────╮
  │ Δr Δθ│                   ╱  r·Δr·Δθ ╲
  └──────┘                  ╱            ╲
  area = ΔrΔθ               ╰────────────╯
                              area = r·ΔrΔθ

  magnification factor = r = |det J_Φ|

§7 — Common Errors and Pathologies

Error 1 — Forgetting the r Factor

∬D​f,dA=∫αβ​∫r1​r2​​f(rcosθ,rsinθ),dr,dθ

The correct formula always has r,dr,dθ. Forgetting r produces a systematically wrong answer with no warning sign.

Mnemonic. dA=dx,dy=r,dr,dθ. The area element on the left is the same object expressed in two coordinate systems.

Error 2 — Wrong θ-Range for a Full Circle

A full circle requires θ∈[0,2π], not [0,π]. The interval [0,π] gives a half-disk.

Error 3 — Origin Included When r1​=0

When r1​=0, Φ is not injective at the origin (every θ maps to (0,0)). This is legal because r=0 has measure zero in the (r,θ)-plane — the change-of-variables theorem allows failures on measure-zero sets.

Error 4 — Applying Polar to a Non-Circular Domain

Polar coordinates are optimal for circular geometry. For ellipses a2x2​+b2y2​≤1, use the elliptic substitution x=arcosθ, y=brsinθ (Jacobian: abr), not standard polar.

Error 5 — Sign of r

Always r≥0 in our convention. If a polar curve formula gives r<0 for some θ, the curve traces a reflected point — handle by restricting θ or using ∣r∣.

§8 — Symmetry in Polar Coordinates

Symmetry is easier to exploit in polar form.

SymmetryConditionSimplification
About x-axisf(r,−θ)=f(r,θ) and D symmetric∫02π​→2∫0π​
About y-axisf(r,π−θ)=f(r,θ) and D symmetric∫02π​→2∫0π​
Full rotationalf depends only on r∫02π​dθ=2π factors out
n-fold rotationalf(r,θ+2π/n)=f(r,θ)∫02π​=n∫02π/n​

Key example. If f(x,y)=g(r) depends only on r (radially symmetric):

∬D​g(r),dA=∫02π​∫0R​g(r),r,dr,dθ=2π∫0R​g(r),r,dr

§9 — The Polar Coordinate Machine: Step-by-Step Protocol

GIVEN: ∫∫_D f(x,y) dA

STEP 1.  Does the domain D have circular/radial structure?
         └── No → stay Cartesian (or use other substitution)
         └── Yes → proceed

STEP 2.  Describe D in polar:
         Find α, β (θ-range) and r₁(θ), r₂(θ) (r-bounds at each θ).
         Draw the region. Label all boundary curves in polar form.

STEP 3.  Substitute:
         x = r cosθ,  y = r sinθ
         Replace all occurrences of x²+y² with r².

STEP 4.  Write the integral:
         ∫_α^β ∫_{r₁(θ)}^{r₂(θ)}  f(rcosθ, rsinθ) · r  dr dθ
                                                        ↑
                                               NEVER FORGET THIS

STEP 5.  Evaluate inner integral (in r), then outer (in θ).

STEP 6.  Check dimensional sanity: Area integrals should give [length]².

§10 — Connection to Probability and Machine Learning

10.1 The Gaussian Distribution — Its Very Existence Depends on This

The univariate normal distribution N(0,1) has density 2π​1​e−x2/2. The normalisation constant 2π​ comes from:

∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx=2π​

Proof: Substitute x=t/2​ to get 2​∫e−t2/2,dt… which reduces to our Gaussian integral. So the very fact that the standard normal density integrates to 1 is a consequence of polar coordinates applied to a double integral. Without this calculation, the Gaussian distribution is not well-defined.

10.2 Multivariate Gaussian

For x∈R2 with covariance Σ=σ2I:

p(x)=2πσ21​e−∣x∣2/(2σ2)

Normalisation check:

∬R2​p(x,y),dA=2πσ21​∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2/(2σ2),r,dr,dθ=2πσ21​⋅2π⋅σ2=1

This is exactly the polar double integral computation, and it must equal 1 for p to be a valid density.

10.3 Reparameterisation Trick in VAEs (Radial Decomposition)

In Variational Autoencoders, the latent space prior is N(0,I). Sampling from this prior and computing expectations uses the identity:

Ez∼N(0,I)​[f(z)]=∬f(x,y),2πe−(x2+y2)/2​,dA=∫0∞​∫02π​f(r,θ),2πe−r2/2​,r,dθ,dr

Decomposing into radial and angular components (polar coordinates) underlies the radial basis function interpretation of the latent space and the geometry of the latent manifold.

10.4 Attention Mechanisms — Radial Decay

In some spatial attention architectures (e.g., for point clouds or 2D feature maps), attention weights decay radially:

α(q,k)=exp!(−2σ2∣q−k∣2​)

Computing the total attention mass over a spatial region D is:

∫D​α(q,k),dA=∬D​e−r2/(2σ2),r,dr,dθ

(centring at q). This is a polar integral, and its closed form informs how to set σ for a desired effective receptive field size.

10.5 Score-Based Generative Models (Diffusion)

The score function ∇x​logp(x) for a radially symmetric density p(x)∝e−V(r) is:

∇x​logp=−V′(r),r^=−V′(r),rx​

Computing normalisation constants and partition functions for such densities requires polar integration. Diffusion model theory (DDPM, Score SDE) extensively uses radially-symmetric reference distributions whose analysis is pure polar calculus.

§11 — Extended Example: Normalising an Unnormalised Density

Problem. The function f(x,y)=Ce−(x2+y2)(1+x2+y2) is a probability density on R2. Find C.

We need ∬R2​f,dA=1, so C−1=∬R2​e−(x2+y2)(1+x2+y2),dA.

Polar form. r∈[0,∞), θ∈[0,2π], integrand =e−r2(1+r2):

C−1=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2(1+r2),r,dr,dθ=2π∫0∞​(re−r2+r3e−r2),dr

Compute each piece using ∫0∞​r2k+1e−r2,dr=2k!​:

  • ∫0∞​re−r2,dr=20!​=21​
  • ∫0∞​r3e−r2,dr=21!​=21​

C−1=2π(21​+21​)=2π⟹C=2π1​​

This is a non-trivial unnormalised density that arises in practice (it is related to the Chi-squared distribution), and polar coordinates make the normalisation tractable.

§12 — Connections to Other Coordinate Systems

Polar coordinates in 2D are the first member of a family:

DimensionCoordinatesJacobianArea/Volume Element
2DPolar (r,θ)rr,dr,dθ
3D (cylinder)Cylindrical (r,θ,z)rr,dr,dθ,dz
3D (sphere)Spherical (ρ,ϕ,θ)ρ2sinϕρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ
General(u1​,…,un​)$\det J

The Jacobian r (polar) generalises to r (cylindrical) and ρ2sinϕ (spherical). In all cases, it is the determinant of the coordinate transformation’s Jacobian matrix.

Main Formula:

x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ,x2+y2=r2,dA=r,dr,dθ
