“Spherical coordinates are nature’s own coordinate system. Gravity, electrostatics, quantum mechanics — every central force law lives here. When the problem has a center, use the coordinates built around it.”

0. Motivation — The Failure of Cartesian and Cylindrical

Consider the integral:

∭E​e(x2+y2+z2)3/2,dV,E:x2+y2+z2≤1

Cartesian: z-limits involve 1−x2−y2​, the integrand is hopeless.

Cylindrical: z-limits become 1−r2​, integrand becomes e(r2+z2)3/2 — still intractable.

Spherical: The sphere x2+y2+z2=1 becomes simply ρ=1. The integrand becomes eρ3.

∫02π​∫0π​∫01​eρ3⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ=4π∫01​ρ2eρ3,dρ=34π​(e−1)

Done. Three lines. This is why spherical coordinates exist.

The rule: whenever a region is a ball, spherical shell, cone, or any surface defined by x2+y2+z2=const, spherical coordinates are almost certainly the right choice.

1. The Coordinate System

1.1 Definition

x=ρsinϕcosθ,y=ρsinϕsinθ,z=ρcosϕ​

with inverse relations:

ρ=x2+y2+z2​,ϕ=arccos!(ρz​),θ=arctan!(xy​)

Domain conventions:

ρ≥0,0≤ϕ≤π,0≤θ≤2π

1.2 What Each Variable Measures

VariableNameMeaning
ρradial distanceDistance from the origin (not from z-axis!)
ϕpolar / zenith angleAngle measured downward from the +z-axis
θazimuthal angleSame θ as in cylindrical — angle in xy-plane from +x-axis

Critical distinction from cylindrical. In cylindrical, r is distance from the z-axis. In spherical, ρ is distance from the origin. At the equator (ϕ=π/2), we have ρ=r, but in general r=ρsinϕ=ρ.

2. The Volume Element — Deriving dV=ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

2.1 Jacobian Derivation (Full Computation)

The transformation T:(ρ,ϕ,θ)↦(x,y,z) has Jacobian matrix:

J=∂(ρ,ϕ,θ)∂(x,y,z)​=(sinϕcosθ​ρcosϕcosθ​−ρsinϕsinθ\[4pt]sinϕsinθ​ρcosϕsinθ​ρsinϕcosθ\[4pt]cosϕ​−ρsinϕ​0​)

Expand along the third row:

detJ=cosϕ⋅M31​+(−ρsinϕ)⋅M32​+0⋅M33​

Computing M31​ (cofactor of position (3,1), sign +):

M31​=(+1)det(ρcosϕcosθrhocosϕsinθ​−ρsinϕsinθρsinϕcosθ​) =ρ2cosϕsinϕcos2θ+ρ2cosϕsinϕsin2θ=ρ2cosϕsinϕ

Computing M32​ (cofactor of position (3,2), sign −):

M32​=(−1)det(sinϕcosθsinϕsinθ​−ρsinϕsinθρsinϕcosθ​) =(−1)(ρsin2ϕcos2θ+ρsin2ϕsin2θ)=−ρsin2ϕ

Combining:

detJ=cosϕ⋅(ρ2cosϕsinϕ)+(−ρsinϕ)⋅(−ρsin2ϕ) =ρ2cos2ϕsinϕ+ρ2sin3ϕ=ρ2sinϕ(cos2ϕ+sin2ϕ)=ρ2sinϕ

Since 0≤ϕ≤π, we have sinϕ≥0, so ∣detJ∣=ρ2sinϕ. Therefore:

dV=ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ​

2.2 Geometric Derivation

The spherical volume element is an infinitesimal "orange wedge":

         ┌─────────────────────────┐
        /│                        /│   ← top face at ρ+dρ
       / │                       / │
      /  │                      /  │
     └───┴─────────────────────┘   │
     │   │                     │   │
     │   └─────────────────────┴───┘
     │  /                      │  /
     │ /                       │ /
     │/                        │/
     └─────────────────────────┘
     ↑ bottom face at ρ

  Three edge lengths:
    Radial:      dρ
    Meridional:  ρ dφ          (arc at radius ρ, angle dφ)
    Azimuthal:   ρ sinφ dθ     (arc at radius ρ sinφ = r, angle dθ)

  Volume = dρ × ρ dφ × ρ sinφ dθ = ρ² sinφ  dρ dφ dθ  ✓

Why ρsinϕ? The azimuthal arc sweeps at horizontal distance r=ρsinϕ from the z-axis. At the pole (ϕ=0), this collapses to zero — a small azimuthal step at the north pole sweeps almost no volume. At the equator (ϕ=π/2), it’s maximized at ρ,dθ.

The sinϕ factor is not bookkeeping. It encodes the fact that spherical coordinates are singular at the poles: all longitude lines converge there, so volume elements vanish.

3. The Integration Formula

Theorem. If E is a solid region described in spherical coordinates, and f is continuous on E:

∭E​f(x,y,z),dV=∫θ1​θ2​​∫ϕ1​ϕ2​​∫ρ1​(ϕ,θ)ρ2​(ϕ,θ)​f(ρsinϕcosθ,,ρsinϕsinθ,,ρcosϕ)⋅ρ2sinϕ,,dρ,dϕ,dθ​

Standard order: dρ,dϕ,dθ — innermost to outermost.

Mnemonic for limits:

  • θ: azimuthal sweep, typically 0 to 2π (or a fraction for partial wedges)
  • ϕ: polar sweep, 0 (north pole) to π (south pole), or a sub-range for cones
  • ρ: radial extent, from inner surface to outer surface

4. Strategy — When to Use Spherical Coordinates

SignalExample
Region is a ball or spherical shellx2+y2+z2≤R2
Integrand contains x2+y2+z2f=e(x2+y2+z2)n, f=(x2+y2+z2)k/2
Cone boundaryz=x2+y2​ (becomes ϕ=π/4)
Sphere meets coneClassic: find ϕ at intersection
Integrand is a function of ρ onlyImmediate factoring of the integral

Do NOT use spherical when the region has flat walls, rectangular cross-sections, or cylindrical symmetry without spherical boundaries — cylindrical or Cartesian will be simpler.

5. Translating Surfaces to Spherical — The Essential Dictionary

This skill is what separates setup errors from clean solutions.

Cartesian / CylindricalSphericalNotes
x2+y2+z2=R2ρ=RSphere
z=x2+y2​ϕ=π/4Upper cone, 45° from +z
z=3(x2+y2)​ϕ=π/6Steeper cone, 30° from +z
z=−x2+y2​ϕ=3π/4Lower cone
z=0ϕ=π/2xy-plane
z=c>0ρ=c/cosϕ=csecϕHorizontal plane above origin
x2+y2+z2=2zρ=2cosϕSphere centered at (0,0,1)
z=R2−x2−y2​ρ=R, 0≤ϕ≤π/2Upper hemisphere

Deriving z=c in spherical. We have z=ρcosϕ=c, so ρ=c/cosϕ=csecϕ. This means the ρ-limit depends on ϕ — always check this.

Deriving the cone z=x2+y2​. In spherical: ρcosϕ=ρ2sin2ϕcos2θ+ρ2sin2ϕsin2θ​=ρsinϕ. So cosϕ=sinϕ⇒tanϕ=1⇒ϕ=π/4.

6. Worked Examples

Example 6.1 — Volume of a Ball (Sanity Check)

V=∫02π​∫0π​∫0R​ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

Inner (ρ): ∫0R​ρ2,dρ=3R3​

Middle (ϕ): ∫0π​sinϕ,dϕ=[−cosϕ]0π​=−(−1)−(−(1))=2

Outer (θ): ∫02π​32R3​,dθ=34πR3​

V=34​πR3​✓

Example 6.2 — Integral Over a Ball

Problem. Evaluate ∭B​(x2+y2+z2),dV where B is the ball ρ≤a.

In spherical: x2+y2+z2=ρ2.

I=∫02π​∫0π​∫0a​ρ2⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ=∫02π​dθ⋅∫0π​sinϕ,dϕ⋅∫0a​ρ4,dρ

=2π⋅2⋅5a5​=54πa5​​

Notice how the integral factored into three independent single-variable integrals — this happens whenever the integrand and the region both have full spherical symmetry.

Example 6.3 — Upper Hemisphere

Problem. Evaluate ∭E​z,dV where E is the upper hemisphere x2+y2+z2≤1, z≥0.

The condition z≥0 means ρcosϕ≥0, i.e., cosϕ≥0, i.e., ϕ∈[0,π/2].

In spherical: z=ρcosϕ.

I=∫02π​∫0π/2​∫01​(ρcosϕ)⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

=∫02π​dθ⋅∫0π/2​cosϕsinϕ,dϕ⋅∫01​ρ3,dρ

θ: 2π

ϕ: ∫0π/2​cosϕsinϕ,dϕ=21​∫0π/2​sin(2ϕ),dϕ=21​⋅[−2cos2ϕ​]0π/2​=21​⋅1=21​

ρ: ∫01​ρ3,dρ=41​

I=2π⋅21​⋅41​=4π​​

Example 6.4 — Solid Between Cone and Sphere (The Canonical Example)

Problem. Find the volume of the solid E that lies inside the sphere x2+y2+z2=4 and above the cone z=x2+y2​.

Translation:

  • Sphere: ρ=2
  • Cone: ϕ=π/4 (from the dictionary above)
  • “Above cone” means z>x2+y2​, i.e., cosϕ>sinϕ, i.e., ϕ<π/4

So: 0≤θ≤2π, 0≤ϕ≤π/4, 0≤ρ≤2.

V=∫02π​∫0π/4​∫02​ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

ρ: ∫02​ρ2,dρ=38​

ϕ: ∫0π/4​sinϕ,dϕ=[−cosϕ]0π/4​=1−22​​=22−2​​

θ: 2π

V=2π⋅22−2​​⋅38​=38π(2−2​)​​

Compare with the cylindrical computation of the same solid in the previous note — same answer, less work.

Example 6.5 — ρ-Limits Depending on ϕ: Cone Cut by Plane

Problem. Find the volume of the solid inside the cone ϕ=π/3 and below the plane z=1.

The plane z=1 in spherical: ρcosϕ=1⇒ρ=secϕ.

For the cone 0≤ϕ≤π/3: at each ϕ, ρ runs from 0 to secϕ.

V=∫02π​∫0π/3​∫0secϕ​ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

Inner (ρ): ∫0secϕ​ρ2,dρ=3sec3ϕ​

Middle (ϕ):

∫0π/3​3sec3ϕ​sinϕ,dϕ=31​∫0π/3​cos3ϕsinϕ​,dϕ

Let u=cosϕ, du=−sinϕ,dϕ:

=31​∫11/2​u3−du​=31​∫1/21​u3du​=31​⋅[−2u21​]1/21​=31​⋅(−21​+2)=31​⋅23​=21​

Outer (θ): 2π⋅21​=π

V=π​

Example 6.6 — Sphere Centered Off-Origin: ρ=2cosϕ

Problem. Find the volume of the solid enclosed by the sphere x2+y2+(z−1)2=1.

Translation. Expand: x2+y2+z2−2z=0⇒ρ2−2ρcosϕ=0⇒ρ=2cosϕ.

This sphere passes through the origin (ρ=0 when ϕ=π/2) and has top at ρ=2 (ϕ=0). The solid is 0≤ρ≤2cosϕ, and ϕ ranges from 0 to π/2 (for ρ≥0 we need cosϕ≥0).

V=∫02π​∫0π/2​∫02cosϕ​ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

Inner (ρ): ∫02cosϕ​ρ2,dρ=38cos3ϕ​

Middle (ϕ):

∫0π/2​38cos3ϕ​sinϕ,dϕ=38​⋅[−4cos4ϕ​]0π/2​=38​⋅41​=32​

Outer (θ): 2π⋅32​=34π​

V=34π​​

Check: The sphere has radius 1, so V=34​π(1)3=34π​. ✓

Example 6.7 — Charge Density Over a Shell

Problem. A spherical shell 1≤ρ≤2 has charge density δ=ρ−2. Find total charge.

Q=∫02π​∫0π​∫12​ρ−2⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ=∫02π​dθ⋅∫0π​sinϕ,dϕ⋅∫12​dρ

=2π⋅2⋅1=4π​

Notice: ρ−2⋅ρ2=1, so the ρ-integrand is just 1. The ρ2 in dV exactly cancelled the ρ−2 density — a clean example of the Jacobian interacting with the integrand.

7. Setting Up Limits — Systematic Procedure

Step 1: Identify all boundary surfaces and translate to spherical

Use the dictionary in §5. Every surface must become an equation in ρ,ϕ,θ.

Step 2: Determine θ-range

  • Full solid of revolution about z-axis: 0≤θ≤2π
  • First octant (x≥0,y≥0): 0≤θ≤π/2
  • Half-space (y≥0): 0≤θ≤π

Step 3: Determine ϕ-range

Think: from which polar angle to which polar angle does the solid span?

  • Full ball: 0≤ϕ≤π
  • Upper hemisphere: 0≤ϕ≤π/2
  • Inside cone z=x2+y2​: ϕ≤π/4 (above cone) or ϕ≥π/4 (below cone)
  • Between two cones: ϕ1​≤ϕ≤ϕ2​

Step 4: Determine ρ-range for each (ϕ,θ)

At each direction (ϕ,θ), the ray from the origin enters the solid at ρ=ρmin​(ϕ,θ) and exits at ρ=ρmax​(ϕ,θ).

  • For convex regions containing origin: 0≤ρ≤ρmax​(ϕ)
  • For shells: ρmin​≤ρ≤ρmax​

Step 5: Substitute and write the iterated integral

∫θ1​θ2​​∫ϕ1​ϕ2​​∫ρ1​(ϕ,θ)ρ2​(ϕ,θ)​f(ρsinϕcosθ,ρsinϕsinθ,ρcosϕ)⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ

11. Connection to AI, Machine Learning & Deep Learning

11.1 Gaussian Integral — The Foundation of Probability in ML

The most important integral in all of statistics and machine learning is:

∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π​

Its n-dimensional generalization is evaluated using spherical coordinates. For n=3:

(∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx)3=∭R3​e−(x2+y2+z2),dV

In spherical: x2+y2+z2=ρ2, and the integral becomes:

∫02π​∫0π​∫0∞​e−ρ2⋅ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ=4π∫0∞​ρ2e−ρ2,dρ=4π⋅4π​​=π3/2

Therefore ∫−∞∞​e−x2,dx=π1/2. The normalization of the Gaussian distribution N(0,1) rests on this exact computation.

Every training objective involving cross-entropy, KL divergence, or maximum likelihood descends from this fact.

11.2 Multivariate Gaussian — The Engine of Deep Learning

The n-dimensional Gaussian:

p(x)=(2π)n/2∣Σ∣1/21​exp!(−21​xTΣ−1x)

When Σ=σ2I (isotropic), the density is constant on spheres ∣x∣2=c — it has perfect spherical symmetry. All integrals over this distribution reduce to 1D integrals over ρ=∣x∣ using the n-dimensional spherical volume element:

dVn​=ρn−1Sn−1​,dρ,Sn−1​=Γ(n/2)2πn/2​

This is the n-dimensional analog of ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ. Every expectation computation under an isotropic Gaussian uses this formula.

11.3 Normalizing Flows and the Spherical Jacobian

In normalizing flows for generative modeling, a base distribution (usually Gaussian) is pushed through an invertible neural network fθ​:Rn→Rn. The density transforms as:

pX​(x)=pZ​(f(x))​det∂x∂f​​

The term ​det∂x∂f​​ is exactly the Jacobian determinant — the generalization of ρ2sinϕ to arbitrary transformations. Computing and differentiating this Jacobian efficiently is the central algorithmic challenge of normalizing flows (hence architectures like RealNVP and Glow that make detJ cheap to compute).

11.4 Diffusion Models and Score Matching

Score-based diffusion models (DDPM, DDIM, Score-SDE) define a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data. The score function ∇x​logp(x) for an isotropic Gaussian points radially toward the mean:

∇x​logN(x;μ,σ2I)=−σ2x−μ​

This is a radial vector field — it points in the ρ^​ direction in spherical coordinates. The denoising step of a diffusion model is geometrically a step along ρ^​. The ergodic theory of the reverse diffusion process (why it converges) relies on integration over spherical shells — Gauss’s divergence theorem in Rn.

11.5 Equivariant Networks — SE(3) and Spherical Harmonics

SE(3)-equivariant neural networks (used in protein structure prediction, molecular dynamics, drug design) decompose features using spherical harmonic basis functions Ylm​(ϕ,θ), which are the natural orthonormal basis on the sphere S2.

The inner product on this space is:

⟨f,g⟩=∫02π​∫0π​f(ϕ,θ),g(ϕ,θ),sinϕ,dϕ,dθ

The sinϕ here is the angular part of the spherical Jacobian — the same sinϕ from dV=ρ2sinϕ,dρ,dϕ,dθ. Every convolution and feature aggregation in SE(3)-equivariant networks (like DiffDock, AlphaFold2’s structure module, NequIP) is an integral weighted by sinϕ,dϕ,dθ.
