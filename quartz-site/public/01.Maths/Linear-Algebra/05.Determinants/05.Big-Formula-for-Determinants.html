05.Big Formula for Determinants

05.Big Formula for Determinants

Apr 07, 202610 min read

What the Big Formula Is

We now have three ways to compute a determinant: the pivot formula (efficient, O(n3)), the cofactor expansion (recursive, good for small n by hand), and the permutation formula. The permutation formula is what Strang calls the big formula — and it earns that name because it is the complete, explicit, fully expanded expression for det(A) with no recursion, no elimination, just a single sum over all n! permutations.

det(A)=σ∈Sn​∑​sgn(σ),a1,σ(1)​,a2,σ(2)​⋯an,σ(n)​​

Every other formula — cofactor expansion, pivot product — is a reorganization or consequence of this one. The big formula is the ground truth.

This note is about understanding it completely: where every term comes from, why the signs are what they are, and how the n! terms organize themselves into the patterns we recognize.

Where the Big Formula Comes From

The derivation starts from the three properties of the determinant and uses nothing else.

Write each row i of A as a linear combination of the standard basis rows:

rowi​=ai1​e1T​+ai2​e2T​+⋯+ain​enT​=∑j=1n​aij​ejT​

Apply multilinearity (Property 3) in every row simultaneously. Expanding row 1 first, then row 2, and so on:

det(A)=∑j1​=1n​∑j2​=1n​⋯∑jn​=1n​a1,j1​​,a2,j2​​⋯an,jn​​⋅det(ej1​T​ ej2​T​ ⋮ ejn​T​​)

This is a sum of nn terms — one for every way to choose a column index ji​∈1,…,n for each row i.

Which terms survive? The matrix inside the determinant has row i equal to eji​T​. If two indices coincide — ji​=jk​ for some i=k — then two rows of this matrix are identical and its determinant is zero (Property 4). So only terms where j1​,j2​,…,jn​ are all distinct contribute.

A sequence of distinct values from 1,…,n is exactly a permutation σ, with ji​=σ(i). The surviving terms:

det(A)=∑σ∈Sn​​a1,σ(1)​,a2,σ(2)​⋯an,σ(n)​⋅det(Pσ​)

where Pσ​ is the permutation matrix with eσ(i)T​ as its i-th row. Since Pσ​ is obtained from I by inv(σ) transpositions, det(Pσ​)=sgn(σ)=(−1)inv(σ).

Therefore:

det(A)=∑σ∈Sn​​sgn(σ)∏i=1n​ai,σ(i)​□

Anatomy of a Single Term

Each term in the big formula has a definite structure. For a fixed permutation σ:

termσ​=sgn(σ)⋅a1,σ(1)​⋅a2,σ(2)​⋯an,σ(n)​

  • One entry from each row: the factor ai,σ(i)​ comes from row i.
  • One entry from each column: since σ is a bijection, the values σ(1),σ(2),…,σ(n) are all distinct — so one entry from each column.
  • A sign: sgn(σ)=±1 depending on the parity of σ.

This is the rook placement picture. Each term corresponds to placing n non-attacking rooks on an n×n chessboard — one per row, one per column — and multiplying the entries at those positions, signed by the permutation’s parity.

The Full 3×3 Formula

A=(a11​​a12​​a13​\a21​​a22​​a23​\a31​​a32​​a33​​)

S3​ has 3!=6 elements. I will go through every single one.

Even permutations (sgn=+1):

σ=e=(1,2,3): picks (a11​,a22​,a33​) — the main diagonal. +a11​a22​a33​

σ=(123)=(2,3,1): picks (a12​,a23​,a31​) — one step right of the diagonal, wrapping around. +a12​a23​a31​

σ=(132)=(3,1,2): picks (a13​,a21​,a32​) — one step left of the diagonal, wrapping around. +a13​a21​a32​

Odd permutations (sgn=−1):

σ=(12)=(2,1,3): picks (a12​,a21​,a33​) — swap columns 1 and 2, keep column 3. −a12​a21​a33​

σ=(23)=(1,3,2): picks (a11​,a23​,a32​) — keep column 1, swap columns 2 and 3. −a11​a23​a32​

σ=(13)=(3,2,1): picks (a13​,a22​,a31​) — the anti-diagonal. −a13​a22​a31​

The full formula:

det(A)=a11​a22​a33​+a12​a23​a31​+a13​a21​a32​−a12​a21​a33​−a11​a23​a32​−a13​a22​a31​

Visualizing the six terms on the matrix:

(a11​​a12​​a13​\a21​​a22​​a23​\a31​​a32​​a33​​)

Positive terms trace three “diagonal-like” paths going right:

  • a11​→a22​→a33​ (main diagonal)
  • a12​→a23​→a31​ (step right, wrap)
  • a13​→a21​→a32​ (step left, wrap — actually the cyclic shift going left)

Negative terms trace three “anti-diagonal-like” paths:

  • a13​→a22​→a31​ (anti-diagonal)
  • a12​→a21​→a33​ (anti-diagonal shifted)
  • a11​→a23​→a32​ (anti-diagonal shifted the other way)

This is Sarrus’ rule — a mnemonic for the 3×3 determinant. Write the matrix and append the first two columns to the right:

(a11​​a12​​a13​​a11​​a12​\a21​​a22​​a23​​a21​​a22​\a31​​a32​​a33​​a31​​a32​​)

Multiply along the three diagonals going down-right (positive) and the three going down-left (negative).

Warning: Sarrus’ rule works only for 3×3. It does not generalize to 4×4.

For a general 4×4 matrix, applying the big formula means computing 24 products of 4 entries each. This is why elimination is preferred — but seeing all 24 terms once makes the structure of the determinant completely concrete.

How the Big Formula Implies All Properties

Every property of the determinant follows from the big formula. Let me check a few.

Row swap flips sign. Swapping rows p and q replaces ap,σ(p)​ by aq,σ(p)​ and aq,σ(q)​ by ap,σ(q)​ in every term. This is the same as replacing σ by the permutation σ∘(pq) — composing σ with the transposition. Since composing with a transposition flips sgn, every term changes sign. The sum changes sign. ✓

Linearity in row r. If row r is replaced by αu+βv, then every term ar,σ(r)​ becomes αuσ(r)​+βvσ(r)​. Distributing across the product gives two sums — one with u entries, one with v entries — exactly the linearity statement. ✓

Equal rows give zero. If rows p and q are equal, ap,j​=aq,j​ for all j. Consider any permutation σ paired with the permutation σ′=σ∘(pq) (compose with the swap of p and q). These two permutations have opposite signs but their products of entries are equal (since ap,⋅​=aq,⋅​). So every term cancels with its partner, and the sum is zero. ✓

The Big Formula for Special Matrices

Diagonal matrix. aij​=0 for i=j. In any term ∏i​ai,σ(i)​, we need σ(i)=i for all i, otherwise some factor is zero. The only permutation with σ(i)=i for all i is the identity. So:

det(diag(d1​,…,dn​))=d1​d2​⋯dn​

Only one of the n! terms survives.

Upper triangular matrix. aij​=0 for i>j. In a term ∏i​ai,σ(i)​, we need σ(i)≥i for all i (otherwise some factor ai,σ(i)​ is below the diagonal and equals zero). The only permutation with σ(i)≥i for all i is the identity (a pigeonhole argument: if σ(1)≥1 and σ is a bijection, the only way is σ=e). So again only one term survives:

det(upper triangular)=a11​a22​⋯ann​

Lower triangular. By the same argument (or by det(A)=det(AT)), the only surviving permutation is again the identity.

det(lower triangular)=a11​a22​⋯ann​

Permutation matrix Pτ​. The entries are (Pτ​)ij​=1 if j=τ(i), else 0. In the big formula, the only term that is nonzero is the one with σ=τ, since any other σ=τ would have σ(i)=τ(i) for some i, making ai,σ(i)​=0. So:

det(Pτ​)=sgn(τ)

as expected.

The Big Formula as a Multilinear Map

The big formula reveals that det:Rn×n→R is a multilinear alternating form on the rows of A.

  • Multilinear: linear in each row separately.
  • Alternating: swapping two rows changes the sign.
  • Normalized: det(I)=1.

These three properties uniquely characterize the determinant. The big formula is the explicit form of the unique multilinear alternating normalized function on n×n matrices.

This is the deepest way to understand what the determinant is: not a formula, but the unique function satisfying those three properties. The big formula is then the derivation of what that function must look like when you expand it completely.

The Three Formulas, Side by Side

It is worth pausing to see how all three formulas relate to one another.

The big formula is the complete sum: det(A)=∑σ∈Sn​​sgn(σ)∏i​ai,σ(i)​

The cofactor expansion groups the n! terms by fixing σ(i)=j for some row i: det(A)=∑j=1n​aij​Cij​ Each cofactor Cij​ collects (n−1)! terms from the big formula.

The pivot formula re-expresses the whole computation as a product after elimination: det(A)=(−1)s⋅d1​d2​⋯dn​ The elimination process implicitly rearranges the n! terms into this product form via the LU factorization.

They compute the same number. They illuminate different aspects of why that number has the properties it does.

Things to Remember (for Myself)

  • The big formula is ∑σ∈Sn​​sgn(σ)∏i​ai,σ(i)​. One term per permutation, n! terms total.
  • Each term picks exactly one entry from each row and one from each column — the rook placement picture.
  • The sign of each term is ±1 depending on the parity of the permutation: even permutations give +, odd give −.
  • For n=2: 2 terms. For n=3: 6 terms. For n=4: 24 terms. Beyond n=4, never apply this by hand.
  • For triangular matrices, only one term survives (the main diagonal). This is the proof that det of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.
  • Sarrus’ rule is the 3×3 big formula organized by diagonal paths. It does not work for 4×4.
  • The big formula is the derivation of all properties — not a consequence of them but the thing from which they follow when derived from Properties 1–3.
