14.The Cauchy Schwarz Inequality

14.The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Apr 12, 202610 min read

Theorem

For any u,v∈Rn: ∣u⋅v∣≤∣u∣,∣v∣​

with equality if and only if u and v are linearly dependent — that is, one is a scalar multiple of the other, or one is zero.

Squaring both sides (valid since both sides are nonnegative):

(u⋅v)2≤∣u∣2,∣v∣2=(u⋅u)(v⋅v)

The squared form is cleaner to work with algebraically and is often what appears in proofs.

What This Says Geometrically

Recall that the dot product satisfies u⋅v=∣u∣∣v∣cosθ, where θ is the angle between the two vectors. Cauchy–Schwarz is nothing more than the statement: ∣cosθ∣≤1

Every form of this inequality in every setting is a version of this statement. The angle between two vectors is real, so its cosine cannot exceed 1 in absolute value. The rest is generalizations to infinite dimensions and abstract spaces.

Proof 1:

This is the proof that generalizes furthest. It works in any inner product space, not just Rn. It requires only that the inner product is positive definite.

Setup. If v=0, both sides are zero — trivial. Assume v=0.

Define f:R→R by: f(t)=∣u−tv∣2 → This is the squared distance from u to the vector tv on the line through v.

Key observation. Since we are squaring a length: f(t)≥0for all t∈R Expand. Using bi-linearity of the inner product: f(t)=(u−tv)⋅(u−tv)=∣u∣2−2t(u⋅v)+t2∣v∣2 Rewriting in standard form at2+bt+c:f(t)=a∣v∣2​​,t2−−b2(u⋅v)​​,t+c∣u∣2​​ Apply the discriminant condition. This is a quadratic in t with positive leading coefficient a=∣v∣2>0. A nonnegative quadratic with positive leading coefficient must have non-positive discriminant: b2−4ac≤0

Substitute. With a=∣v∣2, b=−2(u⋅v), c=∣u∣2: (−2(u⋅v))2−4,∣v∣2,∣u∣2≤0 4(u⋅v)2−4∣v∣2∣u∣2≤0 (u⋅v)2≤∣u∣2,∣v∣2

Taking square roots of both sides (both sides nonnegative): ∣u⋅v∣≤∣u∣,∣v∣■

The Equality Conditio: Proof 1 in Detail

This deserves its own careful treatment.

  • Equality ∣u⋅v∣=∣u∣∣v∣ occurs when (u⋅v)2=∣u∣2∣v∣2, which means the discriminant is exactly zero. Discriminant =0 means the parabola f(t) touches the t-axis at exactly one point t∗. That means: f(t^*) = 0$$$$ \implies |\mathbf{u} - t^*\mathbf{v}|^2 = 0 A squared norm is zero only for the zero vector:

\mathbf{u} - t^*\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} $$$$\implies \mathbf{u} = t^*\mathbf{v} So u is a scalar multiple of v. They are linearly dependent.

What is t∗? Minimise f(t) by differentiating: f'(t) = 2|\mathbf{v}|^2 t - 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) = 0 $$$$ \implies t^* = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2} This is exactly the scalar projection coefficient. The projection of u onto v is: projv​,u=∣v∣2u⋅v​,v=t∗v So t∗ is asking: how much of v do I need to best approximate u? If the minimum distance is zero, u lies exactly on the line spanned by v.

Proof 2 — Lagrange’s Identity (Algebraic, for Rn)

This proof gives more than just the inequality — it gives an identity that the inequality follows from.

Lagrange’s Identity:

∣u∣2∣v∣2−(u⋅v)2=∑1≤i<j≤n​(ui​vj​−uj​vi​)2

Proof of the identity. Expand the left side:

(∑i​ui2​)!(∑j​vj2​)−(∑i​ui​vi​)2=∑i,j​ui2​vj2​−∑i,j​ui​vi​uj​vj​ Separate the diagonal (i=j) terms, which cancel: =∑i=j​ui2​vj2​−∑i=j​ui​uj​vi​vj​ Use the symmetry i↔j to write this as: =21​∑i=j​(ui2​vj2​+uj2​vi2​−2ui​uj​vi​vj​)=21​∑i=j​(ui​vj​−uj​vi​)2=∑i<j​(ui​vj​−uj​vi​)2■

Cauchy–Schwarz follows immediately. The right side is a sum of squares, hence ≥0: ∣u∣2∣v∣2−(u⋅v)2≥0⟹(u⋅v)2≤∣u∣2∣v∣2

Equality condition. Each term (ui​vj​−uj​vi​)2=0, meaning ui​vj​=uj​vi​ for all i,j. This is precisely the condition for u and v to be proportional.

Special case in R2. The sum has exactly one term (i=1,j=2):

∣u∣2∣v∣2=(u⋅v)2+(u1​v2​−u2​v1​)2

The second term is ∣u×v∣2 (the square of the cross product magnitude). So this identity says:

∣u∣2∣v∣2=∣u∣2∣v∣2cos2θ+∣u∣2∣v∣2sin2θ

which is just cos2θ+sin2θ=1. Cauchy–Schwarz in R2 is Pythagoras in disguise.

Proof 3 — The Projection Argument (Most Geometric)

This is the proof that makes everything transparent.

Decompose u. Project u onto v:

u=projv​u∣v∣2u⋅v​v​​+⊥,ve​​

where the error vector e=u−projv​u satisfies e⋅v=0 (it is perpendicular to v).

Apply Pythagoras. Since projv​u⊥e:

∣u∣2=∣projv​u∣2+∣e∣2

Since ∣e∣2≥0:

∣u∣2≥∣projv​u∣2=∣v∣2(u⋅v)2​

Multiply both sides by ∣v∣2:

∣u∣2∣v∣2≥(u⋅v)2■ Why this proof is the right one to understand. Cauchy–Schwarz says: the shadow of u on v is at most as long as u. The shadow is one component of u. A component cannot exceed the whole. This is visually obvious. The proof just makes it rigorous.

Comparing the Three Proofs

All three proofs are the same idea in different clothing:

ProofKey quantityWhy it’s ≥0
Discriminant∥u−tv∥2 as a quadraticSquared norm

Projection∥e∥2=∥u∥2−∥proj∥2Squared norm

In every case, the inequality comes from the nonnegativity of a squared quantity. That is the entire soul of Cauchy–Schwarz: something nonnegative forces the inequality.

The Classical Form — Finite Sums

Before inner products existed as a concept, Cauchy (1821) proved the inequality for sums. For real numbers a1​,…,an​ and b1​,…,bn​:

(∑i=1n​ai​bi​)2≤(∑i=1n​ai2​)(∑i=1n​bi2​)

This is the vector form with u=(a1​,…,an​)T and v=(b1​,…,bn​)T. The sum ∑ai​bi​ is the dot product.

The Integral Form — L2 Functions

Schwarz (1885) extended the inequality to integrals. For square-integrable functions f,g:[a,b]→R:

(∫ab​f(x)g(x),dx)2≤(∫ab​f(x)2,dx)(∫ab​g(x)2,dx)

This is Cauchy–Schwarz in L2([a,b]) with inner product ⟨f,g⟩=∫ab​f(x)g(x),dx.

The proof is identical. Define f(t)=∫ab​(u(x)−t,v(x))2,dx≥0 for all t. Expand, get a nonnegative quadratic, discriminant ≤0. Word-for-word the discriminant argument.

The Probability Form — Random Variables

For square-integrable random variables X and Y:

∣E[XY]∣≤E[X2]​,E[Y2]​

or in the centred form:

∣Cov(X,Y)∣≤SD(X)⋅SD(Y)

Proof. Define g(t)=E[(X−tY)2]≥0 (expectation of a nonnegative quantity). Expand: g(t)=E[X2]−2t,E[XY]+t2E[Y2]. Nonnegative quadratic, discriminant ≤0:

4(E[XY])2−4,E[X2],E[Y2]≤0⟹∣E[XY]∣≤E[X2],E[Y2]​■

The correlation bound. Applying to X−E[X] and Y−E[Y]:

∣ρ(X,Y)∣=SD(X),SD(Y)∣Cov(X,Y)∣​≤1

This is why correlation is bounded between −1 and 1. Cauchy–Schwarz is the reason.

Equality condition. X=cY almost surely for some constant c. Perfect linear dependence.

Cauchy–Schwarz Implies the Triangle Inequality

This is the logical chain that matters.

Claim. ∣u+v∣≤∣u∣+∣v∣.

Proof. Expand ∣u+v∣2:

∣u+v∣2=∣u∣2+2,u⋅v+∣v∣2

Apply u⋅v≤∣u⋅v∣ and then Cauchy–Schwarz:

≤∣u∣2+2∣u⋅v∣+∣v∣2≤∣u∣2+2∣u∣∣v∣+∣v∣2=(∣u∣+∣v∣)2

Take square roots: ∣u+v∣≤∣u∣+∣v∣. ■

The logical order is:

Positive definiteness⟹Cauchy–Schwarz⟹Triangle inequality⟹Norm axioms satisfied

We never need to verify the triangle inequality separately. Cauchy–Schwarz gives it for free.

The Weighted Version

For positive weights w1​,…,wn​>0:

(∑i=1n​wi​ai​bi​)2≤(∑i=1n​wi​ai2​)(∑i=1n​wi​bi2​)

This is Cauchy–Schwarz in the weighted inner product ⟨a,b⟩w​=∑i​wi​ai​bi​. The proof is unchanged. In probability, if X and Y are discrete with P(X=xi​)=wi​, this is exactly the random-variable form.

Applications

Bounding a dot product without the angle. If ∣u∣≤M and ∣v∣≤N, then ∣u⋅v∣≤MN, without needing to know θ.

AM-GM inequality as a special case. Take u=(a​,b​)T and v=(b​,a​)T in R2:

(a​b​+b​a​)2≤(a+b)(b+a)⟹4ab≤(a+b)2⟹ab​≤2a+b​

Jensen’s inequality for x2. Cauchy–Schwarz with Y=1:

∣E[X⋅1]∣2≤E[X2],E[12]⟹E[X]2≤E[X2]

This is Jensen applied to the convex function x2.

Hölder’s inequality is the Lp-Lq generalisation. Cauchy–Schwarz is the p=q=2 case:

∣E[XY]∣≤∣X∣p​,∣Y∣q​where p1​+q1​=1

The Equality Case — Summary

This is worth stating carefully in every form.

SettingEquality condition
Vectors u,v∈Rnu=cv for some scalar c
Sequences (ai​),(bi​)ai​=c,bi​ for all i
Functions f,g∈L2f=c,g almost everywhere
Random variables X,YX=cY almost surely
Correlation$

Equality does not require u=v. It requires proportionality. The scalar c can be negative — antiparallel vectors also achieve equality.

What Breaks the Inequality

Cauchy–Schwarz relies on positive definiteness of the inner product: ⟨w,w⟩≥0 with equality only at 0.

In Minkowski space (the inner product of special relativity, signature (−,+,+,+)), positive definiteness fails. For timelike vectors, the analogue of Cauchy–Schwarz is reversed. This is not a flaw — it is the geometry of spacetime.

The takeaway: Cauchy–Schwarz is not a universal truth. It is a consequence of positive definiteness. Remove that assumption and the inequality can fail.

Common Errors

Missing the absolute value. The inequality is ∣u⋅v∣≤∣u∣∣v∣ — the absolute value on the left is necessary. Without it, u⋅v≤∣u∣∣v∣ is also true but strictly weaker (it only bounds the dot product from above, not from below).

Confusing equality with u=v. Equality requires u=cv, not u=v. Vectors of different lengths pointing in the same direction achieve equality.

Almost sure vs everywhere. In the random variable form, equality is X=cY almost surely — a set of measure zero where this fails is permitted. For pathological probability spaces this distinction matters.

Direction of the inequality. Cauchy–Schwarz gives an upper bound on ∣u⋅v∣. It gives no lower bound. The dot product can be anywhere in [−∣u∣∣v∣,,∣u∣∣v∣].

Things to Think About

  • The discriminant proof used only positive definiteness, bilinearity, and symmetry. Which of these can be weakened? What happens in a semi-inner product space where ⟨w,w⟩=0 does not force w=0?
  • The projection proof assumed that the projection formula t∗=∣v∣2u⋅v​ is valid. What is the underlying assumption? Can you derive t∗ directly from the condition (u−t∗v)⋅v=0?
  • Lagrange’s identity shows that in R2, Cauchy–Schwarz is cos2θ+sin2θ=1. This means Cauchy–Schwarz in R2 is an identity, not just an inequality. Why does this not contradict the strict inequality that holds when the vectors are not parallel?
  • The probability form requires E[X2]<∞ and E[Y2]<∞. What goes wrong if X has infinite variance? Is the inequality still true?
