07.LU Decomposition (Product of Elimination Matrices)

07.LU Decomposition (Product of Elimination Matrices)

Apr 09, 202612 min read

Suppose you need to solve Ax=b for 1000 different right-hand sides b1​,b2​,…,b1000​ using the same matrix A. Running Gaussian elimination fresh each time costs O(n3) per solve — 1000⋅O(n3) total.

Important

LU decomposition says: factor A once at cost O(n3), then each new right-hand side costs only O(n2).

The factorization is: A=LU​ where L is unit lower triangular (1s on diagonal, multipliers below) and U is upper triangular (the result of elimination). Every new Ax=b becomes two fast triangular solves: LUx=b⟹{Ly=b​forward substitution — O(n2) Ux=y​backward substitution — O(n2)​ This is why LAPACK, NumPy, and every serious numerical library uses LU — not matrix inversion — for solving linear systems.

  • Step 1: Elimination as Matrix Multiplication

Each step of Gaussian elimination — “subtract ℓ times row j from row i” — is secretly a left-multiplication by an elimination matrix.

The Elimination Matrix Eij​

The matrix that eliminates entry (i,j) by subtracting ℓij​ times row j from row i is: Eij​=I−ℓij​,ei​ejT​​

  • I = identity matrix
  • ei​ = i-th standard basis column vector (all zeros, 1 in row i)
  • ejT​ = j-th standard basis row vector (all zeros, 1 in column j)
  • ℓij​ = the multiplier (chosen to zero out entry (i,j)) The outer product ei​ejT​ is a matrix of all zeros except a single 1 in position (i,j). So Eij​ is the identity with −ℓij​ placed at position (i,j).

Concrete 4×4 Example

The matrix eliminating entry (3,1) with multiplier ℓ31​: E31​=​10−ℓ31​0​0100​0010​0001​​ When you compute E31​A:

  • Rows 1, 2, 4 are unchanged (identity rows)
  • Row 3 becomes: (Row 3 of A) − ℓ31​× (Row 1 of A) Result: entry (3,1) becomes a31​−ℓ31​⋅a11​. If we chose ℓ31​=a31​/a11​, this equals zero. Done.
The Multiplier Formula ℓij​=ajj(j)​aij(j)​​

here, The superscript (j) means “at the stage when column j is being processed” — the entry has already been modified by all previous column eliminations. The denominator ajj(j)​ is the pivot. The numerator is the entry being eliminated.

⚠️ Common mistake: Multipliers use the current (updated) matrix entries, not the original matrix A. After eliminating column 1, the entries in column 2 have changed.

Inverse of Eij​

Just flip the sign of the off-diagonal entry: Eij−1​=I+ℓij​,ei​ejT​ For Example: E31−1​=​10+ℓ31​0​0100​0010​0001​​ Why so simple? E31​ subtracted ℓ31​ times row 1 from row 3. Its inverse adds it back. Undoing one elementary row operation takes exactly one elementary row operation. Verification: E31−1​E31​=(I+ℓ31​e3​e1T​)(I−ℓ31​e3​e1T​)=I−ℓ312​=e3​(e1T​e3​)e1T​=0 since 1=3(e3​e1T​)(e3​e1T​)​​=I ✓

  • Step 2 : The Full Sequence of Elimination Steps

For a 4×4 matrix, the complete elimination sequence is:

Column being eliminatedEntries zeroed outMatrices applied (left-multiplied in this order)
Column 1 (pivot = a11​)(2,1),;(3,1),;(4,1)E21​, then E31​, then E41​
Column 2 (pivot = a22(2)​)(3,2),;(4,2)E32​, then E42​
Column 3 (pivot = a33(3)​)(4,3)E43​
The full elimination reads (each matrix applied left-to-right, with rightmost acting first on A): E43​;E42​;E32​;E41​;E31​;E21​;A=U
To recover A, multiply both sides on the left by the inverses in reverse order: A=E21−1​;E31−1​;E41−1​;E32−1​;E42−1​;E43−1​;U
Define: L;=def;E21−1​;E31−1​;E41−1​;E32−1​;E42−1​;E43−1​ Then A=LU. The key question: what is this product of 6 matrices?

  • Step 3: The Key Miracle: Multipliers Drop In Cleanly

L=​1ℓ21​ℓ31​ℓ41​​01ℓ32​ℓ42​​001ℓ43​​0001​​ Every multiplier ℓij​ sits exactly in position (i,j) of L — as if you just placed them there by hand. There is no mixing or contamination. The product of 6 complicated matrices gives something this clean.This is “the miracle of LU” and it’s worth understanding deeply.

The Proof — Why Cross Terms Vanish

  • Lemma 1: Same-Column Elimination Matrices Compose Cleanly

Take two inverse elimination matrices from the same column j, operating on different rows i=k: Eij−1​;Ekj−1​=(I+ℓij​ei​ejT​)(I+ℓkj​ek​ejT​) Expand using FOIL: =I+ℓij​ei​ejT​+ℓkj​ek​ejT​+the cross termℓij​ℓkj​;ei​;(ejT​ek​);ejT​​​ The cross term contains ejT​ek​. This is the dot product of two standard basis vectors: it equals 1 if j=k, and 0 otherwise. Since i,k>j and i=k, we have j=k, so: ejT​ek​=0⟹cross term=0 Therefore: Eij−1​;Ekj−1​=I+ℓij​ei​ejT​+ℓkj​ek​ejT​

The two rank-1 corrections add independently — ℓij​ lands in position (i,j) and ℓkj​ lands in position (k,j) with no interaction. This extends to any number of same-column elimination matrices.

  • Lemma 2: Different-Column Elimination Matrices Also Compose Cleanly

Now take two inverse elimination matrices from different columns: Eij−1​ (column j) and Ekl−1​ (column l, with l>j): (I+ℓij​ei​ejT​)(I+ℓkl​ek​elT​)=I+ℓij​ei​ejT​+ℓkl​ek​elT​+cross termℓij​ℓkl​;ei​;(ejT​ek​);elT​​​ The cross term contains ejT​ek​. Since l>j and the matrix Ekl−1​ operates on row k>l>j, we have k=j, so: ejT​ek​=0⟹cross term=0 Again, the multipliers drop independently into their positions.

  • Putting It Together:

Applying both lemmas across all columns by induction: L=∏i>j​​Eij−1​=I+∑i>j​​ℓij​;ei​ejT​ The sum ∑i>j​ℓij​;ei​ejT​ is a matrix whose (i,j) entry (for i>j) equals ℓij​, and all other entries are zero. Adding the identity gives 1s on the diagonal. Therefore: L=​1ℓ21​ℓ31​ℓ41​​01ℓ32​ℓ42​​001ℓ43​​0001​​■

Summary of the key condition: The cross terms vanish because every pair of elimination matrices (i,j) and (k,l) with j≤l satisfies k>l≥j, so k=j, giving ejT​ek​=0.

What Goes Wrong With Row Swaps

  • The Problem

Suppose during column 2 elimination, the pivot a22(2)​=0 — we must swap rows to continue. Apply the permutation matrix P23​ that swaps rows 2 and 3.After swapping, the multiplier ℓ31​ — which was computed relative to the original row order — is now in row 2, not row 3. The subsequent column 2 elimination will modify entry (2,1), contaminating the multiplier that was supposed to sit untouched in L. The cross terms that vanished before now survive, because the permutation matrix moves entries around and breaks the condition k=j.

  • The Fix: PA=LU

Permute A first (choosing all row interchanges in advance via partial pivoting), then factor: PA=LU​ where:

  • P is the Permutation Matrix recording all row swaps
  • L is unit lower triangular with multipliers sitting cleanly in their positions
  • U is upper triangular with the pivots on the diagonal PA=LU exists for any invertible A. In practice, LAPACK and NumPy store P implicitly as a permutation vector (pivot indices).

  • Existence Without Row Swaps

A=LU (without any permutation) exists if and only if all leading principal submatrices are invertible — equivalently, all pivots are nonzero during elimination. Leading principal submatrices are the upper-left k×k corners: A1:k,1:k​ for k=1,…,n.

The Order of Multiplication — A Common Confusion

The elimination operations are applied as: 6thE43​​​5thE42​​​4thE32​​​3rdE41​​​2ndE31​​​1stE21​​​A=U So E21​ acts first (rightmost position), then E31​, and so on. To recover A, invert both sides and rearrange: A=E21−1​E31−1​E41−1​E32−1​E42−1​E43−1​⋅U=LU Note: the product E21−1​E31−1​⋯ is not the same as reversing the order (E43​⋯E21​)−1. Inverting a product reverses the order: (E43​⋯E21​)−1=E21−1​⋯E43−1​ This is the same order as what we wrote for L! The reversal happens automatically. And thanks to the vanishing cross terms, L is clean regardless of the direction.

Using LU to Solve Ax=b

Once A=LU is computed (cost O(n3), computed once):

  • Forward Substitution: Solve Ly=b

L is lower triangular, so solve from top to bottom: y1​=b1​ yi​=bi​−∑j=1i−1​ℓij​,yj​for i=2,…,n

  • Backward Substitution: Solve Ux=y

U is upper triangular, so solve from bottom to top: xn​=yn​/unn​ xi​=(yi​−∑j=i+1n​uij​,xj​)/uii​for i=n−1,…,1 Cost: Each solve costs O(n2). For k right-hand sides: total cost =O(n3)+k⋅O(n2).
Compare to: Computing A−1 explicitly costs O(n3) and then each solve is A−1b at O(n2) — same asymptotic cost but LU has smaller constants and better numerical stability.

Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

  • Mistake 1: Using original matrix entries for multipliers.

    • After column 1 elimination, the matrix entries change. Multiplier ℓ32​ uses a32(2)​ (the entry after column 1 is eliminated), not the original a32​.

  • Mistake 2: Thinking L might be contaminated.

    • The vanishing cross terms guarantee that L stores the multipliers exactly as computed, with no modification during subsequent steps.

  • Mistake 3: Confusing E21−1​E31−1​⋯ with (E21​E31​⋯)−1.

    • These are different. The former is a product of individual inverses in the same order; the latter reverses the order. They both happen to give L only because of the structure above.

  • It fails if any pivot is zero and no row swap is available. Use PA=LU for robustness.

    • Mistake 4: Expecting A=LU to always exist.

  • Mistake 5: Computing A−1 explicitly to solve Ax=b.

    • Never do this numerically. Use LU. Explicit inversion loses numerical precision and is slower.
