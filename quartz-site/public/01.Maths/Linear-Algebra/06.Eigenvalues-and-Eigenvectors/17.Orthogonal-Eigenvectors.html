17.Orthogonal Eigenvectors

17.Orthogonal Eigenvectors

Apr 07, 20267 min read

The Central Question

Why do symmetric matrices have orthogonal eigenvectors? Not just “they do” — I want to feel why the symmetry condition forces eigenvectors from different eigenspaces to land perpendicular to each other. This is the question that actually matters here.

Setup: What We Already Know

Recall that if Ax=λx, then x is an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ. Nothing about orthogonality yet — we are just finding directions that A stretches or flips without rotating.

Two eigenvectors x1​,x2​ corresponding to different eigenvalues λ1​=λ2​ need not be orthogonal in general. For a random matrix, there is no reason to expect x1​⋅x2​=0.

Symmetry changes everything.

The Key Theorem

Theorem (Spectral Theorem, real case). Let A be a real symmetric matrix (A=AT). Then:

  1. All eigenvalues of A are real.
  2. Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  3. There exists an orthonormal basis of Rn consisting entirely of eigenvectors of A.

Point 3 is the full spectral theorem — it requires handling repeated eigenvalues too. I will prove points 1 and 2 here, and note what extra work point 3 needs.

Proof that Eigenvalues are Real

This always trips me up when I first see it, because it requires going to Cn even though A is a real matrix.

Suppose Ax=λx with x∈Cn, x=0. Take the conjugate transpose of both sides:

xˉTAT=λˉxˉT

Since A=AT (and A is real, so Aˉ=A):

xˉTA=λˉxˉT

Now multiply both sides on the right by x:

xˉTAx=λˉxˉTx

But Ax=λx, so the left side is also xˉT(λx)=λxˉTx.

Therefore:

λxˉTx=λˉxˉTx

Now xˉTx=∣x∣2>0 (since x=0), so we can divide both sides by it:

λ=λˉ

This means λ is real. Done.

The same argument works for complex Hermitian matrices (A=AˉT), which is the general version of this. Symmetric real matrices are just Hermitian matrices whose entries happen to be real.

Proof that Eigenvectors for Distinct Eigenvalues are Orthogonal

This is the one I want to really nail. Let Ax1​=λ1​x1​ and Ax2​=λ2​x2​ with λ1​=λ2​.

Step 1. Compute x2T​Ax1​ in two different ways using symmetry.

Using the first eigenvector equation:

x2T​Ax1​=x2T​(λ1​x1​)=λ1​(x2T​x1​)

Using the transpose and symmetry:

x2T​Ax1​=x2T​ATx1​=(Ax2​)Tx1​=(λ2​x2​)Tx1​=λ2​(x2T​x1​)

Step 2. Set the two expressions equal:

λ1​(x2T​x1​)=λ2​(x2T​x1​)

(λ1​−λ2​)(x2T​x1​)=0

Step 3. Since λ1​=λ2​, we have λ1​−λ2​=0, so:

x2T​x1​=0

The eigenvectors are orthogonal. □

The elegance here is in step 1: symmetry lets us “move A to the other side” — x2T​A=(ATx2​)T=(Ax2​)T — which extracts the second eigenvalue from what started as an expression involving the first. Symmetry is exactly the condition that lets you do this.

What About Repeated Eigenvalues?

The proof above only works when λ1​=λ2​. If a symmetric matrix has a repeated eigenvalue, the corresponding eigenspace might be multi-dimensional — but one can still choose an orthonormal basis within that eigenspace (by Gram-Schmidt). The full spectral theorem guarantees the eigenspace has the right dimension to fill out Rn, but that requires a separate argument (usually the inductive proof via the fact that A restricted to the orthogonal complement of an eigenvector is again symmetric).

For now, the case of distinct eigenvalues is the heart of it. The repeated case is a technicality.

A Concrete Example

Take a 3×3 symmetric matrix and watch the orthogonality fall out explicitly.

A=[2​1​0 1​2​0 0​0​3​]

The characteristic polynomial:

det(A−λI)=det[2−λ​1​0 1​2−λ​0 0​0​3−λ​]

Expanding along the third column:

=(3−λ)det[2−λ​1 1​2−λ​]=(3−λ)[(2−λ)2−1]

=(3−λ)(4−4λ+λ2−1)=(3−λ)(λ2−4λ+3)=(3−λ)(λ−1)(λ−3)

So the eigenvalues are λ1​=1, λ2​=3 (repeated).

Eigenvector for λ=1:

A−I=[1​1​0 1​1​0 0​0​2​]

Row reduce: x1​+x2​=0, x3​=0. So x1​=[1 −1 0​].

Eigenvectors for λ=3:

A−3I=[−1​1​0 1​−1​0 0​0​0​]

Row reduce: −x1​+x2​=0 (i.e. x1​=x2​), x3​ free. Two free variables: x2​=[1 1 0​], x3​=[0 0 1​].

Checking orthogonality:

x1​⋅x2​=(1)(1)+(−1)(1)+(0)(0)=0✓ x1​⋅x3​=(1)(0)+(−1)(0)+(0)(1)=0✓ x2​⋅x3​=(1)(0)+(1)(0)+(0)(1)=0✓

Three mutually orthogonal eigenvectors, as the theorem demands. The repeated eigenvalue λ=3 happened to give an eigenspace with an orthogonal basis directly — in general I would need Gram-Schmidt inside the eigenspace.

The Diagonalization Picture: A=QΛQT

Once I have an orthonormal set of eigenvectors q1​,q2​,…,qn​, I can form the orthogonal matrix:

Q=[∣​∣​​∣ q1​​q2​​⋯​qn​ ∣​∣​​∣​]

Since the columns are orthonormal: QTQ=I, so Q−1=QT.

The diagonalization A=QΛQT where:

Λ=[λ1​​0​⋯​0 0​λ2​​⋯​0 ⋮​​⋱​⋮ 0​0​⋯​λn​​]

This is more than just diagonalization — it is a decomposition of A into a sum of rank-one projections:

A=λ1​q1​q1T​+λ2​q2​q2T​+⋯+λn​qn​qnT​

Each term qi​qiT​ is the projection matrix onto the i-th eigenspace. This is the spectral decomposition, and it is the payoff for all of this orthogonality work. It says A acts on any vector by projecting it onto each eigenspace and scaling by the corresponding eigenvalue.

Why This Matters: Quadratic Forms

A symmetric matrix A defines a quadratic form Q(x)=xTAx. The spectral decomposition tells us that in the eigenvector basis, this simplifies to:

Q(x)=λ1​y12​+λ2​y22​+⋯+λn​yn2​

where y=QTx are the coordinates in the eigenvector basis. The cross terms vanish precisely because the eigenvectors are orthogonal. This is the principal axis theorem. Every quadratic form is a pure sum of squares in the right coordinate system — and the right coordinate system is exactly the eigenvectors of A.

Positive definiteness is then just the condition that all λi​>0.

Summary in One Line

Symmetry (A=AT) forces eigenvectors from different eigenspaces to be orthogonal because it lets you compute x2T​Ax1​ in two ways — once extracting λ1​, once extracting λ2​ — and if λ1​=λ2​, the inner product must be zero.
