20.All Symmetric Matrices Are Diagonalizable

20.All Symmetric Matrices Are Diagonalizable

Apr 07, 202610 min read

The Statement

Every real symmetric matrix A=AT is orthogonally diagonalizable. That is, there exists an orthogonal matrix Q (meaning QTQ=I, equivalently Q−1=QT) such that:

A=QΛQT

where Λ is diagonal with the real eigenvalues of A on the diagonal.

This is the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices. It is stronger than ordinary diagonalizability (A=PΛP−1 for some invertible P) in two ways:

  1. The diagonalizing matrix Q is orthogonal, not just invertible.
  2. The eigenvalues are guaranteed to be real.

The theorem is not obvious. For a general matrix, diagonalizability can fail — defective matrices exist. The content of the spectral theorem is that symmetry is exactly the condition that prevents this failure.

What Could Go Wrong: Defective Matrices

A matrix fails to be diagonalizable when it does not have enough linearly independent eigenvectors to form a basis of Rn. This happens when an eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity greater than its geometric multiplicity — that is, the eigenspace is smaller than the multiplicity of the eigenvalue as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

The standard example of a defective matrix:

J=[0​1 0​0​]

Characteristic polynomial: λ2=0, so λ=0 with algebraic multiplicity 2. But (J−0⋅I)x=Jx=0 gives only x=[1\0​], so geometric multiplicity 1. Not enough eigenvectors. Not diagonalizable.

Notice that J is not symmetric. The spectral theorem will say this situation cannot happen for symmetric matrices.

The Proof

I want to prove that every real symmetric n×n matrix has n orthonormal eigenvectors. The proof goes by induction on n.

Base case: n=1. A 1×1 matrix A=[a] is trivially symmetric, has eigenvalue λ=a (real), and eigenvector q1​=[1]. Done.

Inductive step. Suppose the theorem holds for all real symmetric matrices of size (n−1)×(n−1). Let A be a real symmetric n×n matrix.

Step 1: Produce one real eigenvalue.

The characteristic polynomial p(λ)=det(A−λI) is a degree-n polynomial with real coefficients. Over C, by the fundamental theorem of algebra, it has at least one root λ1​∈C.

But from the previous note, all eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real. So λ1​∈R.

Let q1​∈Rn be a corresponding unit eigenvector: Aq1​=λ1​q1​, ∣q1​∣=1.

Step 2: Restrict A to the orthogonal complement.

Let V=q1⊥​=v∈Rn:q1T​v=0. This is an (n−1)-dimensional subspace.

I claim V is invariant under A: if v∈V, then Av∈V.

Proof of invariance. Take any v with q1T​v=0. Then:

q1T​(Av)=(ATq1​)Tv=(Aq1​)Tv=(λ1​q1​)Tv=λ1​(q1T​v)=0

(used AT=A in the second step.) So Av∈q1⊥​=V. □

This is the key step. Symmetry is doing exactly one piece of work here: it lets me move A from one side of the inner product to the other, turning Aq1​ into λ1​q1​. Without symmetry, V need not be A-invariant, and the induction breaks.

Step 3: Apply the inductive hypothesis.

Let AV​:V→V be the restriction of A to V. If I pick any orthonormal basis e2​,…,en​ for V, the matrix of AV​ in that basis is an (n−1)×(n−1) symmetric matrix (since A is symmetric and V is A-invariant).

By the inductive hypothesis, AV​ has n−1 orthonormal eigenvectors q2​,…,qn​ in V.

Step 4: Assemble.

The vectors q1​,q2​,…,qn​ are orthonormal eigenvectors of A:

  • q1​ is orthogonal to all of q2​,…,qn​ because the latter live in V=q1⊥​.
  • q2​,…,qn​ are mutually orthonormal by the inductive hypothesis.

Form Q=[∣​∣​​∣ q1​​q2​​⋯​qn​ ∣​∣​​∣​]. Then QTQ=I and AQ=QΛ, so A=QΛQT. □

Why the Proof Fails for Non-Symmetric Matrices

Let me pinpoint exactly where symmetry was used, because this is the thing worth remembering.

In Step 2, invariance of V=q1⊥​ required:

q1T​(Av)=(ATq1​)Tv

If A=AT, then ATq1​=λ1​q1​ in general — knowing that q1​ is an eigenvector of A says nothing about ATq1​. So we cannot conclude q1T​(Av)=0, and V need not be invariant.

For a non-symmetric matrix, A might map a vector in q1⊥​ outside of q1⊥​. The induction cannot proceed because there is no well-defined (n−1)×(n−1) restriction to work with.

The Repeated Eigenvalue Case: Why Defectiveness Cannot Happen

The inductive proof above handles repeated eigenvalues automatically — the induction does not distinguish cases. But let me also see directly why a symmetric matrix cannot be defective.

Suppose A is symmetric and λ is an eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity k. I claim the geometric multiplicity is also k.

The geometric multiplicity is dim(ker(A−λI)). The algebraic multiplicity is the multiplicity of λ as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

Geometric ≤ algebraic always. I need geometric ≥ algebraic.

Here is the argument. Let Eλ​=ker(A−λI) be the eigenspace. A restricted to Eλ​ is λI. A restricted to Eλ⊥​ is also symmetric (by the same invariance argument as Step 2 of the proof), and λ is not an eigenvalue of this restriction (because if it were, there would be an eigenvector in Eλ⊥​ for λ, which would be orthogonal to Eλ​ — but then it would belong to Eλ​ by definition, contradiction).

So the characteristic polynomial of A factors as (λ0​−λ)dimEλ0​​⋅(polynomial with no root at λ0​) for each eigenvalue λ0​.

The algebraic multiplicity of λ0​ is therefore exactly dimEλ0​​ = geometric multiplicity.

Geometric = algebraic. No defect. Every eigenvalue has a full eigenspace, and Gram-Schmidt gives an orthonormal basis within each eigenspace. □

A Worked Example: Repeated Eigenvalue

I want to see this concretely. Take:

A=[3​0​0 0​3​0 0​0​1​]

This is already diagonal, so trivially symmetric. Eigenvalues: λ=3 (multiplicity 2), λ=1 (multiplicity 1).

Eigenspace for λ=3: ker(A−3I)=ker[0​0​0\0​0​0\0​0​−2​], so x3​=0, x1​,x2​ free. This is the x1​x2​-plane — a 2-dimensional eigenspace. Algebraic multiplicity 2 = geometric multiplicity 2.

Any orthonormal basis of this plane works. The standard choice: q1​=[1\0\0​], q2​=[0\1\0​].

Eigenspace for λ=1: q3​=[0\0\1​].

Now try a less obvious repeated-eigenvalue example:

A=[2​1​1 1​2​1 1​1​2​]

Characteristic polynomial:

det(A−λI)=det[2−λ​1​1 1​2−λ​1 1​1​2−λ​]

Notice all row sums equal 4−λ, so v=[1\1\1​] is an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ=4.

For the remaining eigenvalues, note tr(A)=6=λ1​+λ2​+λ3​=4+λ2​+λ3​, so λ2​+λ3​=2.

Also det(A)=2(4−1)−1(2−1)+1(1−2)=6−1−1=4=4λ2​λ3​, so λ2​λ3​=1.

Then λ2​,λ3​ satisfy t2−2t+1=(t−1)2=0, so λ2​=λ3​=1.

Eigenvalues: λ=4 (simple), λ=1 (multiplicity 2).

Eigenvector for λ=4: q1​=3​1​[1\1\1​].

Eigenspace for λ=1:

A−I=[1​1​1 1​1​1 1​1​1​]

Rank 1, so nullity 2. The eigenspace is x1​+x2​+x3​=0, which is the plane perpendicular to [1\1\1​]. (Of course — eigenvectors for different eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal, as proved in the previous note.)

Two independent vectors in this plane: [1\-1\0​] and [1\0\-1​]. These are not orthogonal to each other, so apply Gram-Schmidt:

u1​=[1\-1\0​],u2​=[1\0\-1​]−∣[1\-1\0​]∣2[1\0\-1​]⋅[1\-1\0​]​[1\-1\0​]

=[1\0\-1​]−21​[1\-1\0​]=[1/2\1/2\-1​]

Normalizing:

q2​=2​1​[1\-1\0​],q3​=6​1​[1\1\-2​]

The three vectors q1​,q2​,q3​ form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. The choice of q2​,q3​ within the eigenspace is not unique — any orthonormal basis of x1​+x2​+x3​=0 works. The spectral decomposition A=QΛQT exists, even though the eigenvalue λ=1 is repeated.

The Orthogonal Diagonalization Written Out

For the matrix above:

Q=[3​1​​2​1​​6​1​\[6pt]3​1​​−2​1​​6​1​\[6pt]3​1​​0​−6​2​​],Λ=[4​0​0 0​1​0 0​0​1​]

And A=QΛQT. This is exact — not approximate. The non-uniqueness is in Q (any valid orthonormal basis within each eigenspace works), but Λ is determined up to ordering of the diagonal entries.

Contrast: Why General Diagonalizability Is Weaker

A general (non-symmetric) matrix A=PΛP−1:

  • P need not be orthogonal; P−1 is not PT in general.
  • Eigenvalues can be complex.
  • Diagonalizability can fail entirely (defective matrices).

Symmetric A=QΛQT:

  • Q is orthogonal; Q−1=QT. The basis transformation is a rigid rotation, not some arbitrary change of coordinates.
  • Eigenvalues are all real.
  • Diagonalizability never fails.

The orthogonality of Q is not a bonus — it is the geometric content. It says that the eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix form a natural, undistorted coordinate system for Rn. In that coordinate system, A acts by pure scaling.

Things to Come Back To

  • The complex version: every Hermitian matrix (A=AˉT) is unitarily diagonalizable. The proof is identical with Cn and unitary matrices in place of Rn and orthogonal matrices.
  • Normal matrices: ATA=AAT is the most general condition guaranteeing orthogonal/unitary diagonalizability. Symmetric, antisymmetric, and orthogonal matrices are all special cases.
  • The spectral theorem and functional calculus: once A=QΛQT, one can define f(A)=Qf(Λ)QT for any function f. This gives meaning to A​, eA, logA, etc. for symmetric positive definite matrices.

Summary

Every real symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable. The proof is inductive: find one real eigenvector q1​, observe that q1⊥​ is A-invariant (this is the one step that uses symmetry), restrict A to that (n−1)-dimensional subspace, and apply the inductive hypothesis. Repeated eigenvalues are handled because the eigenspace always has the right dimension — Gram-Schmidt provides an orthonormal basis within it. The result is A=QΛQT where Q is orthogonal and Λ is real diagonal. The non-symmetric case fails at A-invariance of q1⊥​, which is why defective matrices exist but symmetric defective matrices do not.
