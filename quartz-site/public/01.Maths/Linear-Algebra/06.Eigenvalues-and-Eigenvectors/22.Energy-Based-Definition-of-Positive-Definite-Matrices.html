22.Energy Based Definition of Positive Definite Matrices

22.Energy-Based Definition of Positive Definite Matrices

Apr 07, 202610 min read

Why a Separate Note

The previous note listed five equivalent tests for positive definiteness. The energy definition is test 1 — the quadratic form. But Strang pushes this further than just a test. He treats xTAx as the primary definition and everything else as a consequence. I want to follow that thread carefully because it connects linear algebra to mechanics, calculus, and geometry in a way that the eigenvalue definition does not.

The Energy of a Vector

Given a symmetric matrix A and a vector x, define:

E(x)=xTAx

This is called the energy of x with respect to A. The name comes from mechanics: if A is a stiffness matrix and x is a displacement vector, then 21​xTAx is the potential energy stored in the system. Positive definiteness says the energy is always positive — the system always stores energy when displaced from equilibrium, which is what stability means physically.

Written out for a 2×2 matrix A=[a​b b​d​]:

E(x1​,x2​)=xTAx=ax12​+2bx1​x2​+dx22​

This is a general quadratic form in two variables — a degree-2 homogeneous polynomial. The question positive definiteness asks: is this always >0 except at the origin?

Completing the Square: Energy as Geometry

The most direct way to see positive definiteness from the quadratic form is to complete the square. This is also how Gaussian elimination actually works on the quadratic form.

E(x1​,x2​)=ax12​+2bx1​x2​+dx22​

Group the x1​ terms:

=a(x12​+a2b​x1​x2​)+dx22​

=a(x1​+ab​x2​)2−ab2​x22​+dx22​

=a(x1​+ab​x2​)2+(d−ab2​)x22​

The two coefficients are exactly the two pivots:

d1​=a,d2​=d−ab2​

So: E(x)=d1​(x1​+ab​x2​)2+d2​x22​

This is a sum of two squares, weighted by the pivots. It is positive for all nonzero x if and only if d1​>0 and d2​>0. The pivots appear as the weights on the squared terms.

This is not a coincidence. Completing the square on the quadratic form is the same operation as Gaussian elimination on the matrix. The pivot dk​ that appears during elimination is exactly the coefficient of the k-th squared term when you complete the square step by step.

The 3×3 Case Written Out

Let me do this once at full size so the pattern is clear. Take:

A=[a11​​a12​​a13​ a12​​a22​​a23​ a13​​a23​​a33​​]

The quadratic form is:

E=a11​x12​+a22​x22​+a33​x32​+2a12​x1​x2​+2a13​x1​x3​+2a23​x2​x3​

After completing the square on x1​:

E=a11​(x1​+a11​a12​​x2​+a11​a13​​x3​)2+E′(x2​,x3​)

where E′ is a quadratic form in x2​,x3​ alone — the Schur complement of a11​ in A:

E′(x2​,x3​)=(a22​−a11​a122​​)x22​+2(a23​−a11​a12​a13​​)x2​x3​+(a33​−a11​a132​​)x32​

The coefficient of x22​ in E′ is the second pivot d2​. Completing the square again on x2​ in E′ gives the third pivot d3​. The full decomposition is:

E(x)=d1​(x1​+ℓ21​x2​+ℓ31​x3​)2+d2​(x2​+ℓ32​x3​)2+d3​x32​

where ℓij​ are the multipliers from Gaussian elimination — exactly the entries of L in A=LDLT.

Positive definiteness ⟺ d1​,d2​,d3​>0 ⟺ the energy is a sum of positive-weighted squares ⟺ E(x)>0 for all x=0.

A Specific 3×3 Example

A=[2​−1​0 −1​2​−1 0​−1​2​]

This is the tridiagonal matrix that appears in finite difference approximations of −d2u/dx2 — a discrete Laplacian. The energy has an interpretation: it measures something like the “curvature” of a discrete function.

Quadratic form:

E=2x12​+2x22​+2x32​−2x1​x2​−2x2​x3​

Step 1 — complete the square on x1​:

E=2(x1​−21​x2​)2+(2−21​)x22​−2x2​x3​+2x32​

=2(x1​−21​x2​)2+23​x22​−2x2​x3​+2x32​

First pivot: d1​=2.

Step 2 — complete the square on x2​:

23​x22​−2x2​x3​+2x32​=23​(x2​−32​x3​)2+(2−32​)x32​

=23​(x2​−32​x3​)2+34​x32​

Second pivot: d2​=23​.

Full decomposition:

E=2(x1​−21​x2​)2+23​(x2​−32​x3​)2+34​x32​

Third pivot: d3​=34​.

All three pivots positive: 2,23​,34​. The energy is a sum of three positive-weighted squares. Positive definite. ✓

The energy is zero only when all three squared terms are zero simultaneously: x3​=0, then x2​=0, then x1​=0. Only the zero vector has zero energy.

The Physical Picture

Think of x as a displacement from equilibrium and E(x)=21​xTAx as potential energy.

Positive definite A: the energy bowl opens upward in every direction. The equilibrium x=0 is a stable minimum. Displace the system and it wants to return. Think of a ball sitting at the bottom of a bowl.

Positive semidefinite A: the energy is flat in some direction — the null space of A. The system has a “soft mode” — a direction you can push it without costing any energy. The equilibrium is stable in some directions, neutral in others.

Indefinite A: there exists a direction x where the energy is negative — the quadratic form dips below zero. The equilibrium is a saddle point. The system is unstable: displaced in the wrong direction, it runs away from equilibrium. Think of a ball balanced at the top of a hill.

Negative definite A: the energy bowl opens downward in every direction. The equilibrium is a local maximum — unstable in every direction.

In structural engineering: the stiffness matrix K must be positive definite for the structure to be stable. A negative eigenvalue of K means a buckling mode — a deformation that releases energy rather than storing it. The structure will collapse spontaneously along that eigenvector direction.

Connection to Second Derivatives: Hessians

In multivariable calculus, the second-order behavior of a smooth function f:Rn→R near a critical point x0​ (where ∇f=0) is:

f(x0​+h)≈f(x0​)+21​hTHh+O(∣h∣3)

where H is the Hessian matrix with entries Hij​=∂xi​∂xj​∂2f​.

The Hessian is symmetric (by Clairaut’s theorem, assuming continuous second partials). So the energy-based definition of positive definiteness applies directly:

21​hTHh>0 for all h=0⟺H is positive definite⟺x0​ is a strict local minimum

The classification of critical points is entirely encoded in the definiteness of the Hessian:

H at x0​Type of critical point
Positive definiteStrict local minimum
Negative definiteStrict local maximum
IndefiniteSaddle point
Positive semidefiniteInconclusive (minimum or saddle)

The 2×2 case recovers the standard second derivative test: H=[fxx​​fxy​ fxy​​fyy​​] is positive definite iff fxx​>0 and fxx​fyy​−fxy2​>0 — exactly the familiar conditions.

The Discrete Laplacian: Energy as Finite Differences

The tridiagonal matrix from the worked example deserves more attention. For a discrete function u=(u1​,u2​,…,un​) with boundary values u0​=un+1​=0, define:

A=[2​−1​​ −1​2​−1​ ​⋱​⋱​⋱ ​​−1​2​]

Then the quadratic form is:

uTAu=∑i=1n+1​(ui​−ui−1​)2

This is the sum of squared differences of adjacent values — a discrete measure of how much u varies. It is zero only when all ui​ are equal, but with u0​=0 and un+1​=0, this forces all ui​=0. So uTAu>0 for u=0, confirming positive definiteness directly from the energy interpretation.

The continuous analogue: ∫01​(u′)2,dx>0 for any nonzero u with u(0)=u(1)=0. The matrix A is the discrete version of the operator −d2/dx2, and both are positive definite on their respective spaces.

Gram Matrices as Energy

The factorization A=RTR (from the previous note) now reads:

E(x)=xTRTRx=∣Rx∣2

The energy is a squared norm. This is the cleanest version of the energy definition: A is positive definite iff there exists a change of variables y=Rx such that the energy is just the squared Euclidean length ∣y∣2.

In that coordinate system, all level sets E=c are spheres. In the original coordinate system, they are ellipsoids (because the change of variables y=Rx stretches space). The Cholesky factor R is the map that “undoes” the ellipsoidal distortion and turns it back into a sphere.

Why the Energy Definition is the Right Starting Point

The eigenvalue definition (λi​>0) requires you to already have the eigenvalues — which for a large matrix means running an O(n3) algorithm. The pivot definition (di​>0) is computationally fast but gives no geometric insight. The subdeterminant definition requires computing n determinants.

The energy definition xTAx>0 is:

  • Direct: you can evaluate it for any specific x without decomposing A.
  • Geometric: it tells you the shape of level sets and the stability of equilibria.
  • Physical: it maps immediately onto potential energy, strain energy, and variance in statistics.
  • Universal: it generalizes to infinite-dimensional operators (the continuous Laplacian, covariance operators in Hilbert space) in a way that eigenvalue and pivot definitions do not.

Strang’s point is that when you encounter a symmetric matrix in applications, it almost always arrives with an energy interpretation already attached. Recognizing it as positive definite from the energy is usually more natural than computing eigenvalues.

Things to Come Back To

  • Variational problems and the Rayleigh quotient R(x)=xTAx/xTx: the minimum of R over all x=0 is λmin​(A). This is the bridge from energy minimization to eigenvalues.
  • Covariance matrices in statistics: Σ=n1​XTX for centered data matrix X. Always positive semidefinite, positive definite when the data has full rank.
  • Finite element methods: the global stiffness matrix is assembled from element stiffness matrices, each of which is positive semidefinite. The global matrix is positive definite iff the boundary conditions fix rigid body modes.
  • The connection between xTAx and the norm ∣x∣A​=xTAx​ — the energy norm. Conjugate gradient method minimizes the energy norm of the error at each step.

Summary

The energy definition E(x)=xTAx>0 for all x=0 is the primary definition of positive definiteness. Completing the square on E is the same operation as Gaussian elimination on A, and the weights on the squared terms are precisely the pivots — this is why all five tests are equivalent. Physically, positive definiteness means the system stores energy for every nonzero displacement, which is the condition for a stable equilibrium. The Hessian connection means positive definiteness of the second-order term classifies critical points in multivariable calculus. And the factorization E(x)=∣Rx∣2 shows that every positive definite energy is secretly just a squared Euclidean length in disguise, under a suitable linear change of variables.
