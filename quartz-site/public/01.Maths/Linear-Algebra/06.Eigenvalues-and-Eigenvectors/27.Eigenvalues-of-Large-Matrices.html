27.Eigenvalues of Large Matrices

27.Eigenvalues of Large Matrices

Apr 07, 202612 min read

For a 2×2 or 3×3 matrix, computing eigenvalues by hand via det(A−λI)=0 is painful but feasible. For a 100×100 matrix it is completely out of the question. The characteristic polynomial has degree 100, and there is no general algebraic formula for roots of polynomials of degree 5 or higher (Abel-Ruffini). Even numerically, forming the characteristic polynomial and then finding its roots is a terrible algorithm — the polynomial’s coefficients are catastrophically sensitive to perturbations.

This note is about what you do instead: how to locate, estimate, and compute eigenvalues of large matrices without touching the characteristic polynomial.

What We Can and Cannot Do

Cannot do (for large n):

  • Compute det(A−λI) symbolically
  • Find all eigenvalues exactly in finitely many arithmetic steps (Abel-Ruffini)
  • Diagonalize explicitly by solving n coupled equations

Can do:

  • Locate eigenvalues in regions of the complex plane (Gershgorin)
  • Bound the spectral radius
  • Compute the largest eigenvalue iteratively (Power Method)
  • Compute all eigenvalues numerically to machine precision (QR Algorithm)
  • Exploit structure (symmetry, sparsity, tridiagonal form) heavily

The shift from “solve” to “iterate” is the fundamental move in large-scale linear algebra.

Gershgorin Circle Theorem

This is the first and most elegant tool: a completely non-iterative way to locate all eigenvalues using only the matrix entries.

Theorem (Gershgorin, 1931). Let A be an n×n complex matrix. For each i=1,…,n, define the Gershgorin disc

D_i = \left{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z - a_{ii}| \leq R_i \right}, \qquad R_i = \sum_{j \neq i} |a_{ij}|

That is: center at the diagonal entry aii​, radius equal to the sum of absolute values of the off-diagonal entries in row i.

Then every eigenvalue of A lies in the union ⋃i=1n​Di​.

Proof. Let λ be an eigenvalue with eigenvector x=0. Let k be the index where ∣xk​∣=∣x∣∞​ (the component of largest absolute value). From Ax=λx, the k-th row gives

∑j=1n​akj​xj​=λxk​

(λ−akk​)xk​=∑j=k​akj​xj​

Taking absolute values:

∣λ−akk​∣∣xk​∣≤∑j=k​∣akj​∣∣xj​∣≤∣xk​∣∑j=k​∣akj​∣=Rk​∣xk​∣

Since ∣xk​∣>0, divide through:

∣λ−akk​∣≤Rk​

So λ∈Dk​⊆⋃i​Di​. □

Example. Take

A=[10​1​0 2​8​1 0​1​5​]

Gershgorin discs:

  • Row 1: center 10, radius ∣1∣+∣0∣=1. Disc: ∣z−10∣≤1, so λ∈[9,11].
  • Row 2: center 8, radius ∣2∣+∣1∣=3. Disc: ∣z−8∣≤3, so λ∈[5,11].
  • Row 3: center 5, radius ∣1∣+∣0∣=1. Disc: ∣z−5∣≤1, so λ∈[4,6].

All eigenvalues lie in [4,6]∪[5,11]. Since the matrix is symmetric with positive diagonal, they are all real and positive — so the matrix is positive definite, confirmed without computing a single eigenvalue.

Refinement. If a connected component of ⋃i​Di​ consists of exactly k discs, then it contains exactly k eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity). This follows from a continuity argument — perturb A to a diagonal matrix and track where eigenvalues go.

Diagonal Dominance and the Gershgorin Corollary

A matrix is strictly diagonally dominant if ∣aii​∣>Ri​ for every i. By Gershgorin, all eigenvalues lie in discs that do not contain 0, so 0 is not an eigenvalue, so A is invertible.

Moreover, if A is strictly diagonally dominant with positive diagonal entries, then every Gershgorin disc lies in the right half-plane, so all eigenvalues have positive real part. If A is also symmetric, all eigenvalues are real and positive: A is positive definite.

This is a completely elementary sufficient condition for positive definiteness that requires no computation.

The Power Method

The Power Method computes the dominant eigenvalue — the eigenvalue of largest absolute value, assuming it is unique.

Setup. Assume A has eigenvalues ∣λ1​∣>∣λ2​∣≥⋯≥∣λn​∣. We want λ1​.

Algorithm. Start with an arbitrary vector b0​ (not orthogonal to the eigenvector v1​ — almost any random vector works). Iterate:

bk+1​=∣Abk​∣Abk​​

At each step, multiply by A and renormalize. The Rayleigh quotient

μk​=bkT​bk​bkT​Abk​​

converges to λ1​.

Why it works. Expand b0​ in the eigenvector basis:

b0​=c1​v1​+c2​v2​+⋯+cn​vn​

Then (ignoring renormalization for clarity):

Akb0​=c1​λ1k​v1​+c2​λ2k​v2​+⋯+cn​λnk​vn​=λ1k​(c1​v1​+c2​(λ1​λ2​​)kv2​+⋯)

Since ∣λ2​/λ1​∣<1, all terms except the first decay geometrically. The direction of Akb0​ converges to v1​ at rate ∣λ2​/λ1​∣k.

Rate of convergence. The error after k steps is O(​λ1​λ2​​​k). If λ2​ is close to λ1​, convergence is slow. This is the key weakness.

Shift to accelerate. If you know λ1​≈μ, apply the power method to (A−μI)−1 instead. Its dominant eigenvalue is (λj​−μ)−1 where λj​ is the eigenvalue closest to μ. By shifting close to a target eigenvalue, you can make the ratio arbitrarily favorable. This is inverse iteration.

Inverse Iteration and the Rayleigh Quotient Iteration

Inverse iteration. Fix a shift σ near a target eigenvalue λj​. Solve

(A−σI)bk+1​=bk​,then normalize

at each step. This converges to the eigenvector for the eigenvalue closest to σ, at rate ​λℓ​−σλj​−σ​​k where λℓ​ is the second-closest eigenvalue to σ.

Rayleigh quotient iteration. Update the shift at each step using the current Rayleigh quotient:

σk​=bkT​bk​bkT​Abk​​

then solve (A−σk​I)bk+1​=bk​. For symmetric matrices, this converges cubically once close to an eigenvector. Practically instantaneous near convergence.

The cost per step is solving a linear system with the shifted matrix — for dense matrices this is O(n3) once (via LU factorization) then O(n2) per step.

Reduction to Hessenberg Form

Before running any iterative algorithm on a large dense matrix, reduce it to upper Hessenberg form (for general matrices) or tridiagonal form (for symmetric matrices). A Hessenberg matrix has zeros below the first subdiagonal:

H=[∗​∗​∗​∗​∗ ∗​∗​∗​∗​∗ 0​∗​∗​∗​∗ 0​0​∗​∗​∗ 0​0​0​∗​∗​]

For symmetric A, the Hessenberg reduction produces a symmetric tridiagonal matrix:

T=[α1​​β1​​0​⋯​0 β1​​α2​​β2​​⋯​0 0​β2​​α3​​⋯​0 ⋮​​⋱​⋱​⋮ 0​0​⋯​βn−1​​αn​​]

This reduction is exact (not iterative), accomplished via n−2 Householder reflections in O(n3) time. The resulting H (or T) is similar to A, so has the same eigenvalues. Every subsequent step of the QR algorithm costs only O(n2) for Hessenberg matrices (versus O(n3) for full matrices), making the reduction a one-time investment that pays off over many iterations.

The QR Algorithm

This is the algorithm actually used to compute all eigenvalues of a dense matrix to machine precision. It is one of the ten greatest algorithms of the 20th century.

Basic QR iteration. Start with A0​=A.

At step k:

  1. Factor Ak​=Qk​Rk​ (QR decomposition: Qk​ orthogonal, Rk​ upper triangular)
  2. Set Ak+1​=Rk​Qk​

Note: Ak+1​=Rk​Qk​=QkT​(Qk​Rk​)Qk​=QkT​Ak​Qk​, so each Ak+1​ is orthogonally similar to Ak​, hence similar to A. All eigenvalues are preserved exactly.

Convergence. Under mild conditions, Ak​ converges to a block upper triangular matrix (Schur form) whose diagonal blocks are 1×1 (real eigenvalues) or 2×2 (complex conjugate pairs). The subdiagonal entries decay to zero.

The convergence rate of the (i,i+1) subdiagonal entry is O(​λi​λi+1​​​k) — the same ratio as the power method, which makes sense: QR iteration is essentially running the power method simultaneously for all eigenvalues.

Practical QR algorithm (what LAPACK actually does):

  1. Reduce A to Hessenberg form H=Q0T​AQ0​ via Householder reflections. One-time cost O(n3).
  2. Apply QR iteration with shifts: at each step, choose a shift σk​ (e.g., the bottom-right entry of Ak​, or a Wilkinson shift) and factor Ak​−σk​I=Qk​Rk​, then set Ak+1​=Rk​Qk​+σk​I.
  3. Deflate: once a subdiagonal entry is negligible, split the matrix and work on smaller pieces.

With Wilkinson shifts, the convergence is typically cubic for the last subdiagonal entry. In practice, for a symmetric tridiagonal n×n matrix, the full eigendecomposition takes O(n2) iterations total.

Special Structure: Sparse and Symmetric Matrices

For very large matrices (think n=106) arising in PDE discretizations, finite element methods, or graph problems, even storing the full matrix is impossible. These matrices are sparse: most entries are zero. The algorithms of choice are Krylov subspace methods.

Lanczos algorithm (symmetric case). The key observation: A, Ab0​, A2b0​, … span a Krylov subspace. Instead of iterating randomly, build an orthonormal basis for Kk​=spanb0​,Ab0​,…,Ak−1b0​ via a three-term recurrence (for symmetric A):

βj+1​qj+1​=Aqj​−αj​qj​−βj​qj−1​

where αj​=qjT​Aqj​ and βj+1​=∣Aqj​−αj​qj​−βj​qj−1​∣.

After k steps, you have a k×k tridiagonal matrix Tk​ with diagonal α1​,…,αk​ and off-diagonal β2​,…,βk​. Its eigenvalues (Ritz values) approximate the extreme eigenvalues of A.

Key property. The Lanczos algorithm only requires matrix-vector products Aq. For a sparse matrix stored in compressed format, each product costs O(nnz) where nnz is the number of nonzero entries. You never form A explicitly or do anything that costs more than a matrix-vector multiply per step.

For a matrix with n=106 and nnz =107, you might need k=100 Lanczos steps to get good approximations of the 10 largest eigenvalues — a total cost of 109 operations, entirely feasible.

Arnoldi algorithm (non-symmetric case). Generalization of Lanczos to non-symmetric matrices. Produces an upper Hessenberg projection of A onto the Krylov subspace. Eigenvalues of the Hessenberg projection approximate eigenvalues of A.

Bounding the Spectral Radius

The spectral radius of A is ρ(A)=maxi​∣λi​∣. Several elementary bounds:

From matrix norms. For any induced matrix norm ∣⋅∣:

ρ(A)≤∣A∣

The spectral radius is the infimum of ∣A∣ over all consistent matrix norms. In particular:

ρ(A)≤∣A∣1​=maxj​∑i​∣aij​∣(max column sum) ρ(A)≤∣A∣∞​=maxi​∑j​∣aij​∣(max row sum) ρ(A)≤∣A∣F​=∑i,j​aij2​​(Frobenius norm)

From Gershgorin. ρ(A)≤maxi​(∣aii​∣+Ri​), the radius of the outermost Gershgorin disc.

Perron-Frobenius (for non-negative matrices). If A has all non-negative entries and is irreducible, then the spectral radius is a simple eigenvalue with a positive eigenvector. This is fundamental in Markov chains, PageRank, and network analysis — the dominant eigenvalue of a transition matrix is 1, and the corresponding eigenvector is the stationary distribution.

Why the Characteristic Polynomial is a Bad Algorithm

Worth making this explicit. Suppose you form p(λ)=det(A−λI) for a 20×20 matrix and try to find its roots numerically. The coefficients of p involve sums of products of up to 20 entries of A. These coefficients can be astronomically large or small. A tiny perturbation in a coefficient (say, due to floating point) can shift a root by a huge amount.

The classic demonstration: Wilkinson’s polynomial ∏k=120​(λ−k) has roots 1,2,…,20. Perturbing the coefficient of λ19 by 2−23 moves one root from the real axis to a complex value far from any integer. The roots are wildly sensitive to the coefficients.

The QR algorithm avoids this entirely by working with the matrix directly, using orthogonal transformations that are perfectly conditioned (orthogonal matrices have condition number 1).

A Personal Remark

The gap between “find eigenvalues of a 3×3 matrix” and “find eigenvalues of a 1000×1000 matrix” is not just computational — it is conceptual. The characteristic polynomial approach, which feels so clean theoretically, becomes completely useless. You are forced to think differently: iteratively, approximately, exploiting structure.

The QR algorithm is the right way to think about this. It does not solve an equation; it deforms the matrix continuously, step by step, toward a form where eigenvalues are readable. The eigenvalues were always there in the diagonal entries — you just needed to conjugate away the off-diagonal noise. That is a very different philosophy from “find roots of a polynomial.”
