08.Vectors That Span a Subspace

08.Vectors That Span a Subspace

Apr 06, 202611 min read

The Definition

A set of vectors v1​,v2​,…,vk​∈Rn spans a subspace V if every vector in V can be written as a linear combination of v1​,…,vk​.

That is: for every v∈V, there exist scalars c1​,…,ck​ such that

v=c1​v1​+c2​v2​+⋯+ck​vk​

The subspace V itself is then called the span of v1​,…,vk​, written

V=span(v1​,v2​,…,vk​)

The span is always a subspace — it contains 0 (take all ci​=0), is closed under addition, and is closed under scalar multiplication. These are immediate from the definition of linear combination. The span of any set of vectors is the smallest subspace containing all of them.

Spanning as a Column Space Statement

If I arrange v1​,…,vk​ as columns of a matrix A:

A=(∣​∣​​∣ v1​​v2​​⋯​vk​ ∣​∣​​∣​)

then span(v1​,…,vk​)=C(A), the column space of A.

The question “does v1​,…,vk​ span V?” becomes: “is V⊆C(A)?” And if V=C(A), they span exactly V.

This reframing is important. All the machinery of row reduction — rank, pivot columns, the rref — now applies directly to spanning questions.

A Worked Example

Do the following three vectors span R3?

v1​=(1 0 2​),v2​=(0 1 3​),v3​=(2 1 7​)

Form A=[v1​∣v2​∣v3​] and row reduce:

A=(1​0​2 0​1​1 2​1​7​)

R3​←R3​−2R1​:

(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​1​3​)

R3​←R3​−R2​:

(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​0​2​)

Three pivots. Rank r=3=m. The column space C(A)=R3.

So yes — v1​,v2​,v3​ span R3.

But notice: v3​=2v1​+v2​ from the linear dependence (column 3 is a free column… wait, there are three pivots here, so actually all columns are pivot columns and the vectors are independent). Let me verify: 2(1,0,2)T+1(0,1,3)T=(2,1,7)T=v3​. Yes — v3​=2v1​+v2​.

This is a contradiction: three pivots means full rank means the columns are independent. But I just showed v3​=2v1​+v2​.

Let me recheck. A has three columns and three rows. The echelon form has three nonzero rows. Three pivots. So rank is 3. But if rank is 3 and there are 3 columns, the nullspace is 0, meaning the columns are independent. But 2v1​+v2​−v3​=(2,0,4)T+(0,1,3)T−(2,1,7)T=(0,0,0)T. That is a nontrivial combination equaling zero. So the columns are dependent. There is an error somewhere in my elimination.

Recheck: R3​−2R1​=(2,1,7)−2(1,0,2)=(2−2,1−0,7−4)=(0,1,3). Correct.
R3​−R2​=(0,1,3)−(0,1,1)=(0,0,2).

So the (3,3) entry is 2, not zero. Third pivot exists. But the combination 2v1​+v2​−v3​=0 should mean the nullspace is nontrivial. Let me recheck that combination:

2v1​=(2,0,4)T, v2​=(0,1,3)T, sum =(2,1,7)T. And v3​=(2,1,7)T. So 2v1​+v2​=v3​ means 2v1​+v2​−v3​=0. So c=(2,1,−1)T should be in N(A).

Check: Ac=1⋅(2)+0⋅(1)+2⋅(−1)=0. Row 1: 2+0−2=0. Row 2: 0+1−1=0. Row 3: 4+3−7=0. Yes. So (2,1,−1)T∈N(A).

The error: the echelon form I computed is wrong. Let me redo carefully.

A=(1​0​2 0​1​1 2​1​7​)

R3​←R3​−2R1​: (2−2⋅1,1−2⋅0,7−2⋅2)=(0,1,3).

(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​1​3​)

R3​←R3​−R2​: (0−0,1−1,3−1)=(0,0,2).

(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​0​2​)

Three pivots. Rank 3. Nullspace =0. But the arithmetic above shows (2,1,−1)∈N(A). Contradiction.

There is an error in my combination check. Recompute Ac for c=(2,1,−1)T:

Row 1: 1⋅2+0⋅1+2⋅(−1)=2+0−2=0.
Row 2: 0⋅2+1⋅1+1⋅(−1)=0+1−1=0.
Row 3: 2⋅2+1⋅1+7⋅(−1)=4+1−7=−2=0.

Row 3 is −2, not 0. So (2,1,−1)T∈/N(A). My earlier combination check was wrong. 2v1​+v2​=v3​: the third component gives 2⋅2+3=7=v3​‘s third component. Wait: 2v1​+v2​=2(1,0,2)T+(0,1,3)T=(2,0,4)T+(0,1,3)T=(2,1,7)T=v3​. That is correct.

The issue is in my row 3 matrix-vector product. The matrix A has rows (1,0,2), (0,1,1), (2,1,7) — these are the rows, not the columns. The columns are v1​,v2​,v3​. Row 3 of Ac: 2⋅2+1⋅1+7⋅(−1)=4+1−7=−2. But I want A⋅(2,1,−1)T where the columns of A are v1​,v2​,v3​. That means Ac=2v1​+1⋅v2​+(−1)v3​=(2,1,7)T−(2,1,7)T=0. The row-by-row computation must give the same answer.

Row 3 of A is (2,1,7) (the third entries of v1​,v2​,v3​, i.e., row 3 of A=[v1​∣v2​∣v3​]). So row 3 of Ac=2⋅2+1⋅1+7⋅(−1)… but A3,1​=2 (third entry of v1​), A3,2​=1 (third entry of v2​), A3,3​=7 (third entry of v3​). Then (Ac)3​=2⋅2+1⋅1+7⋅(−1)=4+1−7=−2.

But 2v1​+v2​−v3​ has third component 2⋅2+1⋅3+(−1)⋅7=4+3−7=0.

The discrepancy: row 3 of Ac uses row 3 of the matrix A, which is (A3,1​,A3,2​,A3,3​)=(v1​[3],v2​[3],v3​[3])=(2,3,7), not (2,1,7). I wrote the matrix A incorrectly above. The correct matrix is:

A=(1​0​2 0​1​1 2​3​7​)

because v3​=(2,1,7)T has second entry 1, not matching — wait. v1​=(1,0,2)T, v2​=(0,1,3)T, v3​=(2,1,7)T. So:

A=(1​0​2 0​1​1 2​3​7​)

Row 3 is (2,3,7), not (2,1,7). I misread v2​‘s third entry earlier — I wrote v2​=(0,1,3)T but then used 1 as its third entry. The third entry of v2​ is 3. I will restart with the corrected matrix.

Corrected reduction:

A=(1​0​2 0​1​1 2​3​7​)

R3​←R3​−2R1​: (2−2,3−0,7−4)=(0,3,3).

(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​3​3​)

R3​←R3​−3R2​: (0,3−3,3−3)=(0,0,0).

U=(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​0​0​)

Rank r=2. Column 3 is a free column. The vectors do not span R3. Their span is a 2-dimensional plane — consistent with v3​=2v1​+v2​.

This is a useful lesson in its own right: always carry all entries carefully. A single wrong entry produces a completely wrong conclusion about spanning.

The Spanning Test

The question “do v1​,…,vk​ span Rm?” reduces to:

rank(A)=m

where A is the m×k matrix with vi​ as columns.

This requires k≥m — you need at least m vectors to have any chance of spanning Rm. But having k≥m vectors is not sufficient; they must have m independent ones among them.

More generally, v1​,…,vk​ span a subspace V of dimension d if and only if the column space of A equals V, equivalently if rank(A)=d and C(A)=V.

Too Many Vectors: Spanning with Redundancy

Consider four vectors in R2:

v1​=(1 0​),v2​=(0 1​),v3​=(1 1​),v4​=(2 3​)

These four vectors span R2 — in fact v1​ and v2​ alone already span R2, and v3​,v4​ are redundant. The matrix A=[v1​∣v2​∣v3​∣v4​] has rank 2 (since R2 has dimension 2), and the null space has dimension 4−2=2, reflecting the two redundancies.

Spanning with more vectors than necessary is fine — it just means the spanning set is not a basis. The dependence relations live in the nullspace.

Too Few Vectors: Cannot Span

If k<m, then k vectors in Rm cannot span Rm, because rank(A)≤k<m. The column space C(A) is a proper subspace of Rm — a lower-dimensional flat through the origin.

One vector in R3: spans a line.
Two independent vectors in R3: span a plane.
Three independent vectors in R3: span all of R3.

The dimension of the span equals the rank of the matrix formed by the vectors.

The Subspace Spanned Is the Column Space

This is the point I want to engrave. Given any set of vectors, their span is the column space of the matrix they form. Every question about spanning is a question about the column space. Every question about the column space is a question about rank.

The column space C(A) is spanned by the columns of A. But more usefully, it is spanned by the pivot columns of A alone — the free columns are linear combinations of the pivot columns and contribute nothing new to the span. The pivot columns form a basis for C(A).

From the corrected example:

U=(1​0​2 0​1​1 0​0​0​)

Pivot columns: 1 and 2. So v1​=(1,0,2)T and v2​=(0,1,3)T form a basis for span(v1​,v2​,v3​). The span is the plane

\left{ a\begin{pmatrix}1\0\2\end{pmatrix} + b\begin{pmatrix}0\1\3\end{pmatrix} : a, b \in \mathbb{R} \right} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3

Note carefully: the pivot columns of A (the original matrix, not U) are the basis. Pivot columns of U are e1​ and e2​, which span a different plane. The row operations change the columns.

Spanning vs. Independence: The Inverse Pressures

These two properties pull in opposite directions, and recognizing this tension is fundamental:

  • To span Rm: need rank r=m, which requires k≥m vectors
  • To be independent: need rank r=k, which requires r≤n and no redundancy

A spanning set can always be reduced to a basis by removing redundant vectors (the ones corresponding to free columns).

An independent set can always be extended to a basis by adding vectors that cover new directions.

A basis is a set that is simultaneously spanning and independent. It is the minimal spanning set and the maximal independent set.

Connections to Track Later

  • Basis and Dimension — the minimal spanning set is a basis; dimension equals rank
  • Linear Independence — independence is the complementary condition to spanning; together they define a basis
  • The Column Space and Its Basis — pivot columns of A are the canonical basis for C(A)
  • The Four Fundamental Subspaces — all four subspaces are defined as spans or orthogonal complements
  • Gram-Schmidt and Orthogonal Bases — given a spanning set, Gram-Schmidt produces an orthogonal basis; still spans the same subspace, but with much nicer geometry
