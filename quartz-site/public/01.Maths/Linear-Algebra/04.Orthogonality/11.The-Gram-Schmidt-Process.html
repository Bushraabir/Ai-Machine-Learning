11.The Gram-Schmidt Process

Apr 06, 20267 min read

Why We Need This

The normal equations ATAx^=ATb are the right answer to least squares, but they are not always the right computation. Forming ATA explicitly squares the condition number of A, which can be catastrophic for nearly-dependent columns.

The deeper issue is this: if the columns of A were orthonormal to begin with, the normal equations would collapse to x^=QTb — no matrix to invert, no system to solve. Each coefficient is just a dot product.

Gram-Schmidt is the algorithm that takes any linearly independent set of vectors and produces an orthonormal set spanning the same space. It is how we manufacture that nice situation from any starting basis.

The Idea in One Sentence

At each step, take the next vector, subtract off all its components in the directions already handled, and normalize what remains.

The Process: Two Vectors First

Start with two linearly independent vectors a and b in Rm.

First vector. Just normalize:

q1​=∣a∣a​

Second vector. Take b and subtract its projection onto q1​:

b′=b−(q1T​b),q1​

The vector b′ is orthogonal to q1​ by construction:

q1T​b′=q1T​b−(q1T​b)(q1T​q1​)=q1T​b−q1T​b=0

Then normalize:

q2​=∣b′∣b′​

The pair q1​,q2​ is an orthonormal basis for spana,b.

The Process: Three Vectors

Add a third vector c, linearly independent of a and b.

Subtract off the projections onto both q1​ and q2​:

c′=c−(q1T​c),q1​−(q2T​c),q2​

Verify: q1T​c′=q1T​c−(q1T​c)(1)−(q2T​c)(q1T​q2​)=0 since q1T​q2​=0. Similarly q2T​c′=0.

Then normalize:

q3​=∣c′∣c′​

The set q1​,q2​,q3​ is an orthonormal basis for spana,b,c.

The General Step

Given vectors a1​,a2​,…,an​ linearly independent. After producing q1​,…,qk−1​, the k-th step is:

ak′​=ak​−∑j=1k−1​(qjT​ak​),qj​

qk​=∣ak′​∣ak′​​

The scalar qjT​ak​ is the component of ak​ in the direction qj​. Subtracting removes that component. The result ak′​ is orthogonal to all of q1​,…,qk−1​.

Note: if ak′​=0 at any step, it means ak​ was already in the span of a1​,…,ak−1​ — the original vectors were linearly dependent. Gram-Schmidt detects dependence by producing a zero vector.

A Fully Worked Example

Take three vectors in R3:

a1​=(1 1 0​),a2​=(1 0 1​),a3​=(0 1 1​)

Step 1: Orthogonalize and normalize a1​.

∣a1​∣=1+1+0​=2​

q1​=2​1​(1 1 0​)

Step 2: Orthogonalize a2​ against q1​.

q1T​a2​=2​1​(1⋅1+1⋅0+0⋅1)=2​1​

a2′​=a2​−(q1T​a2​),q1​=(1\0\1​)−2​1​⋅2​1​(1\1\0​)=(1\0\1​)−21​(1\1\0​)=(1/2\-1/2\1​)

∣a2′​∣=41​+41​+1​=23​​=26​​

q2​=6​2​(1/2\-1/2\1​)=6​1​(1\-1\2​)

Check: q1T​q2​=2​1​⋅6​1​(1−1+0)=0. ✓

Step 3: Orthogonalize a3​ against q1​ and q2​.

q1T​a3​=2​1​(0+1+0)=2​1​

q2T​a3​=6​1​(0−1+2)=6​1​

a3′​=a3​−(q1T​a3​),q1​−(q2T​a3​),q2​

=(0\1\1​)−2​1​⋅2​1​(1\1\0​)−6​1​⋅6​1​(1\-1\2​)

=(0\1\1​)−21​(1\1\0​)−61​(1\-1\2​)

=(0\1\1​)−(1/2\1/2\0​)−(1/6\-1/6\1/3​)=(0−1/2−1/6 1−1/2+1/6 1−0−1/3​)=(−2/3\2/3\2/3​)

∣a3′​∣=94​+94​+94​​=912​​=323​​=3​2​

q3​=23​​⋅(−2/3\2/3\2/3​)=3​1​(−1\1\1​)

Check: q1T​q3​=2​3​1​(−1+1+0)=0. ✓

q2T​q3​=6​3​1​(−1⋅1+(−1)⋅1+2⋅1)⋅11​

Wait, more carefully: q2T​q3​=6​1​⋅3​1​(1⋅(−1)+(−1)⋅1+2⋅1)=18​1​(−1−1+2)=0. ✓

The orthonormal basis for R3 is:

q1​=2​1​(1\1\0​),q2​=6​1​(1\-1\2​),q3​=3​1​(−1\1\1​)

The Connection to QR Factorization

This is the payoff. Trace what Gram-Schmidt actually says about the relationship between ak​ and the qj​‘s.

At step k, we wrote:

ak​=ak′​+∑j=1k−1​(qjT​ak​),qj​=∣ak′​∣,qk​+∑j=1k−1​(qjT​ak​),qj​

This expresses ak​ as a linear combination of q1​,…,qk​ with no qk+1​,…,qn​ appearing. The coefficients are:

rjk​=qjT​ak​(j<k),rkk​=∣ak′​∣

In matrix form, if A=[a1​∣a2​∣⋯∣an​] and Q=[q1​∣q2​∣⋯∣qn​], then:

A=QR

where R is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries rkk​=∣ak′​∣>0.

Gram-Schmidt is not just an orthogonalization algorithm. It is the QR factorization, column by column.

For the worked example:

R=(∣a1​∣​q1T​a2​​q1T​a3​ 0​∣a2′​∣​q2T​a3​ 0​0​∣a3′​∣​)=(2​​2​1​​2​1​ 0​26​​​6​1​ 0​0​3​2​​)

Why Orthonormal Columns Make Everything Easy

If A=QR with Q having orthonormal columns (QTQ=I), then the normal equations

ATAx^=ATb

become

(QR)T(QR)x^=(QR)Tb

RTQTQRx^=RTQTb

RTRx^=RTQTb

Since R is invertible (positive diagonal), RT is invertible, so:

Rx^=QTb

A triangular system. No ATA to form. No squaring of the condition number. This is why QR is the numerically preferred method for least squares.

The solution is:

x^=R−1QTb

and the projection of b onto C(A) is:

b^=Ax^=QR⋅R−1QTb=QQTb

When Q is square (full orthonormal basis), QQT=I. When Q is m×n with m>n (thin QR), QQT=I but it is still the projection matrix onto C(A).

Projections With an Orthonormal Basis

The special case where Gram-Schmidt pays off most visibly: if q1​,…,qn​ is an orthonormal basis for a subspace S, then the projection of any b onto S is:

b^=(q1T​b),q1​+(q2T​b),q2​+⋯+(qnT​b),qn​

Each coefficient (qjT​b) is independent of all the others. The normal equations have become diagonal — ATA=I when A=Q. This is the orthonormal basis advantage: projections decompose into independent one-dimensional projections.

In the language of matrices: P=QQT=∑j=1n​qj​qjT​, a sum of rank-one projections.

What Gram-Schmidt Does Not Do

Gram-Schmidt is numerically unstable in its classical form. The issue is error accumulation: later vectors subtract projections onto q1​,…,qk−1​, and rounding errors in those projections propagate. For large n, the vectors qj​ produced by classical Gram-Schmidt can lose orthogonality badly.

The fix is modified Gram-Schmidt: instead of subtracting all projections from the original ak​ at once, subtract one projection at a time, updating the running vector after each subtraction. This gives the same result in exact arithmetic but far better numerical behavior.

In practice, Householder reflections give an even more stable QR factorization than either form of Gram-Schmidt. But that is a numerical linear algebra story.

Things to Remember (for Myself)

  • The formula at each step: ak′​=ak​−∑j<k​(qjT​ak​),qj​. Then qk​=ak′​/∣ak′​∣.
  • If ak′​=0: the original vectors are dependent. Stop — or discard ak​ and continue.
  • The coefficients rjk​=qjT​ak​ are exactly the entries of R in A=QR.
  • QTQ=I always (orthonormal columns). QQT=I only when Q is square.
  • With QR, the normal equations become Rx^=QTb. Back-substitution, done.
  • Projection onto S=spanq1​,…,qn​: just sum up the rank-one projections (qjT​b)qj​.
  • Classical Gram-Schmidt is unstable for large systems. Modified Gram-Schmidt is better. Householder is best.
