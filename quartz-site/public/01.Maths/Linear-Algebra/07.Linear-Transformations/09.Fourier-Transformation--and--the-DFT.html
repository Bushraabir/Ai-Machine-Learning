09.Fourier Transformation & the DFT

09.Fourier Transformation & the DFT

Apr 14, 202612 min read

Why this note exists

Every time I think I understand Fourier analysis, I realize I was thinking about it too narrowly. The first time it was a formula for coefficients. The second time it was an inner product. The third time it was a change of basis. The fourth — and I think this is the right one — it is a statement about which basis diagonalizes the convolution operator.

These are not four different facts. They are the same fact, seen from different distances. This note is my attempt to hold all four views at once.

The Continuous Fourier Transform

Let f∈L2(R). The Fourier transform is the map F:L2(R)→L2(R) defined by

f^​(ξ)=∫−∞∞​f(t),e−2πiξt,dt

The inversion formula recovers f from f^​:

f(t)=∫−∞∞​f^​(ξ),e2πiξt,dξ

These two formulas are nearly symmetric — the only asymmetry is the sign in the exponent. There are different conventions (some put the 2π in the exponent, some put 2π1​ in front of the inverse). The convention above makes Parseval’s theorem clean:

∫−∞∞​∣f(t)∣2,dt=∫−∞∞​∣f^​(ξ)∣2,dξ

In other words, F is a unitary operator on L2(R): it preserves the norm. In finite dimensions, unitary = orthogonal (over C). That is the 18.06 analog.

The Fourier Series (Periodic Case)

Before the DFT, it is worth being clear about what happens for periodic functions, since the DFT is really a discretization of this.

Let f:[0,1]→C be square-integrable and 1-periodic. The Fourier series is

f(t)=∑k=−∞∞​ck​,e2πikt

where the coefficients are

ck​=∫01​f(t),e−2πikt,dt=⟨f,ek​⟩

with ek​(t)=e2πikt.

The key fact: ek​k∈Z​ is an orthonormal basis for L2([0,1]):

⟨ej​,ek​⟩=∫01​e2πijte2πikt,dt=∫01​e2πi(j−k)t,dt=δjk​

So ck​=⟨f,ek​⟩ is just a coordinate in an orthonormal basis. The reconstruction f=∑ck​ek​ is the standard expansion in an ONB. There is nothing mysterious here — it is 18.06 projection, but into an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.

The Discrete Fourier Transform: Setup

Now discretize. Sample f at n equally spaced points: fj​=f(j/n) for j=0,1,…,n−1. We have a vector f=(f0​,f1​,…,fn−1​)∈Cn.

Define the primitive n-th root of unity:

ωn​=e2πi/n

The Discrete Fourier Transform of f is the vector f^∈Cn with

f^​k​=∑j=0n−1​fj​,ωn−jk​,k=0,1,…,n−1

The inverse DFT is

fj​=n1​∑k=0n−1​f^​k​,ωnjk​,j=0,1,…,n−1

Note the n1​ normalization in the inverse — this is one convention. Another places n​1​ symmetrically in both directions (making the DFT matrix unitary). I will keep this convention for now because it matches most implementations.

The DFT as a Matrix: The Fourier Matrix Fn​

This is the 18.06 heart of it. The DFT is a matrix-vector multiplication:

f^=Fn​f

where Fn​ is the n×n Fourier matrix with entries

(Fn​)kj​=ωn−jk​=e−2πijk/n,j,k=0,1,…,n−1

Let me write Fn​ explicitly for n=4, using ω=ω4​=e2πi/4=i:

F4​=(1​1​1​1 1​ω−1​ω−2​ω−3 1​ω−2​ω−4​ω−6 1​ω−3​ω−6​ω−9​)=(1​1​1​1 1​−i​−1​i 1​−1​1​−1 1​i​−1​−i​)

(using ω−1=e−2πi/4=e−iπ/2=−i, ω−2=−1, ω−3=i, and periodicity ω−4=1.)

The key algebraic fact: the columns of Fn​ are orthogonal. Specifically, if F~n​=n​1​Fn​, then F~n​ is unitary: F~n∗​F~n​=I.

Proof. The (j,k) entry of Fn∗​Fn​ is the inner product of column j and column k of Fn​:

(Fn∗​Fn​)jk​=∑m=0n−1​(Fn​)mj​​,(Fn​)mk​=∑m=0n−1​ωnmj​⋅ωn−mk​=∑m=0n−1​ωnm(j−k)​

If j=k, each term is 1, so the sum is n.

If j=k, this is a geometric series with ratio r=ωnj−k​=1 (since j−k≡0(modn)):

∑m=0n−1​rm=r−1rn−1​=ωnj−k​−1(ωnj−k​)n−1​=ωnj−k​−1e2πi(j−k)−1​=ωnj−k​−11−1​=0

So (Fn∗​Fn​)jk​=nδjk​, which gives Fn∗​Fn​=nI. Thus Fn−1​=n1​Fn∗​, and the inverse DFT formula follows immediately. ■

The columns of Fn​ form an orthogonal basis for Cn (not orthonormal, but that is just a scalar factor). The DFT is a change of basis from the standard basis ej​ to the Fourier basis \left{ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \text{col}_k(F_n) \right}.

The General n Fourier Matrix

For completeness, here is Fn​ in full generality. Let ω=e2πi/n. Row index k, column index j, both from 0 to n−1:

Fn​=(1​1​1​1​⋯​1 1​ω−1​ω−2​ω−3​⋯​ω−(n−1) 1​ω−2​ω−4​ω−6​⋯​ω−2(n−1) 1​ω−3​ω−6​ω−9​⋯​ω−3(n−1) ⋮​⋮​⋮​⋮​⋱​⋮ 1​ω−(n−1)​ω−2(n−1)​ω−3(n−1)​⋯​ω−(n−1)2​)

Entry (k,j) is ω−jk. The matrix is symmetric: (Fn​)kj​=(Fn​)jk​. It is not Hermitian — it is complex symmetric.

For real signals f∈Rn, the DFT satisfies the conjugate symmetry: f^​n−k​=f^​k​​. So only the first ⌊n/2⌋+1 coefficients are independent. This is why FFT routines return n/2+1 complex numbers for a real input of length n.

Convolution and the Convolution Theorem

This is, to me, the deepest reason Fourier analysis is important.

Definition. The circular convolution of f,g∈Cn is

(f∗g)k​=∑j=0n−1​fj​,gk−jmodn​

Theorem (Convolution Theorem).

f∗g​=f^⊙g^​

where ⊙ denotes pointwise multiplication.

Proof. Direct computation:

(fg​)​k​=∑m=0n−1​(fg​)m​ω−mk=∑m=0n−1​(∑j=0n−1​fj​gm−j​)ω−mk

Substitute ℓ=m−j:

=∑j=0n−1​fj​∑ℓ=0n−1​gℓ​,ω−(j+ℓ)k=(∑j=0n−1​fj​ω−jk)!(∑ℓ=0n−1​gℓ​ω−ℓk)=f^​k​⋅g^​k​■

The linear algebra statement: the Fourier matrix Fn​ diagonalizes every circulant matrix. A circulant matrix C has the form

C=(c0​​cn−1​​cn−2​​⋯​c1​ c1​​c0​​cn−1​​⋯​c2​ c2​​c1​​c0​​⋯​c3​ ⋮​​​⋱​⋮ cn−1​​cn−2​​cn−3​​⋯​c0​​)

Then C=Fn−1​diag(c^),Fn​, where c^=Fn​c and c=(c0​,c1​,…,cn−1​)T.

In other words: the eigenvectors of every circulant matrix are exactly the columns of Fn​. Convolution with c is multiplication by a circulant, and the Fourier modes ωjk are the eigenvectors. This is the algebraic reason the Fourier transform is the right tool for shift-invariant systems.

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

Naive DFT costs O(n2) — for each of n output frequencies, sum n terms. The FFT reduces this to O(nlogn) by exploiting the structure of Fn​.

Cooley-Tukey decomposition (for n=2N):

Split f into even-indexed and odd-indexed samples:

f^​k​=∑j=0n−1​fj​ωn−jk​=∑j=0n/2−1​f2j​ωn−2jk​+∑j=0n/2−1​f2j+1​ωn−(2j+1)k​

=∑j=0n/2−1​f2j​ωn/2−jk​+ωn−k​∑j=0n/2−1​f2j+1​ωn/2−jk​

(using ωn−2jk​=e−2πi⋅2jk/n=e−2πijk/(n/2)=ωn/2−jk​.)

Let Ek​=DFTn/2​(feven​)k​ and Ok​=DFTn/2​(fodd​)k​. Then:

f^​k​=Ek​+ωn−k​Ok​ f^​k+n/2​=Ek​−ωn−k​Ok​

(using periodicity of Ek​ and Ok​ with period n/2.) This is the butterfly operation. Each level of the recursion does O(n) work; there are log2​n levels, giving O(nlogn) total.

In matrix language: Fn​ factors as

Fn​=(I​D I​−D​)(Fn/2​​0 0​Fn/2​​)P

where D=diag(1,ωn−1​,ωn−2​,…,ωn−(n/2−1)​) (the twiddle factors) and P is the bit-reversal permutation (separates even and odd indices).

Apply recursively: Fn/2​ factors similarly. After log2​n levels, Fn​ is expressed as a product of log2​n sparse matrices, each with only 2 nonzeros per row. This is the factored view from Strang.

For n=8, the full factorization is:

F8​=(I4​​D4​ I4​​−D4​​)(F4​​0 0​F4​​)P8​

Then each F4​ factors similarly. The total operation count: 2n​log2​n=4⋅3=12 complex multiplications (vs. 64 for naive n2).

Parseval / Plancherel for the DFT

For the DFT with the normalization f^=Fn​f:

∑j=0n−1​∣fj​∣2=n1​∑k=0n−1​∣f^​k​∣2

Or equivalently, with the unitary normalization F~n​=n​1​Fn​:

∣f∣2=∣F~n​f∣2

This is just the isometry property of a unitary matrix: ∣Ux∣=∣x∣ for all x. In 18.06 language: orthogonal (unitary) transformations preserve lengths. Parseval is not a special theorem — it is the trivial fact that QTQ=I preserves ∣x∣.

Spectral Interpretation

The DFT coefficient f^​k​ measures “how much of frequency k/n is in f.” More precisely:

  • k=0: DC component, f^​0​=∑j​fj​ (proportional to mean)
  • k=1,…,⌊n/2⌋: positive frequencies, cycles 1 through n/2 per period
  • k=n/2+1,…,n−1: these are aliases of negative frequencies due to sampling; k=n−m corresponds to frequency −m/n

The Nyquist frequency is k=n/2, corresponding to the highest resolvable oscillation: alternating +1,−1,+1,−1,…. Any frequency above this aliases back down — this is the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, which follows directly from the periodicity of ωn​.

Eigenstructure Revisited

The eigenvalues of the circulant matrix C (with first row c) are precisely c^0​,c^1​,…,c^n−1​ — the DFT of the first row. The eigenvectors are the columns of n1​Fn∗​ (or equivalently rows of Fn​).

This gives an extremely fast way to solve circulant linear systems Cx=b:

  1. Compute b^=Fn​b — one FFT, O(nlogn)
  2. Divide: x^k​=b^k​/c^k​ for each k
  3. Invert: x=Fn−1​x^ — one IFFT, O(nlogn)

Compare to Gaussian elimination for a general n×n system: O(n3). For a circulant system of size n=106, this is the difference between 1018 operations and 2×107. This is why PDEs with periodic boundary conditions on uniform grids are solved spectrally.

The 2D DFT

For images (or any function on a 2D grid), the DFT extends naturally as a separable transform. For an M×N image f[m,n]:

f^​[k,ℓ]=∑m=0M−1​∑n=0N−1​f[m,n],e−2πi(mk/M+nℓ/N)

In matrix form: F^=FM​⋅F⋅FNT​ (applying the 1D DFT to rows, then columns). This works because e−2πi(mk/M+nℓ/N)=e−2πimk/M⋅e−2πinℓ/N — the kernel factors.

JPEG (original, not JPEG 2000) computes the 2D DCT (Discrete Cosine Transform, a real-valued variant of the DFT) on 8×8 blocks, quantizes the high-frequency coefficients, and discards the small ones. The reason it works: natural images have energy concentrated in low frequencies. The DFT basis is the right one to exploit this.

Relation to the Wavelet Note

The DFT uses globally supported basis functions — each Fourier mode e2πikt/n involves all n samples. A single spike at position j has nonzero DFT coefficients everywhere (since f^​k​=ωn−jk​ with ∣ωn−jk​∣=1). So localized features are maximally delocalized in the Fourier domain.

Wavelets trade global frequency precision for local time-frequency information. The DFT is the right tool when:

  • The signal is stationary (statistics don’t change over time)
  • Global frequency content is what matters
  • The system is shift-invariant (circulant)

Wavelets are the right tool when:

  • The signal has transients, discontinuities, or nonstationarity
  • We need to know both when and at what frequency something happens
  • We want sparse representations of piecewise smooth functions

The two are not in competition. They are optimal for different signal classes.

Things I Want to Come Back To

  • Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) — real-valued, used in JPEG and MP3; arises naturally from considering symmetric extensions of real signals
  • Chirp-Z transform — computing the DFT on a spiral contour rather than the unit circle; generalizes FFT to arbitrary sampling grids
  • Non-uniform DFT (NUDFT/NFFT) — when samples are not equally spaced; loses the FFT structure but can be approximated
  • Number-theoretic transform — DFT over Z/pZ instead of C; used in fast polynomial multiplication for exact arithmetic
  • Multidimensional FFT and tensor structure — the FM​⋅F⋅FNT​ factorization is a Kronecker product, and Kronecker products appear everywhere in numerical linear algebra

Summary

The DFT is the matrix Fn​ with entries ωn−jk​. Its columns are orthogonal — this is the geometric series identity. Because Fn​ is (up to normalization) unitary, it is an orthonormal change of basis. Because its columns are the eigenvectors of every circulant matrix, the DFT diagonalizes convolution. The FFT is not a different transform — it is the same Fn​, factored into log2​n sparse matrices by recursively exploiting the even-odd structure of ωn2​=ωn/2​.

Everything reduces to: columns of Fn​ form an orthogonal basis, and that basis happens to diagonalize the convolution operator.

This note was written alongside the Wavelet note. The MRA connection — Fourier as the flat case where all detail spaces are just shifted sinusoids — is something I want to make precise eventually. For now, the circulant diagonalization is enough to feel like I actually understand why Fourier works, not just how to apply it.
