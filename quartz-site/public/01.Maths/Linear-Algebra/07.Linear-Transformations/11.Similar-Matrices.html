11.Similar Matrices

The Core Idea

Two matrices A and B are similar if there exists an invertible matrix M such that

B=M−1AM

This is the definition. Everything else in this note is unpacking what it means, why it matters, and what it tells us about the deep structure of a linear map.

The way I think about it: A and B are different representations of the same linear transformation — just written in different bases. The matrix M is the change-of-basis matrix that moves you between those two coordinate systems. A is how the transformation looks in one basis; B is how it looks in another. Same map, different clothes.

The Change-of-Basis Interpretation

Fix a linear transformation T:Rn→Rn. In the standard basis e1​,…,en​, the matrix of T is some A. Now suppose I want to re-express the same transformation in a new basis formed by the columns of M — call them v1​,…,vn​.

The recipe is:

  1. Take a vector expressed in the new basis: multiply by M to convert it to standard coordinates.
  2. Apply A (the transformation, in standard coordinates).
  3. Convert back to the new basis: multiply by M−1.

The composite is M−1AM. That is B.

So B=M−1AM does not define a new transformation. It defines the same transformation, viewed from a different vantage point. This is the whole story, really. Everything downstream follows from this.

Eigenvalues Are Preserved

This is the first big theorem and the reason similarity matters so much.

Theorem. If B=M−1AM, then A and B have the same eigenvalues.

Proof. We look at the characteristic polynomial.

det(B−λI)=det(M−1AM−λI)

Since λI=λM−1IM=M−1(λI)M, we can write

det(B−λI)=det(M−1(A−λI)M)

Using multiplicativity of the determinant:

=det(M−1)⋅det(A−λI)⋅det(M)

=det(M−1)det(M)⋅det(A−λI)

=det(M−1M)⋅det(A−λI)

=det(I)⋅det(A−λI)=det(A−λI)

So the characteristic polynomials are identical, hence the eigenvalues are the same. ■

This should feel obvious from the change-of-basis story: eigenvalues of a linear transformation are intrinsic to the transformation itself, not to the coordinate system you use to describe it. Of course they’re preserved.

What about eigenvectors? They change. If Ax=λx, then in the new basis the eigenvector is M−1x. The direction changes with the coordinates, but the eigenvalue — the scaling factor — does not.

Diagonalization as a Special Case

The most important application of similarity: we want B to be diagonal.

If A has n linearly independent eigenvectors x1​,…,xn​ with eigenvalues λ1​,…,λn​, then we can take M=S, the matrix whose columns are those eigenvectors:

S=[∣​∣​​∣ x1​​x2​​⋯​xn​ ∣​∣​​∣​]

Then:

S−1AS=Λ=[λ1​​0​⋯​0 0​λ2​​⋯​0 ⋮​⋮​⋱​⋮ 0​0​⋯​λn​​]

This is the diagonalization A=SΛS−1, which I’ve written before. But seen through the similarity lens it’s cleaner: we are choosing the eigenvector basis precisely because the transformation looks diagonal in that basis. The eigenvectors are the natural coordinate axes.

The change-of-basis matrix M=S takes standard coordinates into eigenvector coordinates. In those coordinates, the map just scales each axis independently. That’s all diagonalization is.

A Worked Example

Let me do this with an explicit 2×2 matrix so the algebra is honest.

Take

A=[2​1 0​3​]

Step 1: Find eigenvalues.

det(A−λI)=det[2−λ​1 0​3−λ​]=(2−λ)(3−λ)

Eigenvalues: λ1​=2, λ2​=3.

Step 2: Find eigenvectors.

For λ1​=2:

(A−2I)x=[0​1 0​1​]x=0⟹x1​=[1 0​]

For λ2​=3:

(A−3I)x=[−1​1 0​0​]x=0⟹x2​=[1 1​]

Step 3: Build S and invert it.

S=[1​1 0​1​],S−1=[1​−1 0​1​]

Step 4: Verify S−1AS=Λ.

S−1AS=[1​−1 0​1​][2​1 0​3​][1​1 0​1​]

First compute AS:

AS=[2​1 0​3​][1​1 0​1​]=[2​3 0​3​]

Then S−1(AS):

S−1AS=[1​−1 0​1​][2​3 0​3​]=[2​0 0​3​]=Λ

The diagonal entries are exactly the eigenvalues, in the order we stacked the eigenvectors. Good.

When Does Diagonalization Fail?

Not every matrix is diagonalizable. The obstruction is a shortage of eigenvectors. Two cases:

Case 1: Repeated eigenvalues with full eigenspace. A scalar matrix like cI is already diagonal and has all of Rn as its eigenspace. No problem.

Case 2: Repeated eigenvalues with deficient eigenspace (defective matrices). The classic example:

A=[0​1 0​0​]

Both eigenvalues are 0. The eigenspace for λ=0 is spanned only by e1​=(1,0)T — it is one-dimensional, not two. There is only one linearly independent eigenvector. You cannot fill the columns of S.

Such a matrix is not diagonalizable. It is similar to a Jordan block, which is the closest diagonal-like form available:

J=[0​1 0​0​]

In this case A already is a Jordan block. More generally, every matrix is similar to its Jordan normal form — but that story is for a later note.

Similar Does Not Mean Equal Entries

A common confusion worth stamping out. A and B being similar does not mean they are equal, or that they have the same entries, or even the same trace/determinant… wait, actually:

Trace and determinant are preserved under similarity.

Since similar matrices have the same eigenvalues (with multiplicity), and:

  • tr(A)=∑λi​
  • det(A)=∏λi​

Both are determined by the eigenvalues, so they are similarity invariants.

Conversely, having the same trace and determinant does not imply similarity. Take

A=[2​0 0​2​],B=[2​1 0​2​]

Both have tr=4 and det=4 and both have λ=2 (twice). But A=2I is similar only to itself (since M−1(2I)M=2I for any M), while B is a nontrivial Jordan block. They are not similar.

The complete set of similarity invariants is the Jordan normal form — eigenvalues together with the sizes of Jordan blocks.

Summary of Invariants Under Similarity

If B=M−1AM, then A and B have the same:

  • Eigenvalues (with multiplicity)
  • Characteristic polynomial det(A−λI)
  • Trace =∑λi​
  • Determinant =∏λi​
  • Rank
  • Nullity
  • Jordan normal form (up to reordering of blocks)

They do not in general have the same eigenvectors, the same entries, or the same singular values.

Why This Matters

The philosophical point: matrices are representations of linear maps with respect to a chosen basis. The choice of basis is arbitrary — it is a feature of how we coordinatize space, not a feature of the map. Similar matrices are just the same map in different coordinates.

This means any intrinsic property of the linear map — eigenvalues, rank, whether it’s invertible, what its Jordan form looks like — must be a similarity invariant. Basis-dependent quantities (entries, singular values, norms) are not intrinsic and need not be preserved.

When I want to understand a linear map, I should always ask: can I find a basis in which the matrix looks simpler? Diagonalization is the answer when it’s possible. Jordan form is the answer when it’s not.
