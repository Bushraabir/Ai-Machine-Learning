Why Diagonalization Matters

The central question of linear algebra, once you get past solving Ax=b, is: what does a matrix actually do? Most matrices mix coordinates together in a way that is hard to see. Diagonalization is the project of finding a basis in which A acts by pure scaling — where the geometry is transparent.

If A is diagonalizable, then in the right basis it is nothing more than a list of scale factors. Matrix powers, exponentials, differential equations — all become trivial. The work is entirely in finding that basis.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors: The Right Language

A nonzero vector x is an eigenvector of A if

Ax=λx

for some scalar λ∈C, called the eigenvalue. The direction of x is unchanged by A — it is only stretched (or flipped) by λ.

To find eigenvalues, rearrange: (A−λI)x=0 has a nonzero solution iff A−λI is singular:

det(A−λI)=0

This is the characteristic equation. For an n×n matrix it gives a degree-n polynomial in λ — the characteristic polynomial p(λ). Over C, it always has n roots (counted with multiplicity, by the fundamental theorem of algebra). Over R, complex roots come in conjugate pairs.

Once λ is known, the eigenvectors are null(A−λI) — the null space of a singular matrix, which is always nontrivial.

The Diagonalization Theorem

Theorem. An n×n matrix A is diagonalizable if and only if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors.

If x1​,…,xn​ are those eigenvectors with eigenvalues λ1​,…,λn​, form the eigenvector matrix S with these as columns:

S=(∣​∣​​∣ x1​​x2​​⋯​xn​ ∣​∣​​∣​)

Then the identity AS=SΛ holds column by column (Axk​=λk​xk​ for each k), where

Λ=(λ1​​​​ ​λ2​​​ ​​⋱​ ​​​λn​​)

Since S has linearly independent columns, S is invertible. So:

A=SΛS−1​

This is the eigendecomposition of A.

Proof of the column-by-column identity. Look at the k-th column of AS:

[AS]k​=Axk​=λk​xk​

The k-th column of SΛ is λk​xk​ (multiplying column k of S by the k-th diagonal entry of Λ). So AS=SΛ column by column. Since S is invertible, A=SΛS−1. □

Change of Basis: The Real Meaning

The formula A=SΛS−1 is a change of basis statement. To see this, think about what each factor does to a vector v:

  1. S−1v: express v in the eigenbasis (find its coordinates c such that v=Sc)
  2. Λ(S−1v): scale each coordinate by the corresponding eigenvalue
  3. S(ΛS−1v): convert back to the standard basis

So A is, in its own natural basis, nothing but diagonal scaling. The complexity of A in standard coordinates is an artifact of using the wrong basis.

This is Strang’s core point: every diagonalizable matrix is simple in the right coordinates. The eigenvectors are the right coordinates.

When Does Diagonalization Fail?

Two distinct failure modes.

Failure mode 1: Repeated eigenvalues with deficient eigenspaces.

A repeated eigenvalue λ has algebraic multiplicity ma​ (its multiplicity as a root of det(A−λI)) and geometric multiplicity mg​=dim,null(A−λI). Always mg​≤ma​. When mg​<ma​ for any eigenvalue, A does not have n independent eigenvectors and is not diagonalizable.

Example: A=(2​1 0​2​). The only eigenvalue is λ=2 with ma​=2.

A−2I=(0​1 0​0​)

\text{null}(A - 2I) = \text{span}\left{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \ 0 \end{pmatrix}\right}, so mg​=1<2=ma​. Only one independent eigenvector. A is a Jordan block and is not diagonalizable — but it is the canonical “almost diagonal” form (Jordan normal form).

Failure mode 2: No real eigenvalues.

The matrix A=(0​−1 1​0​) (rotation by 90°) has characteristic polynomial λ2+1=0 — no real roots. Over C it diagonalizes with eigenvalues ±i. Over R it does not.

Sufficient conditions for diagonalizability (no computation needed):

  • A has n distinct eigenvalues ⇒ automatically n independent eigenvectors
  • A is real symmetric (A=AT) ⇒ the Spectral Theorem guarantees real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis (see below)
  • A is normal (A^_A = AA^_) over C ⇒ unitarily diagonalizable

The Spectral Theorem (Symmetric Matrices)

The most important special case, and the one that comes up everywhere.

Theorem. If A∈Rn×n is symmetric (A=AT), then:

  1. All eigenvalues of A are real.
  2. Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  3. There is an orthonormal basis of Rn consisting entirely of eigenvectors of A.

In other words, A=QΛQT where Q is orthogonal (Q−1=QT):

A=QΛQT=∑i=1n​λi​qi​qiT​

The last form is the spectral decomposition: A is a sum of rank-1 projection matrices, each scaled by an eigenvalue. Each qi​qiT​ projects onto the i-th eigenspace. This is the cleanest possible picture.

Proof that eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Let Ax=λx and Ay=μy with λ=μ. Compute:

λ,xTy=(Ax)Ty=xTATy=xTAy=μ,xTy

So (λ−μ),xTy=0. Since λ=μ, we get xTy=0. □

The symmetry of A was used exactly once: AT=A in the middle step.

Powers of a Matrix

Diagonalization makes matrix powers effortless. From A=SΛS−1:

Ak=(SΛS−1)k=SΛkS−1

since the S−1S terms in the middle all cancel. And

Λk=(λ1k​​​ ​⋱​ ​​λnk​​)

So A100 costs no more work than A1, once you have the eigendecomposition. In contrast, brute-force matrix multiplication costs O(n3) per step — catastrophic for large k.

Application: Fibonacci numbers. The recurrence Fk+2​=Fk+1​+Fk​ is a linear recurrence. Write:

(Fk+1​ Fk​​)=A(1​1 1​0​)​​(Fk​ Fk−1​​)

So (Fk+1​ Fk​​)=Ak(1 0​).

The eigenvalues of A are λ1,2​=21±5​​ — the golden ratio ϕ and its conjugate ϕ^​=21−5​​. Since ∣ϕ^​∣<1, its contribution decays and Fk​∼5​ϕk​ for large k. The eigenvalues are the growth rate of the sequence.

The Matrix Exponential

For differential equations dtdu​=Au, the solution is u(t)=eAtu(0), where

eAt=∑k=0∞​k!(At)k​

With A=SΛS−1:

eAt=S,eΛt,S−1,eΛt=(eλ1​t​​ ​⋱​ ​​eλn​t​)

The eigenvalues of A directly control the behavior of solutions:

  • Re(λi​)<0 for all i: solution decays to 0 (stable equilibrium)
  • Re(λi​)>0 for some i: solution grows (instability)
  • Re(λi​)=0 for all i, A diagonalizable: oscillation, no growth

This is the entire stability theory of linear ODEs in one sentence.

Trace, Determinant, and Eigenvalues

Two invariants connect A and its eigenvalues without any computation of S:

tr(A)=∑i=1n​λi​,det(A)=∏i=1n​λi​

These follow from expanding the characteristic polynomial:

p(λ)=det(A−λI)=(−1)n(λn−(tr,A)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndetA)

The trace is the sum of diagonal entries (obviously), and also the sum of eigenvalues (less obviously). The determinant is the product of eigenvalues. In particular: A is singular iff at least one eigenvalue is zero. Good internal consistency check.

Similarity invariance. If B=M−1AM, then B and A have the same characteristic polynomial — hence the same eigenvalues, trace, and determinant. The eigenvalues are properties of the linear transformation, not of the particular matrix representing it.

What Diagonalization Is Not

Not all matrices are diagonalizable. As above: defective matrices (insufficient eigenvectors for a repeated eigenvalue) are not. Their canonical form is the Jordan normal form — block diagonal with Jordan blocks. The Jordan form exists for every matrix over C.

Not the same as SVD. The eigendecomposition A=SΛS−1 requires A to be square and uses the same basis S for domain and codomain. The SVD A=UΣVT works for any m×n matrix, uses different orthonormal bases U and V, and always exists. For symmetric positive definite matrices, S=Q=U=V and the two coincide. In general they are different.

Not always numerically stable. If S is nearly singular (nearly dependent eigenvectors), computing S−1 is ill-conditioned. In practice, eigenvalue algorithms (QR algorithm) do not form S−1 explicitly.

The Geometry in Two Dimensions

For a 2×2 diagonalizable matrix with real eigenvalues λ1​,λ2​ and eigenvectors x1​,x2​:

  • Any vector v=c1​x1​+c2​x2​ maps to Av=c1​λ1​x1​+c2​λ2​x2​
  • The eigenvector directions are the principal axes: they don’t rotate, only scale
  • If ∣λ1​∣>∣λ2​∣, repeated application of A collapses everything toward x1​ (the dominant eigenvector)
  • If λ1​>0>λ2​, the matrix is a saddle: stretching along x1​, compressing along x2​

This geometric picture extends to n dimensions — eigenvectors span the directions of pure scaling, everything else is a linear combination.

Connections to the Rest of Linear Algebra

Null space and zero eigenvalues. λ=0 is an eigenvalue iff Ax=0 has a nontrivial solution, i.e., A is singular. The zero eigenspace is the null space.

Rank. For a diagonalizable matrix, rank(A)= number of nonzero eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity).

Positive definiteness. A is positive definite iff all eigenvalues are positive (for symmetric A). The eigenvalues are the principal curvatures of the quadratic form xTAx.

Projections. A projection matrix P2=P has eigenvalues only 0 and 1. Eigenvalue 1: vectors already in the column space (fixed by projection). Eigenvalue 0: vectors in the null space (annihilated by projection). Diagonalizable with Λ=diag(1,…,1,0,…,0).

Orthogonal matrices. An orthogonal matrix Q has ∣λ∣=1 for all eigenvalues. Over C, the eigenvalues are on the unit circle. Real eigenvalues are ±1; complex ones come in pairs e±iθ (corresponding to rotation by θ in some 2D plane).

Things I Want to Come Back To

  • Jordan normal form — the full story of what happens when diagonalization fails; every matrix is similar to a Jordan matrix over C
  • Cayley-Hamilton theorem: p(A)=0 where p is the characteristic polynomial — every matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation; proof using Jordan form or direct computation
  • Schur decomposition: every matrix is unitarily similar to upper triangular over C (weaker than diagonalization, but always exists and is numerically friendly)
  • Generalized eigenvectors and the exponential of a Jordan block: eJt where J is a Jordan block has polynomial factors in t multiplying eλt
  • Simultaneously diagonalizable matrices: AB=BA iff A and B can be diagonalized in the same basis (assuming both are diagonalizable)

Summary

Diagonalization is the factorization A=SΛS−1, valid when A has n linearly independent eigenvectors. It turns a complicated matrix into a transparent one — in the eigenbasis, A is just multiplication by scalars. The symmetric case (A=QΛQT, orthonormal basis, real eigenvalues) is the cleanest version and underlies the spectral theorem, PCA, and the theory of quadratic forms. When diagonalization fails, Jordan form takes over. Powers, exponentials, stability, and projection all reduce to eigenvalue reading once the decomposition is in hand.

Written after Strang Ch. 6. The Fibonacci computation is one of Strang’s best examples — seeing the golden ratio fall out of the eigenvalues of a 2×2 integer matrix never gets old.
