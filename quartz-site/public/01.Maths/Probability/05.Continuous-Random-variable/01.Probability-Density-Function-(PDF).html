01.Probability Density Function (PDF)

01.Probability Density Function (PDF)

Apr 23, 20267 min read

The Core Problem

Discrete probability is clean: P(X=k) makes immediate sense. But once we move to continuous random variables — think a waiting time, a measurement, a temperature — we hit a wall.

The fundamental issue: For any continuous X, P(X=x)=0 for every single x.

This is not a technicality to wave away. It’s the geometric fact that a single point has measure zero on the real line. Probability lives in intervals, not points. So we need a new object entirely.

Definition

Let X be a continuous random variable. A function f:R→R is the probability density function (PDF) of X if:

  1. f(x)≥0 for all x∈R
  2. ∫−∞∞​f(x),dx=1
  3. For any a≤b:

P(a≤X≤b)=∫ab​f(x),dx

That’s it. The PDF is just a non-negative function whose integral over all of R is 1, and which gives probabilities via integration.

Key insight: f(x) is not a probability. f(x) can exceed 1. It’s a density — probability per unit length. Probability only emerges when you integrate over a set.

The CDF Connection — and How to Move Between Them

This is the most mechanically important relationship in all of continuous probability. You need to be completely fluent in both directions.

Definition of the CDF

F(x)=P(X≤x)=∫−∞x​f(t),dt

The CDF accumulates area under the density curve from −∞ up to x. It is always:

  • Non-decreasing
  • Right-continuous
  • limx→−∞​F(x)=0 and limx→∞​F(x)=1

Direction 1: PDF → CDF (integrate)

Given f, integrate from −∞ to x:

F(x)=∫−∞x​f(t),dt

Worked example — Exponential(λ):

f(x)=λe−λx,x≥0

F(x)=∫0x​λe−λt,dt=[−e−λt]0x​=1−e−λx,x≥0

Check: F(0)=0 ✓, F(∞)=1 ✓, F non-decreasing ✓.

The piecewise trap. If f has a restricted support (i.e., f=0 outside some interval), you must handle F piecewise. For X∼Unif(a,b):

F(x)={0​x<a b−ax−a​​a≤x≤b 1​x>b​

Don’t forget the outer cases. This is where exam errors creep in.

Direction 2: CDF → PDF (differentiate)

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, wherever F is differentiable:

f(x)=F′(x)

This is the cleaner direction mechanically — just differentiate. But you need to check:

  1. F′(x)≥0 everywhere (it should be, if F is non-decreasing — a nice sanity check)
  2. ∫F′(x),dx=1 (guaranteed if F goes from 0 to 1)

Worked example. Suppose you’re handed:

F(x)={0​x<0 1−e−x2​x≥0​

Differentiate on x>0:

f(x)=F′(x)=2xe−x2,x>0

And f(x)=0 for x<0. Check: f(x)≥0 ✓. Verify normalization:

∫0∞​2xe−x2,dx=[−e−x2]0∞​=0−(−1)=1✓

What if F isn’t differentiable everywhere? At isolated jump discontinuities of f (like the boundary of a Uniform’s support), F has a kink — it’s continuous but not differentiable there. The PDF is undefined at those exact points, but since they’re measure-zero sets, it doesn’t matter for any integral computation.

The last two are equivalent — use whichever is computationally easier in the moment.

A Common Exam Move: Finding P(a≤X≤b) via the CDF

Once you have F, you never need to integrate again:

P(a≤X≤b)=F(b)−F(a)

This is just the definition of the CDF applied twice, using additivity of integrals. It’s often faster than re-integrating f from scratch.

Example. X∼Expo(2). Find P(1≤X≤3).

F(x)=1−e−2x

P(1≤X≤3)=F(3)−F(1)=(1−e−6)−(1−e−2)=e−2−e−6

Done in two lines, no integration required.

Why f(x) Can Be Greater Than 1

This trips people up. Consider X∼Uniform(0,0.1). Then:

f(x)={10​0≤x≤0.1 0​otherwise​

Here f(x)=10≫1. But check: ∫00.1​10,dx=1. ✓

The density being large just means probability is concentrated in that region. The value f(x) is better read as: “in a tiny window [x,x+dx], the probability is approximately f(x),dx.”

Expectation and Variance

For X with density f:

E[X]=∫−∞∞​x⋅f(x),dx

E[g(X)]=∫−∞∞​g(x)⋅f(x),dx(LOTUS)

Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2

LOTUS (Law of the Unconscious Statistician) is particularly powerful — you don’t need to find the distribution of g(X) first.

The Two Workhorses: Uniform and Exponential

Uniform Distribution: X∼Unif(a,b)

f(x)=b−a1​,a≤x≤b

E[X]=2a+b​,Var(X)=12(b−a)2​

The uniform is the “maximum ignorance” distribution on a bounded interval. Everything is equally likely per unit length.

Exponential Distribution: X∼Expo(λ)

f(x)=λe−λx,x≥0

E[X]=λ1​,Var(X)=λ21​

The exponential is the continuous analogue of the Geometric. It models waiting times, lifetimes, inter-arrival times. Its defining property is memorylessness:

P(X>s+t∣X>s)=P(X>t)

Proof. Direct calculation using P(X>t)=e−λt.

Normal Distribution: X∼N(μ,σ2)

f(x)=σ2π​1​exp!(−2σ2(x−μ)2​)

The Gaussian is everywhere because of the Central Limit Theorem — sums of i.i.d. random variables with finite variance converge in distribution to a normal. The PDF itself doesn’t have a closed-form antiderivative (hence the Φ function and z-tables), but its moment-generating function is beautifully tractable:

MX​(t)=eμt+σ2t2/2

Standardization: if X∼N(μ,σ2), then Z=σX−μ​∼N(0,1).

A Proof Worth Sitting With

Claim: The Exponential is the unique memoryless continuous distribution on [0,∞).

Proof sketch. Let X have CDF F with F(0)=0 and the memoryless property: P(X>s+t)=P(X>s)⋅P(X>t) for all s,t≥0.

Let G(t)=P(X>t)=1−F(t). The condition becomes: G(s+t)=G(s)⋅G(t)

This is Cauchy’s functional equation. The only right-continuous solutions with G(0)=1 and G decreasing to 0 are G(t)=e−λt for some λ>0. ✓

This is the right way to understand the exponential — not as an arbitrary formula, but as the inevitable consequence of memorylessness. The Poisson process makes this even clearer.

Pitfalls and Things to Remember

  • f(x)≥0 everywhere, not just on the “support.” On regions outside the support, f=0.
  • Check normalization. Every time you write down a PDF, verify ∫f=1.
  • Endpoints don’t matter. P(a≤X≤b)=P(a<X<b) for continuous RVs. This is often useful.
  • Density vs. Mass. If X is continuous, never write P(X=x0​) in your calculations as though it contributes anything.
