10. 68 95 99.7 Rule (Empirical Rule)

10. 68-95-99.7 Rule (Empirical Rule)

Mar 28, 20265 min read

This is one of those facts you memorize in week one and spend the rest of your degree actually understanding. Come back to this after MGFs, after CLT, after you’ve actually derived the normal PDF from scratch. It hits differently.

The Statement

Let X∼N(μ,σ2). Then:

P(μ−σ≤X≤μ+σ)≈0.6827 P(μ−2σ≤X≤μ+2σ)≈0.9545 P(μ−3σ≤X≤μ+3σ)≈0.9973

Or in the standardized form — let Z=σX−μ​∼N(0,1):

P(∣Z∣≤1)≈0.6827P(∣Z∣≤2)≈0.9545P(∣Z∣≤3)≈0.9973

These aren’t definitions. They are computed facts about the normal distribution that follow from the shape of the Gaussian PDF.

Where Does This Actually Come From?

The normal PDF is:

f(x)=2π​,σ1​exp!(−2σ2(x−μ)2​)

The rule is just evaluating:

P(μ−kσ≤X≤μ+kσ)=∫μ−kσμ+kσ​2π​,σ1​e−2σ2(x−μ)2​,dx

Standardise with z=σx−μ​, dz=σdx​:

=∫−kk​2π​1​e−z2/2,dz=2,Φ(k)−1

where Φ is the standard normal CDF. Now plug in:

This integral has no closed form — Φ is not elementary. That used to bother me. Now I think it’s beautiful: the most important distribution in all of probability has a CDF you can’t write in closed form. You have to respect it.

The Proof That ∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx=2π​

This is the normalization constant and it’s worth knowing cold. The trick is squaring the integral.

Claim: I=∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx=2π​

Proof.

I2=(∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx)!!(∫−∞∞​e−y2/2,dy)=∬R2​e−(x2+y2)/2,dx,dy

Convert to polar coordinates: x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, dx,dy=r,dr,dθ:

I2=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2/2⋅r,dr,dθ=2π∫0∞​r,e−r2/2,dr

Substitute u=r2/2, du=r,dr:

=2π∫0∞​e−u,du=2π⋅1=2π

So I=2π​. ■

Every time I see this proof I’m briefly annoyed that the solution is “square it and use polar coordinates” — and then immediately grateful. It’s one of the great tricks in analysis.

Symmetry Makes It Cheap

Because the standard normal PDF ϕ(z)=2π​1​e−z2/2 is even — i.e., ϕ(−z)=ϕ(z) — we get:

P(∣Z∣≤k)=2,Φ(k)−1

This symmetry isn’t accidental. It reflects the fact that the normal is determined entirely by μ and σ2, and the density depends only on (x−μ)2.

Concretely, this means:

P(Z>1)=P(Z<−1)=21−0.6827​≈0.1587

The tail probability at 1σ is about 15.87 on each side. At 3σ, each tail is only 0.135 — this is the origin of the ”3σ event” language in physics and finance.

Why This Rule Matters Beyond Memorization

1. It Quantifies Rarity

A ”5σ event” (famously invoked in the Higgs boson discovery) corresponds to: P(∣Z∣>5)≈5.7×10−7 That’s about 1 in 1.7 million. The rule gives you the intuition for what sigma-multiples mean in terms of probability mass.

2. It’s the Practical Output of the CLT

The 07.The Central Limit Theorem says Xˉn​≈N(μ,σ2/n) for large n. The 68-95-99.7 rule immediately tells you:

  • 95% confidence intervals are roughly Xˉ±2⋅n​σ​
  • This is where the “2 standard errors” rule of thumb in statistics comes from.

3. Chebyshev vs. Empirical Rule

Chebyshev’s Inequality gives a distribution-free bound: P(∣X−μ∣≥kσ)≤k21​

At k=2: Chebyshev says at most 25 of mass is outside 2σ. The empirical rule says only $4.55% for the normal. The normal is much better concentrated than Chebyshev guarantees — Chebyshev is tight for pathological distributions, not the bell curve.

I used to confuse these two. They answer the same question but from totally different angles. Chebyshev: any distribution, weak bound. Empirical rule: normal only, sharp fact.

The Tail Behavior — Exponential Decay

For z large, the standard normal tail decays like:

P(Z>z)≈zϕ(z)​=z2π​1​e−z2/2

This is a sub-Gaussian tail — faster than any polynomial, but not as fast as a compactly supported distribution. This decay rate is what makes the normal so well-behaved in probability theory and why tools like MGFs and Hoeffding’s inequality work cleanly for it.

Personal Notes

  • The numbers 68,95,99.7 are worth having in working memory — you’ll use them constantly in applied settings, hypothesis testing, and when reading papers.
  • Don’t just memorize the percentages. Know that they come from 2Φ(k)−1 and know why the factor of 2 is there (symmetry of ϕ).
  • The hardest part conceptually: the rule is only exactly true for the normal. For any other distribution, you need Chebyshev or distribution-specific bounds. The empirical rule is a normal-specific fact, not a universal law.
  • Joe Blitzstein in Stat 110 emphasizes the story behind distributions — the empirical rule is part of the normal’s “story”: it’s the canonical unimodal symmetric distribution, and these percentages are the fingerprint of that shape.
