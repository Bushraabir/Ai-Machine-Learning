16.Binomial Convergence

16.Binomial Convergence

Mar 28, 20268 min read

The Binomial sits at the centre of a web of limits. Push p small and n large — you get Poisson. Push n large with p fixed — you get Normal. These are not just approximations; they are structural theorems about what the Binomial becomes under different asymptotic regimes. Understanding which limit applies when is one of the most practically important skills in probability.

Setup

X∼Bin(n,p). Two parameters, two independent limiting regimes:

Regime 1: n→∞,;p=nλ​→0,;np=λ fixed⟹Pois(λ)

Regime 2: n→∞,;p fixed,;np=μ,;np(1−p)=σ2⟹N(μ,σ2)

These are genuinely different limits. They live in different parts of the (n,p) parameter space and require different proof techniques. Getting them confused is a real mistake.

Limit 1: Binomial → Poisson

(Full proof in 12.Poisson vs Binomial and 09.Poisson Distribution. Reproduced here for completeness.)

Theorem. Let Xn​∼Bin(n,λ/n) for fixed λ>0. Then for each k≥0:

P(Xn​=k)→k!e−λλk​as n→∞

i.e., Xn​d​Pois(λ).

Proof via MGFs:

MXn​​(t)=(1−nλ​+nλ​et)n=(1+nλ(et−1)​)n→eλ(et−1)

which is the Pois(λ) MGF. □

When to use: n large, p small, np moderate. The regime of rare events.

Error: dTV​(Bin(n,p),Pois(np))≤2np2=2λp (Le Cam).

Limit 2: Binomial → Normal (The CLT)

Theorem (De Moivre–Laplace, 1733/1812). Let Xn​∼Bin(n,p) with p∈(0,1) fixed. Then:

np(1−p)​Xn​−np​d​N(0,1)

This is the oldest instance of the Central Limit Theorem — proved for the Binomial by De Moivre (1733) a century before Laplace gave the general version.

Proof via MGFs:

Let Yi​=1Ai​​ be i.i.d. Bernoulli(p), so Xn​=∑i=1n​Yi​. Standardise:

Zn​=np(1−p)​Xn​−np​=n​⋅p(1−p)​∑i=1n​(Yi​−p)​

Let Wi​=(Yi​−p)/p(1−p)​, so E[Wi​]=0, Var(Wi​)=1, and Zn​=n​1​∑Wi​.

The MGF of Wi​:

MWi​​(t)=E[etWi​]=pet(1−p)/p(1−p)​+(1−p)e−tp/p(1−p)​

Expand to second order using ex=1+x+x2/2+O(x3):

MWi​​(t)=1+0⋅t+2t2​⋅1+O(t3/n​)=1+2t2​+O!(n​t3​)

(The O(t3/n​) term arises because Wi​ has bounded third moment, so the remainder is controlled.)

Then:

MZn​​(t)=[MWi​​!(n​t​)]n=[1+2nt2​+O!(n3/2t3​)]n→et2/2

using (1+xn​/n)n→ex when xn​→x. The limit et2/2 is the MGF of N(0,1). □

The MGF proof is clean but hides the geometric intuition. De Moivre’s original proof used Stirling’s approximation directly on the Binomial PMF — much more painful but illuminating. The Stirling proof is in the to-do list.

The Two Regimes, Side by Side

Let me be precise about where each approximation lives.

Bin(n,p)≈{Pois(np)​if p→0,;n→∞,;np=O(1) N(np,;np(1−p))​if p fixed,;n→∞​

The crossover between regimes is roughly p∼1/n​:

  • If p≪1/n​: Poisson regime. np→0 or stays bounded.
  • If p≫1/n​: Normal regime. np→∞ and the CLT kicks in.
  • If p∼1/n​: both approximations are rough; need exact Binomial or a finer analysis.

I find it helpful to think of the (n,p) parameter space as a plane, with the Poisson regime near the p=0 axis and the Normal regime in the bulk. The transition zone is where neither approximation is great.

Why the Two Limits Are Different Objects

This is the conceptually important point, and it confused me for a while.

The Poisson limit is a limit of the PMF — a discrete distribution converges to another discrete distribution. The support stays 0,1,2,….

The Normal limit is a limit of a standardised random variable — the distribution of Xn​ itself does not converge (its mean np→∞). Only after subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation does the limit exist, and it is a continuous distribution.

These are different modes of convergence being used for different purposes:

Poisson LimitNormal Limit
What convergesP(Xn​=k) pointwiseP(Zn​≤z) for all z
Standardisation needed?NoYes — must centre and scale
Limit distributionDiscrete: Pois(λ)Continuous: N(0,1)
What’s fixedλ=npp (and hence μ=np→∞)
Mode of convergencePointwise PMF convergenceConvergence in distribution (CDF)

The Normal limit throws away the discreteness of the Binomial entirely. The Poisson limit preserves it. This is not a flaw — it reflects the fact that in the Normal regime (p fixed, n large), Xn​ takes n+1 values spread over an interval of width ∼n​, and the spacing 1/n​ between consecutive values becomes negligible. The discreteness washes out.

Continuity Correction

Because the Binomial is discrete and the Normal is continuous, naively approximating P(X≤k) by Φ((k−np)/np(1−p)​) underuses the fact that X only takes integer values.

The continuity correction fixes this:

P(X≤k)≈Φ!(np(1−p)​k+1/2−np​)

P(X=k)≈Φ!(np(1−p)​k+1/2−np​)−Φ!(np(1−p)​k−1/2−np​)

The +1/2 accounts for the fact that the integer k corresponds to the interval [k−1/2,k+1/2) on the real line.

Example. X∼Bin(100,0.5). Compute P(X≤55).

Without correction: Φ!(555−50​)=Φ(1)≈0.8413.

With correction: Φ!(555.5−50​)=Φ(1.1)≈0.8643.

Exact: P(X≤55)≈0.8644. The continuity correction is dramatically better.

I used to think the continuity correction was a pedantic adjustment. It’s not — it can change the third significant figure. For hypothesis testing, that matters. Always use it when approximating discrete distributions with the Normal.

The Berry-Esseen Theorem: Quantifying the Normal Approximation

The CLT says Zn​d​N(0,1) but says nothing about the rate. The Berry-Esseen theorem fixes this.

Theorem (Berry-Esseen). Let X=∑i=1n​Yi​ with Yi​ i.i.d., E[Yi​]=0, Var(Yi​)=σ2, E[∣Yi​∣3]=ρ<∞. Then:

supz​​P!(σn​X​≤z)−Φ(z)​≤σ3n​Cρ​

where C≤0.4748 (best known constant).

For the Binomial, Yi​=1Ai​​−p, so σ2=p(1−p) and ρ=E[∣Yi​∣3]≤1.

supz​∣P(Zn​≤z)−Φ(z)∣≤(p(1−p))3/2n​C​≤np(1−p)​0.4748​

So the error in the Normal approximation is O(1/n​), uniform over all z.

Practical implication: The approximation is good to O(1/n​). For n=100, p=0.5: error ≤0.4748/25​≈0.095. Not tiny. For n=10000: error ≤0.0047. Very good.

Berry-Esseen is the rigorous justification for the “rule of thumb” np≥5 and n(1−p)≥5 — it ensures np(1−p)​ is large enough that the 1/n​ error is acceptably small. The rule of thumb is conservative; Berry-Esseen tells you exactly how conservative.

The Local Limit Theorem

The CLT (Berry-Esseen) is about the CDF — the error in P(Zn​≤z). The local limit theorem is about the PMF directly.

Theorem (Local CLT for Binomial). For X∼Bin(n,p) and k in the bulk of the distribution:

P(X=k)=2πnp(1−p)​1​exp!(−2np(1−p)(k−np)2​)⋅(1+O(n−1/2))

This is proved using Stirling’s approximation:

n!≈2πn​,nne−n

applied to (kn​)=n!/(k!(n−k)!). The resulting algebra is the calculation De Moivre originally did.

The local CLT is stronger than the global CLT — it approximates individual probabilities, not just cumulative ones.

Why This All Matters

The Binomial has two faces:

  • The rare-event face: when p is small, it counts rare successes. The Poisson captures this.
  • The averaging face: when n is large and p is fixed, it is a sum of many i.i.d. terms. The Normal captures this.

The De Moivre-Laplace theorem is historically the first CLT — every subsequent CLT (for general i.i.d. sums, for dependent variables, for martingales) is a generalisation of the statement proved here for the Binomial. Understanding this case deeply is understanding the source of the entire CLT family.

Every time I prove the CLT for the Binomial, I feel like I’m watching probability being invented in real time. De Moivre proved this in 1733 to solve gambling problems. He had no idea he was proving the most important theorem in statistics.

The Full Picture

Bin(n,p)p→0,,np→λ​Pois(λ)?​Normal?

Does the Poisson itself converge to Normal? Yes — as λ→∞:

λ​Pois(λ)−λ​d​N(0,1)

This follows from the CLT applied to the decomposition Pois(λ)=∑i=1n​Pois(λ/n) (additivity). So:

Bin(n,p)⟶Pois(λ)⟶N(λ,λ)

The Binomial, Poisson, and Normal form a hierarchy of limits. The Poisson is an intermediate object between the finite Binomial and the continuous Normal.
