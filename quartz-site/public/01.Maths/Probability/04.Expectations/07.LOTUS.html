07.LOTUS

Mar 28, 20266 min read

— the theorem that says you never need to find the distribution of g(X) to compute E[g(X)], and whose proof is so clean it should feel obvious in retrospect_

The Problem LOTUS Solves

You know the distribution of X. You want E[g(X)] for some function g.

The naive approach: find the distribution of Y=g(X), then compute E[Y]=∑y​y,P(Y=y).

This works but is often painful. Finding the distribution of g(X) requires inverting g, grouping preimages, and recomputing an entire PMF or PDF. For something like E[X2] or E[eX], this is unnecessary suffering.

LOTUS says: don’t bother finding the distribution of g(X). Just use the distribution of X directly.

The Theorem

Theorem (LOTUS — Discrete). Let X be a discrete random variable with PMF pX​, and g:R→R any function. Then:

E[g(X)]=∑x​g(x),pX​(x)

Theorem (LOTUS — Continuous). Let X be a continuous random variable with PDF fX​. Then:

E[g(X)]=∫−∞∞​g(x),fX​(x),dx

In both cases: sum or integrate g(x) against the distribution of X, not the distribution of g(X).

The Proof — Discrete Case

Let Y=g(X). By definition:

E[Y]=∑y​y,P(Y=y)

Now expand P(Y=y)=P(g(X)=y)=∑x:,g(x)=y​P(X=x):

E[Y]=∑y​y∑x:,g(x)=y​pX​(x)

Since y=g(x) for all x in the inner sum, substitute:

=∑y​∑x:,g(x)=y​g(x),pX​(x)

The double sum ∑y​∑x:,g(x)=y​ ranges over all x exactly once — every x belongs to exactly one group x:g(x)=y for y=g(x). So:

=∑x​g(x),pX​(x)□

The key step: regrouping. The sum over y and then over preimages of y is just a reindexing of the sum over all x. Every x appears exactly once. The distribution of Y was an intermediate scaffolding that collapses away.

The proof works for any g — injective, many-to-one, non-monotone, discontinuous. The preimage grouping handles everything. g doesn’t need any regularity at all.

The Continuous Case — Why It’s Not Obvious

For continuous X, the proof requires more care because P(X=x)=0 for every x — you can’t just sum over preimages.

The rigorous proof uses measure theory: expectation is the Lebesgue integral E[g(X)]=∫g,dPX​, where PX​ is the pushforward measure. The LOTUS formula is the change-of-variables theorem for Lebesgue integrals.

Without measure theory, the clean argument: let Y=g(X) with PDF fY​. Then by definition E[Y]=∫yfY​(y),dy. Using the change-of-variables formula for PDFs (valid when g is smooth and invertible on pieces):

∫y,fY​(y),dy=∫g(x),fX​(x),dx

This requires justification for general g, but the formula is what it is. For the purposes of Stat 110 and almost all applications: accept LOTUS for continuous RVs and know the discrete proof cold.

LOTUS for Two Variables

For jointly distributed (X,Y):

E[g(X,Y)]=∑x​∑y​g(x,y),pX,Y​(x,y)(discrete)

E[g(X,Y)]=∫∫g(x,y),fX,Y​(x,y),dx,dy(continuous)

Again: use the joint distribution of (X,Y), not the distribution of g(X,Y). The same grouping argument works.

Why It’s Called “Unconscious”

The name is a joke at the statistician’s expense. An “unconscious” statistician — one not paying attention — might just write:

E[g(X)]=∑x​g(x),pX​(x)

without thinking about whether this is valid, without deriving the distribution of g(X) first. The theorem says: that instinct is correct. The unconscious statistician was right all along.

More precisely: the instinct to substitute g(x) into the expectation formula and weight by pX​(x) is the correct procedure, even though it looks like you’re computing the expectation of g(X) as if it were the expectation of X but with g(x) in place of x. That substitution is exactly what LOTUS licenses.

The Applications — Where LOTUS Actually Lives

LOTUS is most useful when g is nonlinear and the distribution of g(X) is messy or unknown. Here are the canonical uses:

Computing E[X2] — The Gateway to Variance

For any X with known PMF or PDF:

E[X2]=∑x​x2,pX​(x)

This is LOTUS with g(x)=x2. The distribution of X2 is never needed.

Then variance falls out:

Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2

Without LOTUS, variance would require finding the distribution of (X−μ)2, which is miserable. With LOTUS, it’s a sum/integral you already know how to set up.

X∼Bernoulli(p)

E[X2]=12⋅p+02⋅(1−p)=p

Var(X)=p−p2=p(1−p)

X∼Poisson(λ)

E[X2]=∑k=0∞​k2k!e−λλk​

Write k2=k(k−1)+k, so E[X2]=E[X(X−1)]+E[X].

E[X(X−1)]=∑k=2∞​k(k−1)k!e−λλk​=e−λλ2∑k=2∞​(k−2)!λk−2​=λ2

So E[X2]=λ2+λ, and:

Var(X)=λ2+λ−λ2=λ

The Poisson has equal mean and variance. Both equal λ. This is a characterizing property of the Poisson distribution.

X∼Uniform(0,1), PDF f(x)=1 on [0,1]

E[X2]=∫01​x2⋅1,dx=31​

E[X]=21​,Var(X)=31​−41​=121​

Moment Generating Functions — LOTUS as Infrastructure

The moment generating function of X:

MX​(t)=E[etX]=∑x​etx,pX​(x)(discrete)

This is LOTUS with g(x)=etx. The entire theory of MGFs rests on LOTUS. Without it, E[etX] would require the distribution of etX, which is both unnatural and painful.

LOTUS vs Linearity — What Each Covers

SituationToolRequires
E[aX+b]LinearityNothing
E[X+Y]LinearityNothing
E[g(X)] for nonlinear gLOTUSDistribution of X
E[g(X,Y)]LOTUS (joint)Joint distribution of (X,Y)
E[g(X)h(Y)] with X⊥YLOTUS + independenceIndependence

Linearity handles sums. LOTUS handles everything else. They are complementary tools, not competing ones.

Note: E[g(X)h(Y)]=E[g(X)],E[h(Y)] when X⊥Y — this is LOTUS applied to the joint distribution, then factoring using independence. Independence is what allows the joint LOTUS integral to split into a product of single integrals.

The One Thing LOTUS Cannot Fix

LOTUS computes E[g(X)] easily. But:

E[g(X)]=g(E[X])for nonlinear g

LOTUS tells you how to compute the left side. Jensen’s inequality tells you the relationship between the two sides. LOTUS and Jensen are complementary: LOTUS is a computational tool, Jensen is a comparison theorem.

The unconscious statistician who writes E[g(X)]=g(E[X]) is wrong — that’s not LOTUS, that’s Jensen’s inequality forgotten. LOTUS says use pX​, not that you can pull E through g.

The thing to carry: LOTUS is a license. It says you are allowed to write ∑x​g(x)pX​(x) and call it E[g(X)] without going through the distribution of g(X). The proof is just reindexing a sum. The unconscious statistician who did this without thinking was accidentally right. Now you know why — and you can do it on purpose.
