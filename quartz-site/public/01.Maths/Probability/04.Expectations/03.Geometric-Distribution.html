03.Geometric Distribution

03.Geometric Distribution

Apr 03, 202611 min read

Big Picture You are flipping a biased coin, over and over. Each flip is independent. p∈(0,1) is the probability of success on any single flip. You stop the moment you get your first success.

How long do you wait?

That waiting time is exactly the Geometric(p) random variable. Simple to state — but the moment you pull on the thread, the memoryless property, the connection to exponential distributions, and the entire structure of waiting-time distributions starts showing up.

1. The Two Conventions — Choose One and Commit

Two Conventions Exist Type 1 (Blitzstein / Stat 110): X = number of failures before the first success. Support: 0,1,2,…

Type 2 (many textbooks): X = number of trials until (and including) the first success. Support: 1,2,3,…

The two differ by exactly 1: if X is Type 1 and Y is Type 2, then Y=X+1.

This note uses Type 1 throughout — it makes the algebra cleaner, and it’s the convention in Stat 110, which is the primary source.

X∼Geom(p)

X counts the number of failures before the first success, in a sequence of independent Bernoulli(p) trials.

2. PMF — Derivation from First Principles

The logic

Suppose X=k. This means:

  • Trials 1,2,…,k are all failures — each with probability 1−p
  • Trial k+1 is a success — with probability p
  • By independence, multiply

P(X=k)=k failures(1−p)(1−p)⋯(1−p)​​⋅successp​​

P(X=k)=(1−p)k⋅p,k=0,1,2,…​

Let q:=1−p for cleaner notation. Then P(X=k)=qkp.

Verification — sum to 1

∑k=0∞​qkp=p∑k=0∞​qk=p⋅1−q1​=p⋅p1​=1✓

using the geometric series ∑k=0∞​rk=1−r1​ for ∣r∣<1.

Why "Geometric"? The PMF values p, qp, q2p, q3p,… form a geometric sequence — each term is a constant multiple q of the previous. The distribution is named after the series that validates it. It's the same word for the same reason.

Shape of the PMF

The PMF is strictly decreasing in k for all p∈(0,1):

P(X=k)P(X=k+1)​=qkpqk+1p​=q<1

The mode is always at k=0. The most likely outcome is zero failures — you succeed on the very first trial. As p→0 (rare success), the distribution flattens and spreads out; as p→1 (near-certain success), the distribution collapses onto k=0.

3. CDF — Complement Method

Direct sum (painful)

P(X≤k)=∑j=0k​qjp=p⋅1−q1−qk+1​=1−qk+1

Complement method (elegant — burn this reflex in)

Instead of summing successes up to k, compute the complement:

P(X>k)=P(first k+1 trials are all failures)=qk+1

The first k+1 trials are all failures with probability qk+1, by independence.

Therefore:

P(X≤k)=1−qk+1=1−(1−p)k+1,k=0,1,2,…​

Complement Reflex For geometric and exponential-type calculations, always try the complement first. P(X>k) has a direct physical interpretation (all trials fail) that P(X≤k) does not. The complement route avoids summing a geometric series by reading the answer directly from the physics.

CDF staircase

The CDF jumps at each integer k≥0, with jump size P(X=k)=qkp:

F(k)
  1  |                              ●────
     |                    ●────────○
     |          ●────────○
 1-q |●────────○
     |
  0  ●
     ─────────────────────────────────── k
     0         1         2         3

Each step is smaller than the last (by factor q), consistent with the decreasing PMF.

4. Expectation — Three Derivations

Derivation 1: Geometric series differentiation (the calculus route)

E[X]=∑k=0∞​k⋅qkp=p∑k=0∞​kqk

We need ∑k=0∞​kqk. Start from the geometric series:

∑k=0∞​qk=1−q1​

Differentiate both sides with respect to q:

∑k=0∞​kqk−1=(1−q)21​

Multiply both sides by q:

∑k=0∞​kqk=(1−q)2q​=p2q​

Therefore:

E[X]=p⋅p2q​=pq​

E[X]=p1−p​​

Derivation 2: Story proof — memorylessness (the Stat 110 route)

No calculus. One equation.

On the first trial, two things can happen:

  • Success (probability p): X=0 failures. We’re done immediately.
  • Failure (probability q): we’ve accumulated 1 failure. By the memoryless property (proven in §6), the remaining wait is distributed as a fresh Geom(p).

This gives the recursion:

E[X]=p⋅0+q⋅(1+E[X])

E[X]=q+q,E[X]

EX=q

E[X]=pq​✓

Why This Proof is Profound It uses no calculus, no summation formulas — only the physical structure of the process and memorylessness. This is a story proof: the equation mirrors what actually happens on the first trial. The algebra is just bookkeeping. This style of reasoning recurs constantly in Stat 110 and should be your first instinct.

Derivation 3: Tail sum formula

For any non-negative integer-valued RV: E[X]=∑k=0∞​P(X>k).

E[X]=∑k=0∞​P(X>k)=∑k=0∞​qk+1=q∑k=0∞​qk=q⋅1−q1​=pq​✓

Three derivations, one answer. Each illuminates a different tool.

Intuition

pE[X]=q/pInterpretation
10Success guaranteed — no failures expected
1/21Expect 1 failure before first success
1/109Rare success — expect 9 failures first
1/nn−1Very rare — expect roughly n trials total

For small p: E[X]=(1−p)/p≈1/p. Waiting time scales as 1/p — the rarer the event, the longer you wait.

5. Variance — Full Derivation

Step 1: Compute E[X(X−1)] (factorial moment)

The factorial moment avoids the clutter of E[X2] directly:

E[X(X−1)]=p∑k=0∞​k(k−1)qk

The k=0 and k=1 terms vanish. For k≥2, differentiate the geometric series twice.

Start from ∑qk=1−q1​.

First derivative: ∑k=1∞​kqk−1=(1−q)21​

Second derivative: ∑k=2∞​k(k−1)qk−2=(1−q)32​

Multiply by q2:

∑k=2∞​k(k−1)qk=(1−q)32q2​=p32q2​

Therefore:

E[X(X−1)]=p⋅p32q2​=p22q2​

Step 2: Recover E[X2]

E[X2]=E[X(X−1)]+E[X]=p22q2​+pq​

Step 3: Apply variance formula

Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2=p22q2​+pq​−p2q2​

=p2q2​+pq​=p2q2+qp​=p2q(q+p)​=p2q⋅1​

Var(X)=p21−p​​

Intuition

Var(X)=p2q​=pq​⋅p1​=E[X]⋅p1​

The variance is the mean multiplied by 1/p. Since 1/p≥1 always, the variance always exceeds the mean — the geometric distribution is overdispersed relative to the Poisson (where mean = variance). For small p, the variance scales as 1/p2 while the mean scales as 1/p — the distribution becomes increasingly spread out relative to its mean.

6. The Memoryless Property — The Theorem That Makes Geometric Special

Statement

If X∼Geom(p), then for all m,n≥0:

P(X≥m+n∣X≥m)=P(X≥n)​

The conditional distribution of additional failures, given that m failures have already occurred, is still Geom(p).

Proof

P(X≥m+n∣X≥m)=P(X≥m)P(X≥m+n and X≥m)​=P(X≥m)P(X≥m+n)​

Using P(X≥k)=qk:

=qmqm+n​=qn=P(X≥n)✓

What it means, physically

“Given that I’ve already waited through m failures, the probability distribution of how much longer I still have to wait is the same as if I hadn’t waited at all.”

The coin has no memory. Past failures are irrelevant to future outcomes. The process resets after every failure.

The characterisation theorem

The converse is equally important:

Theorem. If X takes values in 0,1,2,… and satisfies P(X≥m+n∣X≥m)=P(X≥n) for all m,n≥0, then X∼Geom(p) for some p.

The geometric is the unique discrete memoryless distribution.

Proof sketch. Let f(n)=P(X≥n). The memoryless condition gives:

f(m+n)=f(m)⋅f(n)for all m,n≥0

This is Cauchy’s functional equation on the integers. The only solutions with f(0)=1, f non-increasing, are f(n)=qn for some q∈[0,1]. This means P(X≥n)=qn, which gives P(X=k)=qk−qk+1=qk(1−q)=qkp. This is Geom(p). ■

Gambler's Fallacy The memoryless property is the mathematical proof that the gambler's fallacy is a fallacy.

After 10 consecutive tails, the next flip is still heads with probability p. The sequence of 10 tails changes nothing about the future. The coin does not “owe” you a head. The universe does not balance.

Mathematically: P(head on flip 11∣10 tails in a row)=p.

Don’t anthropomorphise the coin.

7. MGF — Full Derivation

MX​(t)=E[etX]=∑k=0∞​etk⋅qkp=p∑k=0∞​(qet)k

This is a geometric series in qet, valid when qet<1, i.e., t<−lnq=−ln(1−p):

MX​(t)=1−(1−p)etp​,t<−ln(1−p)​

Recover E[X] from MGF

MX′​(t)=[1−(1−p)et]2p⋅(1−p)et​

At t=0:

MX′​(0)=(1−q)2p⋅q​=p2pq​=pq​=E[X]✓

Recover E[X2] from MGF

Differentiate again (using the quotient rule):

MX′′​(0)=p2q(1+q)​

Var(X)=MX′′​(0)−[MX′​(0)]2=p2q(1+q)​−p2q2​=p2q+q2−q2​=p2q​✓

8. Geometric as Exponential Family

Write the PMF:

P(X=k)=qkp=p⋅exp(klogq)

Matching to h(x)exp(ηT(x)−A(θ)):

ComponentValueMeaning
h(k)1No base measure needed
η(p)log(1−p)=logqNatural parameter
T(k)kSufficient statistic — the count of failures
A(p)−logpLog-partition: logp1​

Verify: h(k)exp(ηT(k)−A(p))=1⋅exp(klogq+logp)=p⋅qk ✓

The sufficient statistic is T(k)=k — the total number of failures is all you need to estimate p. You don’t need to know the positions of the failures, only how many there were.

9. Connection to Negative Binomial

The Geometric is the r=1 special case of the Negative Binomial.

If we ask “how many failures before the r-th success?”, the answer is NegBin(r,p).

Geom(p)=NegBin(1,p)

More importantly: if X1​,…,Xr​∼iidGeom(p), then:

X1​+X2​+⋯+Xr​∼NegBin(r,p)

Proof via MGFs:

MX1​+⋯+Xr​​(t)=(1−qetp​)r

This is the MGF of NegBin(r,p). The Negative Binomial is to the Geometric what the Binomial is to the Bernoulli — the sum of r independent copies.

10. Connection to Exponential Distribution

The Geometric is the discrete analogue of the Exponential distribution. Both share the memoryless property — one on 0,1,2,…, the other on [0,∞).

The limiting argument

Let each trial take time Δt, and set p=λΔt (probability of success in a small time window). Let XΔt​ be the number of failures before success.

The total waiting time is TΔt​=XΔt​⋅Δt (time per failure × number of failures).

As Δt→0 (continuous time limit):

TΔt​d​Exp(λ)

Why: The MGF of TΔt​ converges to λ−sλ​, which is the MGF of Exp(λ).

The analogy

PropertyGeometric(p)Exponential(λ)
Support0,1,2,…[0,∞)
Memoryless?✅ (unique discrete)✅ (unique continuous)
Meanq/p1/λ
Varianceq/p21/λ2
”Success rate”p per trialλ per unit time
Sum of r copiesNegBin(r,p)Gamma(r,λ)

The Poisson process is the bridge: it generates Exponential inter-arrival times in continuous time, and Geometric waiting counts in discrete time.

12. Worked Example

Defective Parts A machine produces defective parts with probability p=0.1. Parts are inspected one by one. Let X = number of non-defective parts inspected before the first defective one (treating "defective" as "success").

X∼Geom(0.1), q=0.9.

PMF: P(X=k)=(0.9)k(0.1)

Expected failures before first defect: E[X]=0.10.9​=9

Variance: Var(X)=0.010.9​=90

Probability of finding first defect by the 5th inspection (X≤4): P(X≤4)=1−(0.9)5=1−0.59049=0.40951

Memoryless check: Given that the first 5 parts were all fine, what’s the probability the next 3 are also fine? P(X≥8∣X≥5)=P(X≥3)=(0.9)3=0.729 Same as P(X≥3) from the start. The conditioning on 5 prior successes changes nothing.
