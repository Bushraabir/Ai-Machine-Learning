04.Code Breaking

04.Code Breaking

Apr 02, 202618 min read

“Shannon’s 1949 paper on secrecy systems is one of the most beautiful things I’ve ever read. He took a problem that had been purely combinatorial — how do you hide a message? — and recast it as a problem in probability theory. A cipher is a random variable. Security is a statement about conditional distributions. The perfect cipher is one where the ciphertext and plaintext are independent. And he proved, with elementary probability, exactly how much key you need to achieve this — and exactly how many ciphertext characters you need to read before the key is determined. The whole paper is just probability, applied with ruthless clarity.”

The Setup: A Probabilistic Model of Secrecy

Let me fix notation. A cryptosystem consists of:

  • M: the message space (plaintexts). Each message M∈M is a random variable with some prior distribution P(M=m).
  • K: the key space. Key K∈K drawn uniformly at random (or from some distribution), independent of M.
  • C: the ciphertext space.
  • Encryption: C=EK​(M) — a deterministic function of M and K.
  • Decryption: M=DK​(C) — the inverse: DK​(EK​(m))=m for all m,k.

The adversary observes C and wants to recover M (or learn something about it). The question: how much does C reveal about M?

This is a conditional distribution question:

P(M=m∣C=c)=P(C=c)P(C=c∣M=m)⋅P(M=m)​

Security means the posterior P(M∣C) is close to the prior P(M) — the ciphertext teaches the adversary nothing.

Classical Ciphers

The Caesar Cipher

Encryption: Shift each letter by a fixed key k∈0,1,…,25:

Ek​(mi​)=(mi​+k)mod26

Key space: ∣K∣=26.

Breaking it: Exhaustive search — try all 26 keys, find the one that produces readable English. Even by hand, this takes under a minute. Computationally, it’s O(26⋅n) where n is the message length.

Probabilistically: If M is a single letter drawn from English letter frequencies, the posterior after observing one ciphertext letter C=c is:

P(K=k∣C=c,M=m)=1[c=(m+k)mod26]

But I don’t know M. I know P(M=m) — the English letter frequencies. Bayes gives:

P(M=m∣C=c)∝P(C=c∣M=m)P(M=m)=1[Ek​(m)=c]⋅P(M=m)

For a fixed key k, the cipher is a bijection on letters, so each ciphertext letter corresponds to exactly one plaintext letter under that key. There’s no probabilistic uncertainty — if I know k, I know m exactly. The uncertainty is purely about k.

The Caesar cipher has essentially zero security: H(K)=log2​26≈4.7 bits of key entropy — trivially exhausted.

The Vigenère Cipher

Encryption: Use a key k=(k1​,…,kd​)∈0,…,25d (period d). Encrypt the i-th letter by shifting by kimodd​:

Ek​(mi​)=(mi​+kimodd​)mod26

Key space: ∣K∣=26d — exponentially larger than Caesar.

Naive analysis: If the key length d is unknown, brute force is infeasible for large d. Key entropy is H(K)=dlog2​26≈4.7d bits.

But: The Vigenère is just d interleaved Caesar ciphers. Letters at positions 1,d+1,2d+1,… are all encrypted with the same shift k1​; letters at positions 2,d+2,… with shift k2​; and so on.

Breaking it — two steps:

Step 1: Determine d (the key length).

Kasiski test: Look for repeated substrings in the ciphertext. If a plaintext substring appears at positions i and j, and the key repeats with period d, then the ciphertext will repeat iff d∣(j−i). So: find all repeated trigrams in the ciphertext, compute the differences j−i, take gcd of all differences — that’s likely d (or a multiple of it).

Index of Coincidence: More elegant. Define the index of coincidence of a text c1​,…,cn​ as:

IC=∑l=025​n(n−1)fl​(fl​−1)​

where fl​ = frequency of letter l in the text. This is an estimate of P(two random letters are equal)=∑l=025​pl2​, where pl​ is the true frequency of letter l.

For English text: ICEnglish​≈0.065 (because English is non-uniform — ‘e’ appears ∼13 of the time, boosting ∑pl2​).

For a uniformly random text: ICuniform​=∑l=025​(1/26)2=1/26≈0.038.

For a Vigenère-encrypted English text with key length d: if I take every d-th character, I get a Caesar-shifted English text — IC ≈0.065. If I take every d′-th character for d′=d, the letters mix like random — IC ≈0.038.

Algorithm: For d′=1,2,3,…, compute IC of every d′-th subsequence. The true d is the smallest d′ where IC jumps to ≈0.065.

Step 2: Break each Caesar cipher separately.

Once I know d, split the ciphertext into d streams. Each stream is a Caesar cipher on English text. For each stream, compare the observed letter frequencies (shifted by kj​) to the English frequency distribution — find the shift that best aligns them.

Formally: Let p^​0​,…,p^​25​ be the observed letter frequencies in stream j. The true frequencies are p0​,…,p25​ (English). The key kj​ satisfies p^​(l+kj​)mod26​≈pl​ for all l. Find kj​ maximizing:

∑l=025​p^​(l+kj​)mod26​⋅pl​

(dot product of shifted observed with true frequencies — maximum at the correct shift). This is a maximum likelihood estimate under the assumption that letters are i.i.d. with English frequencies.

The IC is a beautiful object. It’s just ∑pl2​ estimated from data — the probability that two randomly drawn letters match. The fact that this single number distinguishes “Caesar-shifted English” (IC ≈0.065) from “random noise” (IC ≈0.038) is what makes the Vigenère breakable. The non-uniformity of English letter frequencies is the lever. The more non-uniform the source, the easier the cipher is to break.

Frequency Analysis as Maximum Likelihood

Let me be precise about what frequency analysis is doing probabilistically.

Model. Assume plaintext letters M1​,M2​,…,Mn​ are i.i.d. with distribution p=(p0​,…,p25​) (English frequencies). Ciphertext is Ci​=(Mi​+k)mod26 for unknown k.

Likelihood of observing ciphertext counts f=(f0​,…,f25​) given key k:

L(k)=P(f∣k)=∏l=025​p(l−k)mod26fl​​

(Letter l in the ciphertext came from plaintext letter (l−k)mod26, which has probability p(l−k)mod26​.)

Log-likelihood:

logL(k)=∑l=025​fl​logp(l−k)mod26​=n∑l=025​p^​l​logp(l−k)mod26​

where p^​l​=fl​/n. Maximizing over k∈0,…,25:

k^MLE​=argmaxk​∑l=025​p^​l​logp(l−k)mod26​

This is the cross-entropy between p^​ and the shifted English distribution — frequency analysis is maximum likelihood, nothing more.

Connection to KL divergence: Note ∑l​p^​l​logp(l−k)mod26​=−H(p^​)−DKL​(p^​∣p−k​) where p−k​ is p shifted by −k. Maximizing log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing DKL​(p^​∣p−k​) — finding the shift that makes the English frequencies closest to the observed frequencies, in KL-divergence.

Perfect Secrecy: Shannon’s Definition

Definition (Perfect Secrecy). A cryptosystem has perfect secrecy if for every message m∈M and ciphertext c∈C:

P(M=m∣C=c)=P(M=m)

Equivalently (by Bayes): P(C=c∣M=m)=P(C=c) — C and M are independent. Observing the ciphertext gives zero information about the plaintext.

Equivalently (in terms of mutual information): I(M;C)=0.

Theorem (Shannon, 1949). A cryptosystem with ∣M∣=∣C∣=∣K∣ achieves perfect secrecy if and only if:

  1. Every key is used with equal probability: K∼Uniform(K).
  2. For every plaintext m and ciphertext c, there exists exactly one key k such that Ek​(m)=c.

Proof.

(⇒) Assume perfect secrecy. Fix any ciphertext c. For every m:

P(C=c∣M=m)=P(C=c)

Since P(C=c∣M=m)=∑k​P(K=k)⋅1[Ek​(m)=c], summing over all messages:

P(C=c)=∑m​P(M=m)P(C=c∣M=m)=P(C=c)⋅∑m​P(M=m)=P(C=c)

This is tautological, but the constraint comes from fixing m: for each m, P(C=c∣M=m)=P(C=c) must hold simultaneously for all m. This forces the map k↦Ek​(m) to cover C with the same distribution regardless of m. Since ∣M∣=∣K∣=∣C∣, the function k↦Ek​(m) is a bijection for each fixed m, and uniform K gives uniform C for any m. The key must be uniform, and the bijection must hold — conditions 1 and 2.

(⇐) Assume conditions 1 and 2. Then for any m,c:

P(C=c∣M=m)=P(EK​(m)=c)=P(K=unique k s.t. Ek​(m)=c)=∣K∣1​

This is independent of m, so C⊥M. ■

Corollary (Key Length Lower Bound). For perfect secrecy: ∣K∣≥∣M∣. Equivalently, H(K)≥H(M). The key must be at least as long as the message.

Proof: From condition 2, for each c, there are ∣K∣ pairs (m,k) with Ek​(m)=c (one per key). These must cover all ∣M∣ messages. So ∣K∣≥∣M∣.

The One-Time Pad: Perfect Secrecy Achieved

Construction. M=K=C=0,1n (bitstrings of length n). Key K∼Uniform(0,1n). Encrypt:

C=M⊕K(bitwise XOR)

Decrypt: M=C⊕K (XOR is its own inverse).

Theorem. The one-time pad has perfect secrecy.

Proof. For any m,c∈0,1n:

P(C=c∣M=m)=P(M⊕K=c∣M=m)=P(K=m⊕c)=2n1​

This is independent of m. So C⊥M. ■

The one-time pad is perfectly secure — and completely impractical. The key must be:

  1. As long as the message (n bits for n bits of plaintext).
  2. Truly random (not pseudorandom).
  3. Never reused. (Hence “one-time.“)

Why no reuse? If C1​=M1​⊕K and C2​=M2​⊕K, then C1​⊕C2​=M1​⊕M2​. The XOR of two ciphertexts equals the XOR of two plaintexts — and M1​⊕M2​ leaks massive information about both M1​ and M2​ (especially for natural language, where many bits are redundant). This is the “two-time pad” attack, and it completely breaks one-time pad reuse.

The two-time pad attack is what broke the Venona project intercepts — Soviet intelligence reused one-time pad pages due to production pressure during WWII, and the NSA spent decades exploiting the resulting M1​⊕M2​ relationships in intercepted cables. Perfect theoretical security, destroyed by a single implementation mistake.

Unicity Distance: How Much Ciphertext Do I Need?

Shannon asked: given a ciphertext of length n, how many keys are consistent with it producing meaningful (English) plaintext?

Definition. The unicity distance n0​ is the minimum ciphertext length such that, in expectation, only one key is consistent with the ciphertext producing a valid plaintext. Formally, n0​ is the smallest n such that the expected number of spurious keys — wrong keys that still decrypt to meaningful text — drops below 1.

Derivation. The number of meaningful n-letter English plaintexts is approximately 2nHEnglish​ where HEnglish​ is the per-letter entropy of English (empirically ≈1.0–1.5 bits per letter, accounting for all correlations; often approximated as 1.0 bits/letter for long texts).

The total number of n-letter strings is 26n=2nlog2​26≈24.7n.

The redundancy of English per letter: r=log2​26−HEnglish​≈4.7−1.5=3.2 bits/letter.

For a key k chosen uniformly from ∣K∣ keys, the expected number of spurious keys is:

sˉ(n)≈∣K∣⋅26n2nHEnglish​​=∣K∣⋅2−nr

Setting sˉ(n0​)=1:

∣K∣⋅2−n0​r=1⟹n0​=rlog2​∣K∣​=rH(K)​

For Caesar (∣K∣=26, H(K)=log2​26≈4.7 bits):

n0​=3.24.7​≈1.5 letters

With just 2 ciphertext letters, in expectation fewer than 1 spurious key — essentially unique decryption. (This matches intuition: even with 3-4 letters of Caesar, the correct key is usually obvious.)

For Vigenère with period d (∣K∣=26d, H(K)=dlog2​26≈4.7d bits):

n0​=3.24.7d​≈1.47d

With about 1.5d letters of ciphertext, the key is essentially determined. Impressively small — a Vigenère cipher with 10-letter key is broken with about 15 letters of ciphertext (in principle; in practice you need more for the statistical estimates to be accurate).

For one-time pad (∣K∣=26n, H(K)=nlog2​26):

n0​=rnlog2​26​=3.2n⋅4.7​>n

The unicity distance exceeds the message length — there are always multiple valid keys consistent with any ciphertext, so the key can never be uniquely determined from the ciphertext alone. This is perfect secrecy, recovered from the information-theoretic angle.

The unicity distance is the information-theoretic “point of no return” — below n0​ characters, there isn’t enough ciphertext to pin down the key; above n0​, the key is essentially determined by the ciphertext (given a meaningful plaintext model). It’s the boundary between “computationally hard to break” and “theoretically impossible to break.” The OTP lives permanently below this boundary — by design.

Entropy and the Security of Ciphers

Let me collect the information-theoretic picture.

Key equivocation H(K∣Cn): the remaining entropy in the key after observing n ciphertext characters. As n increases:

  • For a finite-key cipher over redundant plaintext: H(K∣Cn)→0 as n→∞. Eventually the key is determined.
  • For the one-time pad: H(K∣Cn)=H(K) for all n. Observing ciphertext gives zero information about the key (and hence about the plaintext).

Shannon showed:

H(K∣Cn)=H(K)+H(Mn)−H(Cn)

(from the chain rule, since Cn is a deterministic function of (Mn,K)). For a “good” cipher, H(Cn)≈nlog2​26 (ciphertext looks uniform). So:

H(K∣Cn)≈H(K)+H(Mn)−nlog2​26=H(K)−n(log2​26−HEnglish​)=H(K)−nr

This decreases at rate r per ciphertext character. It hits zero at n=H(K)/r — the unicity distance.

Mutual information I(Mn;Cn): How much the ciphertext reveals about the plaintext.

I(Mn;Cn)=H(Mn)−H(Mn∣Cn)

For perfect secrecy: I(Mn;Cn)=0 for all n.

For a finite-key cipher: I(Mn;Cn)>0 and grows with n past the unicity distance — the ciphertext increasingly pins down the plaintext.

For messages of length ≥100 characters, this recovers the key exactly. For shorter messages, the IC estimate is noisy and the key length detection can fail — consistent with the unicity distance analysis.

The Redundancy of English: Why Ciphers Are Breakable

The entire analysis depends on English having HEnglish​≪log2​26. Let me make this precise.

n-gram entropy. Model English text as a stationary stochastic process. The per-character entropy rate:

H∞​=limn→∞​nH(M1​,M2​,…,Mn​)​

Empirical estimates (Shannon’s original 1951 paper on entropy of English):

  • Unigram model (letters i.i.d. with English frequencies): H≈4.03 bits/letter.
  • Bigram model: H≈3.32 bits/letter.
  • Trigram model: H≈3.1 bits/letter.
  • Full English (estimated via human prediction experiments): H∞​≈1.0–1.5 bits/letter.

Each model captures more structure. The first-order frequency analysis only uses the unigram approximation — it treats letters as i.i.d. with English frequencies. This already gives enough redundancy (r=4.7−4.03=0.67 bits/letter at the unigram level, or r≈3.2 bits/letter using the true entropy rate) to break the Vigenère.

Higher-order attacks. If I model English as a bigram or trigram process, I can use the transition probabilities for frequency analysis — this works with shorter ciphertexts and handles more complex ciphers. The principle is identical: maximum likelihood over the key space, using a richer language model.

This is the moment where code-breaking and natural language processing meet. A better language model (lower H∞​ estimate, more accurate n-gram probabilities) gives a stronger attack. Modern cryptanalysis of classical ciphers often uses large n-gram frequency tables from billions of words of text. The math is the same as Shannon; the model is just better.

Modern Implication: Why Modern Ciphers Are Different

Classical ciphers fail because:

  1. Short keys relative to message: H(K)<H(M), so unicity distance is finite.
  2. Structured operations (shifts, substitutions) that preserve statistical structure.

Modern symmetric ciphers (AES) avoid both:

  1. Long keys: AES-256 has H(K)=256 bits. At r≈1 bit/bit for binary data, unicity distance ≈256 bits of ciphertext — a fraction of one block. But:
  2. Pseudorandomness: AES output is computationally indistinguishable from uniform random — not just statistically, but even for computationally bounded adversaries. Frequency analysis finds no structure because the cipher actively destroys it.

The distinction: Shannon’s perfect secrecy is information-theoretic — even an adversary with unlimited computation can’t break it. Modern cipher security is computational — an adversary with polynomial-time computation can’t break it (under hardness assumptions like P=NP). The OTP is the only information-theoretically secure symmetric cipher. Everything else, including AES, is computationally secure.

One-Line Summary for Future Me

Code-breaking is Bayesian inference: the adversary updates P(K∣C) using the prior P(M) (English language model); a cipher is broken when H(K∣Cn)≈0, which happens at the unicity distance n0​=H(K)/r; perfect secrecy (I(M;C)=0, achieved only by the one-time pad) places n0​>∞ by making H(K)=∞ relative to any finite message.
