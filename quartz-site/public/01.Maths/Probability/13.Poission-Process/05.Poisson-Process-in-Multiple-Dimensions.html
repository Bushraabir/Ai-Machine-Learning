05.Poisson Process in Multiple Dimensions

1. Motivation — Why Go Beyond 1D?

In 1D, the Poisson process models events in time. But many real phenomena are spatially distributed:

ApplicationSpace
Stars in the sky2D sphere projection
Trees in a forest2D plane
Defects in a material3D volume
Neurons firing in a brain region3D space + time = 4D
Wi-Fi access points in a city2D plane
Cosmic ray hits on a detector2D surface

The 1D intuition extends cleanly — but the geometry becomes richer.

2. Definition — Spatial Poisson Process

Let S⊆Rd be a region in d-dimensional space. A spatial Poisson process (also: Poisson Point Process, PPP) with intensity measure Λ is a random collection of points xi​⊂S satisfying:

Axiom 1 — Poisson Counts

For any bounded region A⊆S:

N(A)∼Poisson(Λ(A))

where Λ(A) is the intensity measure of A (the expected number of points in A).

Axiom 2 — Independent Scattering

For any disjoint regions A1​,A2​,…,Ak​⊆S:

N(A1​),,N(A2​),,…,,N(Ak​)are mutually independent

These two axioms completely define the spatial Poisson process — no reference to ordering, interarrivals, or time.

3. Homogeneous vs. Inhomogeneous

3.1 Homogeneous (Stationary) PPP

The intensity measure is proportional to volume:

Λ(A)=λ⋅∣A∣

where ∣A∣ is the d-dimensional volume (area in 2D, length in 1D, volume in 3D) and λ>0 is the intensity (points per unit volume).

N(A)∼Poisson(λ⋅∣A∣)​

Homogeneous = uniform density everywhere. The process looks statistically the same from any location.

3.2 Inhomogeneous PPP

The intensity varies across space via an intensity function λ(x)≥0:

Λ(A)=∫A​λ(x),dx

N(A)∼Poisson!(∫A​λ(x),dx)​

Points are denser where λ(x) is large.

4. The d-Dimensional Setup

Key Quantities by Dimension

| Dimension | Space | Volume element | ∣A∣ for sphere/disk radius r | |---|---|---|---| | 1D | R | dx | 2r (interval) | | 2D | R2 | dx,dy | πr2 (disk) | | 3D | R3 | dx,dy,dz | 34​πr3 (ball) | | dD | Rd | dx1​⋯dxd​ | Γ(d/2+1)πd/2​rd |

Count Distribution

For homogeneous PPP(λ) in d dimensions, in a ball of radius r centred at origin:

N(Br​)∼Poisson!(λ⋅Γ(d/2+1)πd/2​rd)

5. The Fundamental Conditioning Theorem in dD

Theorem: Given N(A)=n, the n points in A are distributed as n iid draws from the normalized intensity measure on A:

Each point∣N(A)=n∼iidf(x)=Λ(A)λ(x)​,x∈A

For homogeneous PPP: Given N(A)=n, the n points are iid Uniform(A).

(x1​,…,xn​)∣N(A)=n∼iidUniform(A)​

This is the 1D conditioning theorem, verbatim, in d dimensions. The geometry of A only affects the uniform distribution’s support.

6. Superposition and Thinning in dD

Both theorems carry over without modification:

Superposition

PPP(λ1​)+PPP(λ2​)=PPP(λ1​+λ2​)

(adding point patterns from independent processes → denser homogeneous PPP)

Thinning

Each point of PPP(λ) is independently retained with probability p(x) (can depend on location):

Retained points∼PPP(λ⋅p(x)) Removed points∼PPP(λ⋅(1−p(x)))

Both independent. For homogeneous thinning (p(x)=p constant):

PPP(λ)thin p​PPP(λp)⊥PPP(λ(1−p))

7. Nearest Neighbour Distance

One of the most important derived quantities in spatial statistics.

Setup: Homogeneous PPP(λ) in Rd. Fix a point at the origin (or pick a random point). Let R = distance to the nearest other point.

In 2D

The probability that no point falls within radius r:

P(R>r)=P(N(Br​)=0)=e−λπr2

So the CDF and PDF of R:

FR​(r)=1−e−λπr2​

fR​(r)=2λπr⋅e−λπr2,r≥0​

This is a Rayleigh distribution (special case of Weibull).

E[R]=2λ​1​,Var(R)=4πλ4−π​

In dD (General)

Let ωd​=Γ(d/2+1)πd/2​ be the volume of the unit ball in Rd.

P(R>r)=e−λωd​rd

fR​(r)=λωd​d⋅rd−1⋅e−λωd​rd

E[R]=(λωd​)1/dΓ(1+1/d)​

DimensionfR​(r)Distribution name
1D2λe−2λr (for r>0)Exponential(2λ)
2D2λπr⋅e−λπr2Rayleigh
3D4λπr2⋅e−34​λπr3Weibull

1D check: In 1D, nearest neighbour = min of left and right Exp(λ) gaps. By superposition and race: R∼Exp(2λ). ✓

8. The Empty Space Function

Definition: F(r)=P(no point of PPP within distance r of origin)

For homogeneous PPP(λ) in Rd:

F(r)=1−e−λωd​rd​

Same formula as the nearest-neighbour CDF — because for a homogeneous PPP, these two quantities coincide (the process looks the same from any fixed point as from a typical random point).

This is used in spatial statistics to test whether an observed point pattern is consistent with a PPP (CSR — Complete Spatial Randomness).

9. Campbell’s Theorem

A fundamental result for computing expectations of sums over PPP points.

Theorem: For PPP with intensity λ(x) and any measurable function f:S→R:

E![xi​∈PPP∑​f(xi​)]=∫S​f(x),λ(x),dx​

Intuition: Replace the random sum over points with an integral against the intensity measure.

Example — Total signal power:
Transmitters distributed as PPP(λ) in 2D. Each transmitter at location x contributes signal power f(x)=∣x∣−α (path-loss model). Expected total received power:

E![∑i​∣xi​∣−α]=λ∫R2​∣x∣−α,dx=λ∫0∞​r−α⋅2πr,dr=2πλ∫0∞​r1−α,dr

(Converges for α>2.)

Second moment — Campbell’s extended theorem:

Var![∑xi​​f(xi​)]=∫S​f(x)2,λ(x),dx

10. Marked Poisson Process

Each point xi​ of the PPP is equipped with an independent random mark mi​∼Fm​ (a scalar, vector, or label).

Result: The marked process is itself a PPP on S×M (space × mark space) with intensity:

λ(x,m)=λ(x)⋅fm​(m)

Examples:

  • Trees (PPP in 2D) with random heights (marks)
  • Wi-Fi access points (PPP in 2D) with random transmission powers
  • Stars (PPP in 3D) with random luminosities

Key property: Projecting back to S (ignoring marks) recovers the original PPP. Thinning by mark value recovers the thinning theorem.

11. Stochastic Geometry — Voronoi and Delaunay

Given a PPP in R2, two canonical geometric structures arise:

Voronoi Tessellation

Each point xi​ “owns” the region of space closer to it than to any other PPP point:

Vi​=y∈R2:∣y−xi​∣≤∣y−xj​∣;∀j=i

The Voronoi cells of a PPP(λ) form a random tessellation. Each cell has:

E[area of cell]=λ1​,E[number of sides]=6

Delaunay Triangulation

The dual graph of the Voronoi tessellation — connects PPP points whose Voronoi cells share an edge. Used in mesh generation, spatial interpolation.

Application in ML: Voronoi diagrams of training data → nearest-neighbour classifiers; each Voronoi cell is the decision region for one training point.

12. 2D PPP — Worked Examples

Example 1 — Stars in the Sky

Stars distributed as homogeneous PPP(λ=0.01/deg²) on a 2D projection.

(a) Expected stars in a 10° × 10° patch: E[N]=0.01×100=1

(b) Probability of seeing exactly 3 stars in the patch: P(N=3)=3!e−1⋅13​=6e−1​≈0.0613

(c) Expected nearest-star distance (in degrees): E[R]=2λ​1​=20.01​1​=0.21​=5 degrees

Example 2 — Wi-Fi Access Points

Access points distributed as PPP(λ=0.05/m²) in a 2D office floor.

(a) Probability of no access point within 2 metres: P(R>2)=e−λπr2=e−0.05×π×4=e−0.628≈0.534

(b) Given 20 access points in a 20m × 20m room, where are they?

By conditioning theorem: each is iid Uniform on the 400m² room.
Expected position of each: centre of the room (10,10).

Example 3 — Defects in a Material

Defects in a 3D material follow PPP(λ=10−6/mm³).

Volume of a 10mm × 10mm × 10mm cube: ∣A∣=1000 mm³.

N(A)∼Poisson(10−6×1000)=Poisson(10−3)

P(N=0)=e−0.001≈0.999(almost surely defect-free)

14. Connection to ML and CS229

14.1 Stochastic Geometry in Wireless Networks

Base stations: PPP(λb​) in 2D. Users: PPP(λu​) in 2D. Independent.

Coverage probability (probability a random user is covered by at least one base station within radius r):

P(covered)=1−e−λb​πr2

This is the empty space function — the PPP model makes this analytically tractable.

14.2 Object Detection / Point Cloud Processing

LiDAR point clouds are often modelled as inhomogeneous PPPs. Detecting an object = testing H0​: PPP vs H1​: non-PPP clustering in a region.

14.3 Neural Processes and Spike Trains

Neuron spike trains: modelled as inhomogeneous PPP(λ(t)) where λ(t) is the firing rate (a function of stimulus). Learning λ(t) from data = density estimation problem.

14.4 Gaussian Processes and PPP

The Cox Process (doubly stochastic Poisson process): λ(x) is itself a random field (e.g., a log-Gaussian process). Used in Bayesian spatial statistics and GP-based generative models in PRML Ch.3/6.

18. Mental Model

Imagine a cosmic dart-thrower standing outside all of space, throwing darts uniformly at random into Rd at rate λ per unit volume. Each dart lands independently of all others, with no memory of where previous darts landed.

  • Ask “how many darts in region A?” → Poisson(λ∣A∣)
  • Ask “given n darts in A, where are they?” → iid Uniform(A)
  • Ask “how far to the nearest dart?” → depends on d-dimensional ball geometry

The geometry of space enters only through the volume of regions and balls. The Poisson randomness is purely in the count; the spatial structure is purely in the uniform scatter.
