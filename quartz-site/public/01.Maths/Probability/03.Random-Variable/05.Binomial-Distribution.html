05.Binomial Distribution

05.Binomial Distribution

Apr 23, 202610 min read

Big Picture The Binomial distribution answers one precise question:

“If I repeat the same yes/no experiment n times independently, how likely am I to get exactly k successes?”

It is the natural extension of Bernoulli Distribution from one trial to n trials. Every concept you derived for Bernoulli — PMF, expectation, variance, MGF, exponential family — reappears here, scaled up by n.

The Three Conditions

The Binomial model is valid if and only if all three hold:

Binomial Conditions

  1. Fixed number of trials — n is decided before you start. You cannot stop early based on outcomes.
  2. Independence — the outcome of trial i tells you nothing about trial j.
  3. Constant probability — p does not shift between trials. Every trial has the same p.

If any one of these breaks, the Binomial breaks:

  • Condition 3 fails → sampling without replacement → use Hypergeometric Distribution
  • Condition 1 fails (you stop at k-th success) → use Negative Binomial Distribution
  • All three hold but n→∞, p→0, np→λ → use Poisson Distribution as approximation

Definition and Parameters

X∼Binomial(n,p)

SymbolTypeMeaning
nNNumber of trials
p[0,1]Probability of success on each trial
XRandom VariableCount of successes across all n trials

Support

Support(X)=0,1,2,…,n

X can never be negative (you cannot have fewer than 0 successes) and can never exceed n (you only run n trials). Both bounds are tight — P(X=0)>0 and P(X=n)>0 for p∈(0,1).

PMF

  • Step 1: Fix a specific sequence

    Suppose you want exactly k successes in n trials. Consider one specific sequence — say, successes on trials 1,2,…,k and failures on trials k+1,…,n: kS,S,…,S​​,n−kF,F,…,F​​ By independence, the probability of this exact sequence is: kp⋅p⋯p​​⋅n−k(1−p)(1−p)⋯(1−p)​​=pk(1−p)n−k

  • Step 2: Count all valid sequences

    We don’t care which k trials succeed — only that k of them do. Every arrangement of k S’s and (n−k) F’s is equally valid, and each has the same probability pk(1−p)n−k (by independence — order doesn’t affect the product).

    How many such arrangements exist? We are choosing k positions out of n for the successes: (kn​)=k!(n−k)!n!​

  • Step 3: Add up all valid sequences

    Since the sequences are mutually exclusive (each is a distinct outcome), we add their probabilities: P(X=k)=arrangements(kn​)​​⋅probability of eachpk(1−p)n−k​​ P(X=k)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k,k=0,1,…,n​

TermWhat it counts
(kn​)=k!(n−k)!n!​Number of distinct orderings of k successes in n trials
pkProbability that those k chosen trials are all successes
(1−p)n−kProbability that the remaining (n−k) trials are all failures

The Core Insight We are counting weighted arrangements. The binomial coefficient counts the arrangements. pk(1−p)n−k weights each arrangement equally (by independence). Multiplication works because the trials are independent — joint probability = product of marginals.

Verification: Probabilities Sum to 1

We need ∑k=0n​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k=1.

This follows directly from the Binomial Theorem: (a+b)n=∑k=0n​(kn​)akbn−k Set a=p, b=1−p: ∑k=0n​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k=(p+(1−p))n=1n=1✓

The PMF is valid. The Binomial Theorem is not a coincidence — it is exactly why the distribution is called “Binomial.”

The Indicator Variable Decomposition

This is the most powerful structural insight about the Binomial. Write: X=X1​+X2​+⋯+Xn​ where each Xi​∼Bernoulli(p) independently, and Xi​=1 if trial i succeeds, 0 otherwise.

X is the sum of n independent, identically distributed Bernoulli random variables.

This decomposition is not just a notational trick — it is the proof engine for expectation, variance, and the MGF, all with minimal algebra. Every derivation below uses it.

ExpectatioN:

  • The direct way (painful)

    E[X]=∑k=0n​k(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k The k=0 term vanishes. For k≥1, use k(kn​)=n(k−1n−1​): =∑k=1n​n(k−1n−1​)pk(1−p)n−k =np∑k=1n​(k−1n−1​)pk−1(1−p)(n−1)−(k−1) Substitute j=k−1: =np∑j=0n−1​(jn−1​)pj(1−p)(n−1)−j=np⋅1=np →(The sum is a Binomial(n−1,p) PMF summing to 1.)

  • The elegant way (indicator variables)

    By the indicator decomposition X=∑i=1n​Xi​ and Linearity of Expectations (no independence required): E[X]=∑i=1n​E[Xi​]=∑i=1n​p=np E[X]=np​

Internalize This Linearity of expectation requires no assumptions about dependence. It always holds. This is why the indicator decomposition is the preferred path in Stat 110 — it converts hard combinatorial sums into trivial additions.

Intuition:

If each trial contributes p in expectation, n independent trials contribute np. Out of 100 coin flips with p=0.5, you expect 50 heads. Exactly as intuition demands. see more01.Expectation

Variance:

  • Via indicator variables

    Var(X)=Var!(∑i=1n​Xi​) Since X1​,…,Xn​ are independent: =∑i=1n​Var(Xi​)=∑i=1n​p(1−p)=np(1−p) Var(X)=np(1−p)​

Independence Was Needed Here Variance of a sum equals the sum of variances only when the variables are independent (or at least uncorrelated). Linearity of expectation has no such requirement. Know exactly which step demands independence.

  • The direct way

    Use Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2. Compute E[X(X−1)] first (easier than E[X2] directly): E[X(X−1)]=∑k=0n​k(k−1)(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k For k≥2, use k(k−1)(kn​)=n(n−1)(k−2n−2​): =n(n−1)p2∑k=2n​(k−2n−2​)pk−2(1−p)n−k=n(n−1)p2 Then E[X2]=E[X(X−1)]+E[X]=n(n−1)p2+np.

    Var(X)=n(n−1)p2+np−n2p2=np−np2=np(1−p)✓

Standard Deviation

σX​=np(1−p)​

Maximized when p=0.5: σX​=2n​​. The uncertainty scales as n​, not n — a fundamental fact in statistics (it’s why standard errors shrink as 1/n​).

Skewness

By the indicator decomposition, X=∑Xi​ with each Xi​ i.i.d. Bernoulli(p). The standardized third central moment of a sum of i.i.d. variables satisfies: γ1​(X)=n​γ1​(Xi​)​ This follows from the fact that the third cumulant scales as n while σ3 scales as n3/2.

From the Bernoulli Distribution note, γ1​(Xi​)=p(1−p)​1−2p​. Therefore: γ1​(X)=n​1​⋅p(1−p)​1−2p​ γ1​=np(1−p)​1−2p​​

Direct verification

E[(X−np)3] =n⋅E[(Xi​−p)3] =n⋅p(1−p)(1−2p)

γ1​ =[np(1−p)]3/2n⋅p(1−p)(1−2p)​ =np(1−p)​1−2p​✓

ConditionSkewnessShape
p<0.5γ1​>0Right-skewed — success is rare, most mass near 0
p=0.5γ1​=0Perfectly symmetric
p>0.5γ1​<0Left-skewed — failure is rare, most mass near n

As n→∞, γ1​→0 — the distribution becomes more symmetric. This is the Central Limit Theorem in action. see more02.Skewness

Kurtosis

By the same cumulant scaling argument (the fourth cumulant scales as n, σ4 scales as n2): γ2​(X)=nγ2​(Xi​)​ From Bernoulli Distribution: γ2​(Xi​)=p(1−p)1−6p(1−p)​. γ2​=np(1−p)1−6p(1−p)​​ As n→∞, γ2​→0 — excess kurtosis vanishes, consistent with convergence to Normal (which has γ2​=0). see more03.Kurtosis

CDF

There is no closed-form expression for the Binomial CDF — it is always a finite sum: F(k)=P(X≤k)=∑i=0k​(in​)pi(1−p)n−i For computation, use the regularized incomplete beta function: F(k;n,p)=I1−p​(n−k,k+1) see more10.Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

Moment Generating Function (MGF)

  • Via indicator variables

    Since X=∑i=1n​Xi​ with independent Xi​∼Bernoulli(p): MX​(t) =E[etX] =E![et(X1​+⋯+Xn​)] =E![∏i=1n​etXi​] By independence, the expectation of a product equals the product of expectations: =∏i=1n​E[etXi​] =∏i=1n​MXi​​(t)

    From Bernoulli Distribution: MXi​​(t)=1−p+pet. Therefore:MX​(t)=(1−p+pet)n​

  • Direct verification

    MX​(t) =∑k=0n​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−ketk =∑k=0n​(kn​)(pet)k(1−p)n−k By the Binomial Theorem with a=pet and b=1−p: =(pet+1−p)n=(1−p+pet)n✓

Recovering moments

  • First derivative: MX′​(t) =n(1−p+pet)n−1⋅pet

    At t=0: MX′​(0) =n(1)n−1⋅p =np =E[X]✓

  • Second derivative: MX′′​(t)
    =n(n−1)(1−p+pet)n−2(pet)2+n(1−p+pet)n−1pet

    At t=0: MX′′​(0) =n(n−1)p2+np =E[X2]

Var(X) =E[X2]−(E[X])2 =n(n−1)p2+np−n2p2 =np(1−p)✓ read more:06.Variance

Binomial as Exponential Family

Write the PMF: P(X=k)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k Take the exponential of the log: =(kn​)exp!(klogp+(n−k)log(1−p)) =(kn​)(1−p)nexp!(klog1−pp​) Matching to h(x)exp(ηT(x)−A(θ)):

ComponentValueMeaning
h(k)(kn​)Combinatorial base measure
η(p)log1−pp​Log-odds (logit) — same as Bernoulli
T(k)kSufficient statistic: just the count of successes
A(p)−nlog(1−p)=nlog(1+eη)Log-partition function

Sufficiency in Practice T(k)=k means: given n and p, the total number of successes is all you need to estimate p. You don't need to know which specific trials succeeded — only how many. This is the formal justification for reporting "68 out of 100" rather than the full sequence of 100 outcomes.

Symmetry

If X∼Bin(n,p), then: Y=n−X∼Bin(n,1−p)

Proof: Y counts failures. Each failure has probability 1−p. By symmetry of the setup, Y satisfies all three Binomial conditions with parameter 1−p.

Consequence: P(X=k;n,p)=P(Y=n−k;n,1−p)=(n−kn​)(1−p)n−kpk, which equals P(X=k) since (kn​)=(n−kn​). ✓

At p=0.5: X and n−X have the same distribution → perfect symmetry.

Limiting Distributions

  • Poisson Limit (Rare Events)

    As n→∞, p→0, with np=λ fixed: Bin(n,p)d​Poisson(λ)

    Why: When p is tiny and n is large, the binomial coefficient and powers simplify to the Poisson PMF. See Poisson Distribution for the full derivation.

    When to use: n≥20, p≤0.05 (rule of thumb).

  • Normal Approximation (CLT)

    By the 09.Central Limit Theorem, for large n: X≈N(np,;np(1−p)) Or equivalently, np(1−p)​X−np​≈N(0,1).

    When to use: np≥5 and n(1−p)≥5 (rule of thumb).

    Continuity correction: Since X is discrete and Normal is continuous, use P(X≤k)≈P!(Z≤np(1−p)​k+0.5−np​).
