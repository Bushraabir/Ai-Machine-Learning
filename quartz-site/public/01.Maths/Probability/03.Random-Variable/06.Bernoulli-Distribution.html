06.Bernoulli Distribution

Apr 23, 20267 min read

Big Picture The Bernoulli distribution is the atomic unit of probability theory. Every model involving binary outcomes — coin flips, spam/not-spam, disease/healthy, click/no-click — begins here. Master this completely. Everything from Binomial to Logistic Regression to Neural Network output layers is built on top of it.

What Problem Are We Modelling?

We have a single experiment with exactly two possible outcomes:

  • Success — coded as X=1
  • Failure — coded as X=0

The coding 0,1 is not arbitrary. It is chosen precisely so that algebraic expressions for expectation and variance come out cleanly. You’ll see why below.

We define one parameter: p=P(success)=P(X=1),p∈[0,1] And immediately: P(failure)=P(X=0)=1−p

This is forced : the two probabilities must sum to 1. There is no other free parameter.

PMF

  • The Piecewise Form (Naive)

    The most honest way to write the PMF is: P(X=x)={p1−p​if x=1if x=0​

    This is perfectly correct but clunky — two cases, hard to manipulate algebraically.

  • The Compact Form — Why px(1−p)1−x?

    We want a single expression that automatically selects the right branch.

    Observe what happens when we write px(1−p)1−x and substitute: When x=1: p1⋅(1−p)1−1 =p1⋅(1−p)0 =p⋅1 =p✓ When x=0: p0⋅(1−p)1−0 =1⋅(1−p)1 =1−p✓ The exponents x and 1−x act as a binary switch:

  • When x=1: the first factor is “on” (p1), the second is “off” ((1−p)0=1)

  • When x=0: the first factor is “off” (p0=1), the second is “on” ((1−p)1)

Therefore the compact PMF is P(X=x)=px(1−p)1−x,x∈0,1​

Why This Form Matters This compact form is not just aesthetic. It is required for:

  • Writing the likelihood function for multiple observations: ∏i=1n​pxi​(1−p)1−xi​
  • Taking the log-likelihood (which turns products into sums)
  • Deriving the MLE for p
  • Recognizing Bernoulli as a member of the Exponential Family

Verification: Does It Sum to 1?

∑x∈0,1​px(1−p)1−x =(1−p)+p=1✓

Support

Support(X)=0,1 Only two points carry probability mass. Everything else has probability zero and is excluded from the support by definition. See 09.Support of a Random Variable.

Expectation

  • From the Definition

    The expected value of a discrete RV is E[X]=∑x​x⋅P(X=x). E[X]=∑x∈0,1​x⋅P(X=x) =0⋅P(X=0)+1⋅P(X=1) =0⋅(1−p)+1⋅p =0+p E[X]=p​

Intuition

The expected value equals exactly the probability of success. This is why the coding 0,1 (not −1,+1 or 1,2) is canonical — it makes E[X] directly interpretable as a probability.

Variance

  • Step 1: Compute E[X2]

    E[X2]=∑x∈0,1​x2⋅P(X=x) =02⋅(1−p)+12⋅p =0+p =p Notice: for a 0,1-valued variable, x2=x for both values (02=0, 12=1). So E[X2]=E[X]=p.

  • Step 2: Apply the Variance Formula

    Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2 =p−p2 =p(1−p) Var(X)=p(1−p)​
Intuition: Where Is Variance Maximized?

Take the derivative with respect to p and set to zero: dpd​[p(1−p)] =1−2p =0 ⟹p=21​

At p=21​: Var(X)=21​⋅21​=41​, the maximum. At p=0 or p=1: Var(X)=0 — no uncertainty when the outcome is certain. Maximum uncertainty at p=0.5, zero uncertainty at the extremes. This curve is the foundation of entropy in information theory.

Standard Deviation

σ=Var(X)​ =p(1−p)​

No separate derivation needed — it is the square root of variance by definition. The maximum standard deviation is 1/4​=0.5, occurring at p=0.5.

Skewness

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution: γ1​=σ3E[(X−μ)3]​

  • Step 1: Compute E[(X−μ)3]

    Let μ=p. We need E[(X−p)3]: E[(X−p)3]=(0−p)3(1−p)+(1−p)3⋅p =−p3(1−p)+p(1−p)3 Factor out p(1−p): =p(1−p)[−(p2)+(1−p)2] =p(1−p)[−p2+1−2p+p2] =p(1−p)(1−2p)

  • Step 2: Divide by σ3

    σ3=[p(1−p)]3/2

    γ1​=[p(1−p)]3/2p(1−p)(1−2p)​ =p(1−p)​1−2p​ γ1​=p(1−p)​1−2p​​

p<0.51−2p>0Right-skewed — most mass at 0, long tail toward 1
p=0.5γ1​=0Perfectly symmetric
p>0.51−2p<0Left-skewed — most mass at 1, long tail toward 0

Kurtosis (Excess)

Excess kurtosis measures “tailedness” relative to a Normal distribution: γ2​=σ4E[(X−μ)4]​−3

The −3 subtracts the Normal’s baseline kurtosis.

  • Step 1: Compute E[(X−p)4]

    E[(X−p)4]=(−p)4(1−p)+(1−p)4⋅p =p4(1−p)+p(1−p)4 =p(1−p)[p3+(1−p)3] Expand (1−p)3=1−3p+3p2−p3:p3+(1−p)3=p3+1−3p+3p2−p3=1−3p+3p2 =1−3p(1−p) So: E[(X−p)4]=p(1−p)[1−3p(1−p)]

  • Step 2: Divide by σ4 and subtract 3

    σ4=[p(1−p)]2 σ4E[(X−p)4]​ =[p(1−p)]2p(1−p)[1−3p(1−p)]​ =p(1−p)1−3p(1−p)​

    γ2​=p(1−p)1−3p(1−p)​−3 = \frac{1 - 3p(1-p) - 3p(1-p)}{p(1-p)}$$ \boxed{\gamma_2 = \frac{1 - 6p(1-p)}{p(1-p)}}$

Intuition

At p=0.5: γ2​=0.251−6(0.25)​=0.25−0.5​=−2.

Negative excess kurtosis means the Bernoulli at p=0.5 is platykurtic (lighter tails, flatter than Normal). A two-point distribution has no tails at all — makes sense. see more03.Kurtosis

CDF

The CDF is F(x)=P(X≤x). We evaluate at all real x:

  • Case 1: x<0 No values in support are ≤x, so F(x)=0.
  • Case 2: 0≤x<1 Only X=0 is ≤x, so: F(x)=P(X=0)=1−p
  • Case 3: x≥1 Both X=0 and X=1 are ≤x, so: F(x)=P(X=0)+P(X=1)=(1−p)+p=1 F(x)={0​x<0 1−p​0≤x<1 1​x≥1​​

Moment Generating Function (MGF)

MX​(t)=E[etX]

  • Derivation:

    MX​(t)=∑x∈0,1​etx⋅P(X=x) =et⋅0⋅(1−p)+et⋅1⋅p =1⋅(1−p)+et⋅p MX​(t)=1−p+pet​

Recovering Moments from the MGF

The n-th moment is E[Xn]=MX(n)​(0) (the n-th derivative evaluated at t=0).

  • First derivative: MX′​(t)=pet MX′​(0)=p=E[X]✓
  • Second derivative: M_X''(t) = pe^t$$ M_X”(0) = p = \mathbb{E}[X^2] \checkmarkThisconfirms\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = p - p^2 = p(1-p)$.

Notice All derivatives of MX​(t) equal pet, so all moments of X equal p. This makes sense because Xn=X for X∈0,1 and any n≥1.

see more05.Moment Generating Function (MGF),06.Generating Moments with the MGF

Bernoulli as Exponential Family

Rewrite the PMF to expose the exponential family structure: P(X=x)=px(1−p)1−x Take the exponential of the log: =exp!(xlogp+(1−x)log(1−p)) =exp!(xlogp−xlog(1−p)+log(1−p)) =exp!(xlog1−pp​+log(1−p))

Matching to h(x)exp(ηT(x)−A(θ)):

ComponentValueMeaning
h(x)1No base measure needed
η(p)log1−pp​The log-odds (logit) of p
T(x)xIdentity — x itself is sufficient
A(p)−log(1−p)=log(1+eη)Log-partition function

The natural parameter η=log1−pp​ is the logit function — the same function that defines logistic regression. When you model η=w⊤x, you recover logistic regression exactly. See Generalized Linear Models (GLMs).

Worked Example

Biased Coin Toss a biased coin with p=0.7 (probability of heads). Let X=1 for heads, X=0 for tails.

PMF: P(X=1)=0.7,P(X=0)=0.3 Expectation: E[X]=0.7 Variance: Var(X)=0.7×0.3=0.21 Skewness: γ1​=0.21​1−2(0.7)​=0.458−0.4​≈−0.873 Negative — left-skewed, as expected since p>0.5 (most mass on heads). MGF: MX​(t)=0.3+0.7et
