03.Kurtosis

Mar 28, 202610 min read

Skewness was about which side the tail pulls toward. Kurtosis is about how heavy the tails are — how often the distribution produces extreme values. I find this one harder to visualize, and I think most people secretly do too. The “peakedness” intuition is wrong. Write this carefully.

The Core Idea

Variance measures spread. Skewness measures asymmetry. But two distributions can share a mean, variance, and skewness, yet still differ in how often they produce extreme outliers. Kurtosis captures tail heaviness — the propensity of the distribution to generate values far from the mean.

The standard reference point is the Normal distribution, which has kurtosis 3. Almost everything is defined relative to this benchmark, via excess kurtosis =Kurt(X)−3.

The Normal as benchmark is not arbitrary — it’s the distribution that the CLT pushes everything toward, so it’s the natural “neutral” shape. Distributions with heavier tails than the Normal have excess kurtosis >0; lighter tails, <0.

Definition

Let X be a random variable with μ=E[X], σ2=Var(X)>0, and E[X4]<∞.

Kurt(X)=E![(σX−μ​)!4]=σ4E[(X−μ)4]​​

The excess kurtosis (what most software and most people actually mean when they say “kurtosis”) is:

κ(X)=Kurt(X)−3=κ22​κ4​​​

where κ4​ and κ2​ are the fourth and second cumulants respectively.

Why the Fourth Moment?

  • Even power: (X−μ)4≥0 always, so kurtosis is always non-negative. Unlike skewness (which uses an odd power to detect direction), kurtosis uses an even power because it measures magnitude of deviation, not direction.
  • Fourth power: Points far from the mean contribute quartically. A deviation of 2σ contributes 16× more than a deviation of σ. Kurtosis is exquisitely sensitive to rare extreme events — far more than variance.
  • Why not just use E[(X−μ)4]? Same reason as skewness — without standardization by σ4, the quantity has units [X]4 and changes under rescaling. We want a pure shape descriptor.

Why subtract 3?

Because Kurt(N(μ,σ2))=3 for any μ,σ2. Subtracting 3 recenters so the Normal has excess kurtosis 0, making deviations from Normal-tail-behavior immediately readable. Distributions with κ>0 are called leptokurtic (heavy-tailed); κ<0 are platykurtic (light-tailed); κ=0 is mesokurtic (Normal-like tails).

Properties

Proposition 1 (Affine invariance).
For constants a=0 and b∈R: Kurt(aX+b)=Kurt(X)

Proof.
Let Y=aX+b, so E[Y]=aμ+b and SD(Y)=∣a∣σ. Then: Kurt(Y)=(∣a∣σ)4E[(aX−aμ)4]​=a4σ4a4E[(X−μ)4]​=Kurt(X).□

Proposition 2 (Non-negativity and lower bound).
Kurt(X)≥1

Proof.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to E[(X−μ)2⋅1]: (E[(X−μ)2])2≤E[(X−μ)4]⋅E[1]=E[(X−μ)4] So σ4≤E[(X−μ)4], i.e. Kurt(X)≥1.

Equivalently, by Jensen’s inequality: since x↦x2 is convex, E[(X−μ)4]=E![((X−μ)2)2]≥(E[(X−μ)2])2=σ4.□

So excess kurtosis ≥−2 always. The lower bound κ=−2 is achieved only by a Bernoulli(1/2) — the most “light-tailed” possible distribution on two points.

Proposition 3 (Symmetry is not implied by zero excess kurtosis).
Just as with skewness, κ(X)=0 does not imply X is Normal or even symmetric. It only means the fourth cumulant vanishes. The Normal is characterized by all cumulants of order ≥3 vanishing simultaneously.

The Peakedness Misconception

This tripped me up for longer than I’d like to admit.

Kurtosis is often described as measuring “peakedness” — how sharp the central peak of the density is. This is misleading at best, wrong at worst.

The correct interpretation is tail heaviness. Here is why the peak story fails:

You can construct two distributions with identical variances where the “more peaked” one (sharper center) also has lighter tails — contradicting the supposed peak-kurtosis link. The fourth moment is dominated by the tails, not the center. A point at 5σ from the mean contributes 54=625 times more than a point at σ. The bulk of the distribution near the mean contributes almost nothing to E[(X−μ)4].

Correct mental model:
High kurtosis = the distribution occasionally produces extreme outliers. The tails have more probability mass than the Normal. The “peak” may or may not be sharp — that’s a separate feature. Think of the t-distribution with small degrees of freedom: heavy tails, occasional wild values, kurtosis →∞ as ν→4+.

Relation to the CGF

As with skewness, the CGF is the cleanest framework. Let K(t)=logE[etX] with cumulants κn​=K(n)(0).

The fourth cumulant is: κ4​=μ4′​−4κ3​κ1​−3κ22​−κ14​=E[(X−μ)4]−3σ4

where μ4′​ denotes the fourth raw moment. Rearranging:

E[(X−μ)4]=κ4​+3κ22​

Therefore: Kurt(X)=κ22​κ4​+3κ22​​=κ22​κ4​​+3

And so: κ(X)=κ22​κ4​​=(K′′(0))2K(4)(0)​​

For independent X,Y: κ4​(X+Y)=κ4​(X)+κ4​(Y)

For i.i.d. sum Sn​=X1​+⋯+Xn​: κ(Sn​)=(nκ2​)2nκ4​​=κ22​κ4​​⋅n1​

The excess kurtosis decays like 1/n. Even faster than skewness (1/n​). The CLT is once again visible: Sn​ approaches Normality (κ=0) as n→∞, and the fourth cumulant is the next correction term.

Computing Kurtosis: The Expansion Formula

E[(X−μ)4]=E[X4]−4μE[X3]+6μ2E[X2]−3μ4

Proof. Expand (X−μ)4 via the binomial theorem: (X−μ)4=X4−4μX3+6μ2X2−4μ3X+μ4 Take expectations and use E[X]=μ: E[(X−μ)4]=E[X4]−4μE[X3]+6μ2E[X2]−4μ3⋅μ+μ4 =E[X4]−4μE[X3]+6μ2E[X2]−3μ4.□

In practice, for standardized X (i.e., μ=0, σ=1), this collapses to just E[X4], which is why the standardization step is always worth doing first.

Kurtosis of Named Distributions

Normal Distribution X∼N(μ,σ2)

Kurt(X)=3,κ(X)=0

Proof via MGF.
WLOG take X∼N(0,1). The MGF is M(t)=et2/2, so the CGF is: K(t)=2t2​ All derivatives of order ≥3 vanish identically: K(n)(t)=0 for n≥3. So κn​=0 for all n≥3. In particular κ4​=0, giving κ=0. □

This is the cumulant characterization of the Normal: it is the unique distribution (up to location and scale) whose CGF is a polynomial — specifically, a degree-2 polynomial. All higher cumulants vanish.

Exponential Distribution X∼Expo(λ)

The CGF of Expo(λ) is: K(t)=−log!(1−λt​),t<λ

Differentiating: K′(t)=λ−t1​,K′′(t)=(λ−t)21​,K′′′(t)=(λ−t)32​,K(4)(t)=(λ−t)46​

At t=0: κ2​=1/λ2, κ4​=6/λ4.

κ(X)=(1/λ2)26/λ4​=1/λ46/λ4​=6​

The Exponential has excess kurtosis 6, independent of λ. Heavy-tailed relative to the Normal — those rare large values really do contribute.

Gamma Distribution X∼Gamma(a,λ)

By cumulant additivity (Gamma(a,λ) is the sum of a i.i.d. Expo(λ) variables): κ4​=a⋅λ46​,κ2​=λ2a​

κ(X)=(a/λ2)26a/λ4​=a2/λ46a/λ4​=a6​

κ(Gamma(a,λ))=a6​​

Decays like 1/n — exactly the cumulant additivity prediction. As a→∞, Gamma → Normal and κ→0.

Poisson Distribution X∼Pois(λ)

The CGF is K(t)=λ(et−1). All derivatives at 0 equal λ: κn​=λ for all n≥1.

κ(X)=κ22​κ4​​=λ2λ​=λ1​

κ(Pois(λ))=λ1​​

Again decays like 1/n as λ→∞ (Pois(λ) is the sum of λ i.i.d. Pois(1) variables).

Uniform Distribution X∼Unif(a,b)

E[(X−μ)4]=80(b−a)4​,σ4=144(b−a)4​

Kurt(X)=(b−a)4/144(b−a)4/80​=80144​=59​

κ(Unif(a,b))=59​−3=−56​​

Negative excess kurtosis — the Uniform is platykurtic. Its tails are as light as possible (the distribution has bounded support), so it produces fewer extreme values than the Normal would with the same variance. This makes sense: there are no tails at all beyond [a,b].

Proof of the fourth central moment:
WLOG X∼Unif(−1/2,1/2) (by affine invariance, kurtosis is the same). E[X4]=∫−1/21/2​x4,dx=2∫01/2​x4,dx=2⋅5x5​​01/2​=5⋅322​=801​ And σ2=1/12, so σ4=1/144. Then Kurt=(1/80)/(1/144)=144/80=9/5. □

Bernoulli Distribution X∼Bern(p)

E[(X−μ)4]=p(1−p)4+(1−p)p4=p(1−p)[(1−p)3+p3]

Use the identity (1−p)3+p3=1−3p(1−p): E[(X−μ)4]=p(1−p)(1−3p(1−p))

And σ4=(p(1−p))2. So: Kurt(X)=p2(1−p)2p(1−p)(1−3p(1−p))​=p(1−p)1−3p(1−p)​

κ(X)=p(1−p)1−3p(1−p)​−3=p(1−p)1−3p(1−p)−3p(1−p)​=p(1−p)1−6p(1−p)​

κ(Bern(p))=p(1−p)1​−6​

At p=1/2: κ=1/41​−6=4−6=−2. This is the global minimum of excess kurtosis — consistent with Proposition 2 (Kurt≥1, i.e. κ≥−2).

As p→0 or p→1: κ→∞. The Bernoulli becomes highly kurtotic when one outcome is very rare — the occasional occurrence of the rare event is the “extreme outlier.”

The t-Distribution: Kurtosis in the Wild

The t-distribution with ν degrees of freedom has:

κ(tν​)=ν−46​,ν>4

For ν≤4, the fourth moment does not exist and kurtosis is undefined. For ν>4:

  • As ν→∞: κ→0 (the t converges to Normal).
  • As ν→4+: κ→∞ (tails become so heavy the fourth moment barely exists).

This is why the t-distribution is the canonical example of a heavy-tailed distribution — and why it matters in statistics, where we use it precisely when we’re worried about outliers.

The CLT Through the Lens of Cumulants

For i.i.d. X1​,…,Xn​ with mean μ, variance σ2, let Xˉn​=n1​∑Xi​ (standardized appropriately). The cumulants of the standardized sum satisfy:

κr​!(σn​Sn​−nμ​)=σr⋅nr/2−1κr​(X)​

So:

  • κ1​=0 (centered)
  • κ2​=1 (unit variance)
  • κ3​∼1/n​→0 (skewness vanishes)
  • κ4​∼1/n→0 (excess kurtosis vanishes)
  • Higher cumulants vanish even faster

The Normal has κr​=0 for all r≥3. The CLT says that as n→∞, every cumulant of order ≥3 vanishes, and we’re left with a distribution characterized only by κ1​ and κ2​ — i.e., a Normal. Kurtosis going to zero is part of what it means to converge to normality.

Things I Keep Getting Confused

Honest accounting.

  1. “High kurtosis means a sharp peak.” No. High kurtosis means heavy tails. The peak is irrelevant — the fourth moment is dominated by distant values.

  2. Forgetting that kurtosis of Normal is 3, not 0. When computing, I need Kurt(X)−3 for excess kurtosis. Several times I’ve checked a computation against 0 when I should check against 3.

  3. Kurtosis undefined when E[X4]=∞. The Cauchy distribution has no finite moments at all — kurtosis undefined. The t3​ distribution has finite variance but no fourth moment — kurtosis undefined. Always check existence first.

  4. Confusing “leptokurtic” and “platykurtic.” Mnemonic: lepto = thin (the tails are thin in the center, fat at the extremes) — so leptokurtic = heavy tails. Platy = flat (the distribution is flatter, more spread, lighter tails). Actually I just remember: κ>0 is leptokurtic. That’s enough.
