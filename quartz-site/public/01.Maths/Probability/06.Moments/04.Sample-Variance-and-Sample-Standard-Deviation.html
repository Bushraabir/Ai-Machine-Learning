04.Sample Variance and Sample Standard Deviation

04.Sample Variance and Sample Standard Deviation

Mar 28, 20268 min read

This is one of those topics where the formula is deceptively simple and the justification is genuinely subtle. The n−1 denominator is not a correction pulled from thin air — it comes from a real calculation, and understanding why is worth every minute. I’ve seen people use this formula for years without knowing where it comes from. Don’t be that person.

Setup and Motivation

We have a population with some unknown mean μ and unknown variance σ2. We observe a random sample X1​,X2​,…,Xn​ — i.i.d. draws from this population. We want to estimate σ2 from the data.

The natural first instinct: just compute the average squared deviation from the mean. But we don’t know μ. So we substitute the sample mean:

Xˉn​=n1​∑i=1n​Xi​

This substitution has a cost, and the n−1 is the price we pay for it.

Definitions

Sample mean: Xˉn​=n1​∑i=1n​Xi​

Sample variance: Sn2​=n−11​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2

Sample standard deviation: Sn​=Sn2​​=n−11​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2​

The n−1 in the denominator is called Bessel’s correction. The quantity with n in the denominator — n1​∑(Xi​−Xˉn​)2 — is sometimes called the biased sample variance or the MLE of σ2 under Normality. We will see exactly why n is wrong and n−1 is right.

The Central Theorem: Unbiasedness of Sn2​

Theorem. E[Sn2​]=σ2.

That is, Sn2​ is an unbiased estimator of the population variance σ2.

Proof.

We compute E![∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2] by a standard algebraic trick — adding and subtracting μ:

Xi​−Xˉn​=(Xi​−μ)−(Xˉn​−μ)

Let Zi​=Xi​−μ (centered observations) and Zˉ=Xˉn​−μ. Then:

∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2=∑i=1n​(Zi​−Zˉ)2=∑i=1n​Zi2​−nZˉ2

Justification of the last step: ∑i=1n​(Zi​−Zˉ)2=∑i=1n​Zi2​−2Zˉ∑i=1n​Zi​+nZˉ2=∑i=1n​Zi2​−2nZˉ2+nZˉ2=∑i=1n​Zi2​−nZˉ2

Now take expectations:

E![∑i=1n​Zi2​]=n⋅E[Zi2​]=n⋅E[(Xi​−μ)2]=nσ2

E![nZˉ2]=n⋅E![(Xˉn​−μ)2]=n⋅Var(Xˉn​)=n⋅nσ2​=σ2

Therefore:

E![∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2]=nσ2−σ2=(n−1)σ2

Dividing by n−1:

E[Sn2​]=n−1(n−1)σ2​=σ2□

Why the Biased Version Is Biased

Define the biased sample variance: Sn2​=n1​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2

From the proof above: E[Sn2​]=n(n−1)σ2​=σ2−nσ2​<σ2

So Sn2​ systematically underestimates σ2. The bias is −σ2/n, which vanishes as n→∞ but is real for finite samples.

Intuition for why it underestimates:
We are computing deviations from the sample mean Xˉn​, not from the true mean μ. The sample mean is the point that minimizes the sum of squared deviations from the data — it is by definition closer to the data than μ is (unless Xˉn​=μ, which happens with probability zero for continuous distributions). So deviations from Xˉn​ are systematically smaller than deviations from μ, and we are underestimating σ2.

Dividing by n−1 instead of n inflates the estimate just enough to correct for this.

Degrees of Freedom: The Right Way to Think About n−1

The most conceptually satisfying explanation of n−1 is through degrees of freedom.

The n residuals X1​−Xˉn​,X2​−Xˉn​,…,Xn​−Xˉn​ satisfy the constraint: ∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)=0

This is an identity — always true, for any data. So the n residuals lie in an (n−1)-dimensional subspace of Rn. They have only n−1 degrees of freedom — only n−1 of them can vary freely; the last is determined by the constraint.

We estimated one parameter (μ, replaced by Xˉn​) from n observations, consuming one degree of freedom. We have n−1 left. The sample variance divides by the remaining degrees of freedom.

This generalizes. In linear regression with p predictors (including intercept), the residual variance is n−p1​∑ε^i2​, dividing by n−p degrees of freedom — one lost per estimated parameter. The n−1 here is the special case p=1 (just the mean).

Properties of Xˉn​ and Sn2​

Proposition 1. E[Xˉn​]=μ and Var(Xˉn​)=σ2/n.

Proof. By linearity of expectation and independence: E[Xˉn​]=n1​∑i=1n​E[Xi​]=μ Var(Xˉn​)=n21​∑i=1n​Var(Xi​)=n2nσ2​=nσ2​□

Proposition 2. Xˉn​ and Sn2​ are uncorrelated for any population. They are independent when the population is Normal.

Proof of independence under Normality (sketch).
Write X=(X1​,…,Xn​)T∼N(μ1,σ2I). The sample mean is Xˉn​=aTX for a=n1​1. The residual vector (Xi​−Xˉn​) lives in the orthogonal complement of 1 in Rn. Since jointly Normal, orthogonality implies independence. And Sn2​ is a function of the residuals alone, so Sn2​⊥Xˉn​. □

This independence is far from obvious and is special to the Normal. It underlies the validity of the t-test: the test statistic Sn​/n​Xˉn​−μ​ has a t-distribution precisely because numerator and denominator are independent.

Proposition 3. Under Normality: σ2(n−1)Sn2​​∼χn−12​.

Proof (sketch).
The residual vector (Xi​−Xˉn​) is a Normal vector of dimension n constrained to a (n−1)-dimensional subspace. Its squared norm, scaled by 1/σ2, is a sum of n−1 squared independent standard Normals — i.e., χn−12​.

More precisely: σ2(n−1)Sn2​​=σ21​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2=∑i=1n​(σXi​−Xˉn​​)2∼χn−12​□

This immediately gives: E![σ2(n−1)Sn2​​]=n−1⟹E[Sn2​]=σ2✓

and: Var!(σ2(n−1)Sn2​​)=2(n−1)⟹Var(Sn2​)=n−12σ4​

Sample Standard Deviation Is Biased

Here is a subtle and frequently overlooked point:

Sn​ is not an unbiased estimator of σ, even though Sn2​ is unbiased for σ2.

By Jensen’s inequality, since x↦x​ is concave for x>0: E[Sn​]=E[Sn2​​]<E[Sn2​]​=σ2​=σ

So Sn​ systematically underestimates σ. The bias is of order 1/n and vanishes asymptotically, but it is real.

Under Normality, the exact correction factor is known. Since (n−1)Sn2​/σ2∼χn−12​, we can compute E[Sn​] exactly using the moments of the χ distribution:

E[Sn​]=σ⋅cn​,where cn​=n−12​​⋅Γ((n−1)/2)Γ(n/2)​

An unbiased estimator of σ under Normality is therefore Sn​/cn​. In practice nobody uses this — Sn​ is consistent and the bias is negligible for moderate n. But it’s good to know that unbiasedness of Sn2​ does not pass through the square root.

Consistency

Both estimators are consistent: they converge in probability to the true parameter as n→∞.

Proposition. Sn2​p​σ2 as n→∞.

Proof.
By the Law of Large Numbers: n1​∑i=1n​Xi2​p​E[X2]=σ2+μ2 Xˉn​p​μ⟹Xˉn2​p​μ2

Now use the computational formula: Sn2​=n−1n​(n1​∑i=1n​Xi2​−Xˉn2​)p​1⋅((σ2+μ2)−μ2)=σ2□

Similarly Sn​p​σ.

The Computational Formula

For hand calculation (less numerically stable but instructive):

∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2=∑i=1n​Xi2​−nXˉn2​

Proof. ∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2=∑Xi2​−2Xˉ∑Xi​+nXˉ2=∑Xi2​−2nXˉ2+nXˉ2=∑Xi2​−nXˉ2□

So: Sn2​=n−11​(∑i=1n​Xi2​−nXˉn2​)

⚠️ Numerical warning. This formula is algebraically correct but numerically dangerous for floating-point arithmetic when ∑Xi2​ and nXˉ2 are both large numbers that nearly cancel. Always use the definition n−11​∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2 in code, or Welford’s online algorithm.

The t-Statistic

The canonical application of sample variance: constructing a pivot for inference on μ when σ is unknown.

Under Normality, define the one-sample t-statistic: T=Sn​/n​Xˉn​−μ​

Theorem. T∼tn−1​.

Proof.
Write: T=σ/n​Xˉn​−μ​⋅Sn​/n​σ/n​​=Z⋅Sn​σ​=V/(n−1)​Z​

where:

  • Z=σ/n​Xˉn​−μ​∼N(0,1)
  • V=σ2(n−1)Sn2​​∼χn−12​
  • Z⊥V (by Proposition 2 — independence of Xˉn​ and Sn2​ under Normality)

The ratio Z/V/(n−1)​ is the definition of a tn−1​ random variable. □

This is the reason the t-distribution exists. Without the n−1 in Bessel’s correction — without Sn2​ being built the right way — this pivot would not have a clean distribution and t-tests would not work.

Things I Keep Getting Confused

Honest accounting.

  1. Dividing by n instead of n−1 “just because it’s the sample size.” No. The n−1 is the whole point. The biased version is fine asymptotically but conceptually wrong for finite samples.

  2. Thinking Sn​ is unbiased for σ because Sn2​ is unbiased for σ2. Jensen kills this. Concavity of ⋅​ means E[Sn​]<σ always.

  3. Forgetting that Xˉn​⊥Sn2​ is special to the Normal. For a general population they are uncorrelated but not independent. The t-test technically requires Normality for exact validity.

  4. The degrees of freedom story sounding like a hand-wave. It’s not — it’s a precise statement about the dimension of the subspace in which the residual vector lives. The constraint ∑(Xi​−Xˉn​)=0 is exact, not approximate.

  5. Using the computational formula in floating-point without thinking. Catastrophic cancellation is real. Use the two-pass formula or Welford’s algorithm when implementing.
