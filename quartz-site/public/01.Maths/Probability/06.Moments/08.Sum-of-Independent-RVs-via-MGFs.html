08.Sum of Independent RVs via MGFs

Mar 28, 20266 min read

The MGF is one of those tools that seems like overkill until the day you need it — and then it becomes indispensable. The key insight here is almost embarrassingly clean: independence turns convolution into multiplication.

Why MGFs at all?

Computing the distribution of X+Y directly means convolving the densities (or PMFs). That’s painful. The MGF converts this convolution problem into an algebraic one: independence means the joint factors, and the expectation of a product becomes a product of expectations.

This is the same philosophical move as using Fourier transforms for convolution in signal processing. The MGF is (essentially) a two-sided Laplace transform of the distribution.

The Main Theorem

Theorem. Let X1​,X2​,…,Xn​ be independent random variables with MGFs MX1​​(t),…,MXn​​(t), each finite in some open interval (−h,h) around 0. Define Sn​=X1​+X2​+⋯+Xn​. Then:

MSn​​(t)=i=1∏n​MXi​​(t)​

Proof

This is one of those proofs where, once you see it, you wonder why anyone writes it out. But let’s be careful.

MSn​​(t)=E[etSn​]=E[et(X1​+⋯+Xn​)]=E[∏i=1n​etXi​]

Now I use the key step: for independent random variables, the expectation of a product equals the product of expectations. This is not obvious — it requires independence, not just uncorrelatedness.

Lemma. If X1​,…,Xn​ are independent, then for any Borel functions g1​,…,gn​ (with finite expectations): E[∏i=1n​gi​(Xi​)]=∏i=1n​E[gi​(Xi​)]

Proof sketch. For n=2: independence means the joint density (or PMF) factors as fX1​,X2​​(x1​,x2​)=fX1​​(x1​)fX2​​(x2​). Then: E[g1​(X1​)g2​(X2​)]=∫∫g1​(x1​)g2​(x2​)fX1​​(x1​)fX2​​(x2​),dx1​,dx2​ =(∫g1​(x1​)fX1​​(x1​),dx1​)(∫g2​(x2​)fX2​​(x2​),dx2​)=E[g1​(X1​)]⋅E[g2​(X2​)] Induction handles n>2. □

Applying the lemma with gi​(x)=etx:

MSn​​(t)=∏i=1n​E[etXi​]=∏i=1n​MXi​​(t)□

The Uniqueness Theorem (Critical!)

The above theorem would be useless without this:

Theorem (Uniqueness of MGFs). If two distributions have MGFs that are equal and finite on some open interval (−h,h), then the two distributions are identical.

This is the theorem that lets you “read off” the distribution from the MGF. Without uniqueness, knowing MSn​​ tells you nothing about the law of Sn​.

I always need to remind myself: the uniqueness theorem is non-trivial and its proof goes through the theory of moment problems / analytic functions. Blitzstein & Hwang sketch this; the full proof lives in Billingsley or Durrett. Accept it as a black box for now unless you want to go down that rabbit hole.

Working Examples

1. Sum of Independent Poissons

Let Xi​∼Poisson(λi​), independent. Recall: MX​(t)=eλ(et−1)

Then: MSn​​(t)=∏i=1n​eλi​(et−1)=e(∑i=1n​λi​)(et−1)

This is the MGF of a Poisson(λ1​+⋯+λn​). By uniqueness:

∑i=1n​Xi​∼Poisson!(∑i=1n​λi​)

This is the reproductive property of the Poisson. Makes sense intuitively: merging independent Poisson processes (with rates λi​) gives a Poisson process with rate ∑λi​.

2. Sum of Independent Normals

Let Xi​∼N(μi​,σi2​), independent. Recall: MX​(t)=eμt+21​σ2t2

Then: MSn​​(t)=∏i=1n​eμi​t+21​σi2​t2=e(∑μi​)t+21​(∑σi2​)t2

By uniqueness:

∑i=1n​Xi​∼N!(∑μi​,,∑σi2​)

This is the most important special case. The MGF proof is far cleaner than the density convolution approach.

3. Sum of n i.i.d. Exponentials → Gamma

Let Xi​∼Expo(λ) (rate λ), i.i.d. Recall: MX​(t)=λ−tλ​,t<λ

Then: MSn​​(t)=(λ−tλ​)n

This is exactly the MGF of a Gamma(n,λ) distribution. So:

X1​+⋯+Xn​∼Gamma(n,λ)

This is how you prove that a Gamma is the sum of i.i.d. Exponentials — the “waiting time for the n-th arrival in a Poisson process” interpretation made rigorous.

4. Sum of Independent Gammas (same rate)

More generally: Xi​∼Gamma(ai​,λ), independent, same rate λ. Then:

MSn​​(t)=∏i=1n​(λ−tλ​)ai​=(λ−tλ​)∑ai​

So Sn​∼Gamma(∑ai​,λ). The Gamma is also reproductive — in its shape parameter, holding rate fixed.

⚠️ Watch out: This breaks down if the rates differ. Gamma(a1​,λ1​)+Gamma(a2​,λ2​) with λ1​=λ2​ does not have a closed-form Gamma distribution. The MGF is (λ1​−tλ1​​)a1​(λ2​−tλ2​​)a2​, which doesn’t simplify to a standard family.

Cumulant Generating Function (a cleaner object)

Define the CGF: KX​(t)=logMX​(t).

Then independence gives:

KSn​​(t)=∑i=1n​KXi​​(t)

The CGF turns products into sums, and its derivatives at 0 give the cumulants: κ1​=E[X], κ2​=Var(X), κ3​=E[(X−μ)3], etc.

So the fact that Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y) (for independent X,Y) is just KX+Y′′​(0)=KX′′​(0)+KY′′​(0), which is obvious from the sum formula.

Pitfalls and Subtleties

1. MGF may not exist.
The MGF MX​(t)=E[etX] can be infinite. For example, the log-normal distribution has no finite MGF at any t>0. In those cases, the characteristic function φX​(t)=E[eitX] always exists (since ∣eitX∣=1), and the same product formula holds for sums of independent RVs. This is the basis of the proof of the CLT.

2. Independence is essential.
The product formula MX+Y​(t)=MX​(t)MY​(t) requires independence. For general X,Y: MX+Y​(t)=E[et(X+Y)], and this does not factor unless X⊥Y.

3. Domain must overlap.
Each MXi​​(t) must be finite in a common open interval containing 0. If the domains don’t overlap, the product isn’t valid.

Connection to the CLT

The MGF/CF approach is the clean path to the Central Limit Theorem. If Xi​ are i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ2, standardize:

Zn​=σn​Sn​−nμ​

Then MZn​​(t)→et2/2 as n→∞ — the MGF of N(0,1). The machinery behind this is a Taylor expansion of logMX​(t/σn​) around 0. See the CLT note.
