03.Box Muller Transformation

03.Box Muller Transformation

Apr 03, 20268 min read

Big Picture The Box-Muller transform is the answer to a question that sounds impossible: how do you generate Normal random variables from uniform ones? Computers can only generate Uniform(0,1) at the hardware level. Everything else — Exponential, Gamma, Normal — must be constructed. Box-Muller is the elegant, geometric solution to the Normal problem. It uses the polar coordinate Jacobian to escape the Gaussian integral, and produces two independent Normals for the price of two Uniforms. Understanding it means understanding probability transforms at a deep level.

1. The Problem — Why This Is Hard

We want to simulate Z∼N(0,1).

The CDF of the Normal is Φ(z)=∫−∞z​2π​1​e−t2/2,dt. There is no closed form for Φ−1 — so the standard inverse-CDF method breaks down. We cannot simply write Z=Φ−1(U) and compute it in closed form.

We need a different strategy.

Personal note: This is the moment where probability theory and computation meet in a genuinely beautiful way. The trick isn’t brute force — it’s geometry. Two Uniforms, polar coordinates, and the magical cancellation of 2π in the Gaussian integral. The first time I saw this I stopped and re-read it three times.

2. The Key Ingredient — The Gaussian Integral in Polar Coordinates

The reason the Normal is hard to invert is that e−z2/2 has no elementary antiderivative. But the square of the Gaussian integral does:

(∫−∞∞​e−x2/2,dx)2=∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​e−(x2+y2)/2,dx,dy

Switch to polar coordinates x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, with Jacobian r:

=∫02π​∫0∞​e−r2/2⋅r,dr,dθ=2π∫0∞​re−r2/2,dr

The inner integral: let u=r2/2, du=r,dr:

∫0∞​re−r2/2,dr=∫0∞​e−u,du=1

So the squared integral is 2π, and the Gaussian integral is 2π​. This is the normalizing constant of N(0,1).

The crucial observation: when we write e−(x2+y2)/2=e−r2/2, the two-dimensional Gaussian factors beautifully in polar coordinates. The angle θ disappears entirely. This is the geometric miracle that Box-Muller exploits.

3. The Setup — Two Independent Standard Normals

Let Z1​,Z2​∼iidN(0,1). Their joint PDF is:

fZ1​,Z2​​(z1​,z2​)=2π1​e−(z12​+z22​)/2

Now convert to polar: R=Z12​+Z22​​, Θ=arctan(Z2​/Z1​).

From the Jacobian calculation in Jacobian Formula — Change of Variables:

fR,Θ​(r,θ)=2π1​e−r2/2⋅r,r>0,;θ∈[0,2π)

This factors:

=fR​(r)(re−r2/2)​​⋅fΘ​(θ)2π1​​​

So R and Θ are independent, with:

Θ∼Uniform(0,2π)

fR​(r)=re−r2/2,r>0(Rayleigh distribution)

4. Identifying the Distribution of R2

Let V=R2=Z12​+Z22​. Apply the Jacobian: r=v​, dvdr​=2v​1​.

fV​(v)=fR​(v​)⋅2v​1​=v​,e−v/2⋅2v​1​=21​e−v/2,v>0

This is Exponential(1/2), equivalently Gamma(1,1/2), equivalently χ2(2).

And the Exponential distribution has a closed-form inverse CDF:

V∼Exp(1/2)⟹FV​(v)=1−e−v/2

FV−1​(u)=−2log(1−u)

Since U∼Uniform(0,1) implies 1−U∼Uniform(0,1), we can write:

V=d−2logU1​

for U1​∼Uniform(0,1).

5. The Box-Muller Transform — The Full Algorithm

We now have everything. Given U1​,U2​∼iidUniform(0,1):

Set:

R=−2logU1​​,Θ=2πU2​

Then define:

Z1​=RcosΘ=−2logU1​​cos(2πU2​)​ Z2​=RsinΘ=−2logU1​​sin(2πU2​)​

Claim: Z1​,Z2​∼iidN(0,1).

6. Proof That It Works

We need to verify that (Z1​,Z2​) has joint PDF 2π1​e−(z12​+z22​)/2.

Step 1. We have U1​,U2​∼iidUniform(0,1), so:

fU1​,U2​​(u1​,u2​)=1,(u1​,u2​)∈(0,1)2

Step 2. The transformation (U1​,U2​)↦(R,Θ) is:

R=−2logU1​​,Θ=2πU2​

These are independent by construction (R depends only on U1​, Θ only on U2​), and:

R∼Rayleigh,Θ∼Uniform(0,2π)

so fR,Θ​(r,θ)=re−r2/2⋅2π1​.

Step 3. The transformation (R,Θ)↦(Z1​,Z2​) via z1​=rcosθ, z2​=rsinθ has Jacobian determinant r (computed in Jacobian Formula — Change of Variables). This is the forward Jacobian, so for the inverse direction ∣J−1∣=1/r:

fZ1​,Z2​​(z1​,z2​)=fR,Θ​(r,θ)⋅r1​=re−r2/2⋅2π1​⋅r1​=2π1​e−r2/2

where r=z12​+z22​​, so r2=z12​+z22​:

fZ1​,Z2​​(z1​,z2​)=2π1​e−(z12​+z22​)/2=2π​1​e−z12​/2⋅2π​1​e−z22​/2

The joint PDF factors — so Z1​⊥Z2​ — and each marginal is N(0,1). ■

7. The Logical Flow — End to End

It is worth laying out the complete chain of reasoning in one place:

what a computer gives usU1​,U2​∼Uniform(0,1)​​

↓V=−2logU1​∼Exp(1/2)=χ2(2)(inverse CDF)

↓R=V​∼Rayleigh(Jacobian)

↓Θ=2πU2​∼Uniform(0,2π)(linear scaling)

↓(R,Θ) independent, polar representation of (Z1​,Z2​)

↓Z1​=RcosΘ,;Z2​=RsinΘ∼N(0,1) independent(Jacobian, reverse)

Each arrow is a justified probability transform. Nothing is magic — just the Jacobian, the inverse CDF, and the structure of the Gaussian in polar coordinates.

8. Why Two Normals Come Out

One of the aesthetically satisfying features: two Uniforms → two independent Normals. This is not a coincidence. The two-dimensional Gaussian is rotationally symmetric — it depends only on r=z12​+z22​​, not on θ. This symmetry decouples the angle from the radius, and the angle becomes a free Uniform on [0,2π). The two Normals are the Cartesian coordinates of a point generated by:

  1. Picking a radius from the Rayleigh distribution (determined by U1​)
  2. Picking a random direction uniformly (determined by U2​)

The two coordinates of that random point are the two Normals. Geometrically: we are placing a point uniformly on the “probability mass” of the 2D Gaussian — not uniformly in area, but with density 2π1​e−r2/2.

9. Generating N(μ,σ2)

Once we have Z1​,Z2​∼iidN(0,1), we get any Normal by the location-scale transform:

Xi​=μ+σZi​∼N(μ,σ2)

Two Uniforms give two N(μ,σ2) samples. The algorithm is maximally efficient in this sense.

10. Numerical Stability — The Practical Note

In implementation, U1​ must be strictly positive (since log(0)=−∞). In practice: draw U1​∼Uniform(0,1) and if U1​=0 (probability zero but possible in floating point), resample. Most implementations draw from (0,1) open interval directly.

The trigonometric functions cos and sin are computationally expensive. The Marsaglia polar method (a rejection-sampling variant) avoids them, instead using the identity cosθ=x/r, sinθ=y/r for a uniformly sampled point (x,y) in the unit disk. It is faster in practice but conceptually less clean. Worth knowing it exists; worth understanding Box-Muller first.

12. Worked Numerical Example

One Draw from Box-Muller

Suppose the computer generates U1​=0.23, U2​=0.71.

Step 1: Compute R. R=−2log(0.23)​=−2×(−1.4697)​=2.9393​≈1.7144

Step 2: Compute Θ. Θ=2π×0.71≈4.4610 radians

Step 3: Compute Z1​,Z2​. Z1​=1.7144×cos(4.4610)≈1.7144×(−0.2675)≈−0.4586 Z2​=1.7144×sin(4.4610)≈1.7144×(−0.9636)≈−1.6519

Both are draws from N(0,1). Verify sanity: both are within 2 standard deviations, which covers ~95% of the distribution. Looks right.

13. Connections to Other Topics

  • Jacobian Formula — Change of Variables: The entire transform rests on the polar coordinate Jacobian. The factor of r in fR,Θ​ is what makes R2∼Exp(1/2) and allows the inverse CDF step.
  • Chi-Squared Distribution: R2=Z12​+Z22​∼χ2(2). The sum of squares of k independent standard Normals is χ2(k). Box-Muller gives you k=2 for free.
  • Exponential Distribution: The inverse CDF −2logU∼Exp(1/2) is the standard exponential generation trick. Memorize: −λ−1logU∼Exp(λ).
  • Multivariate Normal Distribution: To generate X∼N(μ,Σ): generate Z=(Z1​,…,Zn​) via Box-Muller, then set X=μ+LZ where LL⊤=Σ is the Cholesky decomposition.
  • Simulation — Inverse CDF Method: Box-Muller is a special case of transform methods, combining the inverse CDF (for R2) with a geometric insight (polar coordinates) to handle the case where Φ−1 has no closed form.
  • Rayleigh Distribution: R=Z12​+Z22​​ where Z1​,Z2​∼iidN(0,1) is Rayleigh. This appears in signal processing — the amplitude of a 2D Gaussian noise vector.
