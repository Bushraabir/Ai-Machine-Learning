04.Delta Method

04.Delta Method

Apr 03, 20269 min read

Big Picture The Delta Method answers one question: if n​(θ^−θ)d​N(0,σ2), what is the asymptotic distribution of g(θ^)? It is the workhorse of asymptotic statistics — every time you transform an estimator (take its log, square it, take a ratio), you need the Delta Method to propagate uncertainty through that transformation. It is Taylor expansion dressed in probability theory clothing.

1. The Problem — Why We Need This

Suppose we have an estimator θ^n​ that is asymptotically Normal:

n​(θ^n​−θ)d​N(0,σ2)

Now we care about g(θ^n​) for some smooth function g. What is its asymptotic distribution?

The naive wrong answer: ”g(θ^n​) is approximately N(g(θ),σ2).”

Wrong on two counts — the variance is not simply σ2, and the centering requires care. The transformation g distorts both the center and the spread. The Delta Method tells us exactly how.

Personal note: I first encountered this when trying to find the asymptotic distribution of the sample coefficient of variation σ^/Xˉ. You can’t just say “it’s Normal with variance σ2/n” — you have to propagate through the ratio function. That’s the Delta Method. Once I had the need, the formula became obvious.

2. Heuristic Derivation — Taylor Expansion

The idea is disarmingly simple. If g is differentiable at θ, Taylor expand around θ:

g(θ^n​)=g(θ)+g′(θ)(θ^n​−θ)+21​g′′(θ)(θ^n​−θ)2+⋯

When θ^n​→θ (which it does, by consistency), the higher-order terms are op​(∣θ^n​−θ∣) and vanish asymptotically. So:

g(θ^n​)−g(θ)≈g′(θ)(θ^n​−θ)

Multiply both sides by n​:

n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))≈g′(θ)⋅n​(θ^n​−θ)

Since n​(θ^n​−θ)d​N(0,σ2), and g′(θ) is a constant:

g′(θ)⋅n​(θ^n​−θ)d​N(0,,[g′(θ)]2σ2)

3. The Delta Method — Statement

Delta Method Let θ^n​ be a sequence of estimators satisfying: n​(θ^n​−θ)d​N(0,σ2) Let g:R→R be differentiable at θ with g′(θ)=0. Then: n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))d​N(0,,[g′(θ)]2σ2)

Equivalently, in the language of approximate distributions:

g(θ^n​)∼⋅N!(g(θ),,n[g′(θ)]2σ2​)

The punchline in one line:

Var(g(θ^))≈[g′(θ)]2⋅Var(θ^)​

The variance gets multiplied by the square of the derivative. This is the propagation of uncertainty formula from physics, made rigorous.

4. Rigorous Proof — Via Slutsky and CMT

The heuristic is convincing but hand-wavy. Here is the clean proof.

Setup. Write θ^n​−θ=n​1​⋅n​(θ^n​−θ). Since n​(θ^n​−θ)=Op​(1), we have θ^n​−θ=Op​(n−1/2)→0 in probability. So θ^n​p​θ.

Taylor expansion with remainder. By the mean value theorem, there exists θ~n​ between θ^n​ and θ such that:

g(θ^n​)−g(θ)=g′(θ~n​)(θ^n​−θ)

Therefore:

n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))=g′(θ~n​)⋅n​(θ^n​−θ)

Convergence of g′(θ~n​). Since θ~n​ is squeezed between θ^n​ and θ, and θ^n​p​θ, we get θ~n​p​θ. By continuity of g′ at θ:

g′(θ~n​)p​g′(θ)

Apply Slutsky’s Theorem. We have:

n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))=p​g′(θ)g′(θ~n​)​​⋅d​N(0,σ2)n​(θ^n​−θ)​​

By Slutsky: if An​p​a and Bn​d​B, then An​Bn​d​aB. Therefore:

n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))d​g′(θ)⋅N(0,σ2)=N(0,,[g′(θ)]2σ2)■

Personal note: The mean value theorem makes this rigorous — no sloppy “higher order terms vanish.” The remainder is exactly g′(θ~n​), not an approximation, and Slutsky handles the convergence cleanly. This proof is the one to write in an exam.

5. The Case g′(θ)=0 — Second-Order Delta Method

When g′(θ)=0, the first-order term vanishes and the leading term is second order. The usual Delta Method gives the degenerate result N(0,0) — useless.

Go to second order in the Taylor expansion:

g(θ^n​)−g(θ)≈21​g′′(θ)(θ^n​−θ)2

Multiply by n:

n(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))≈2g′′(θ)​⋅n(θ^n​−θ)2=2g′′(θ)​⋅[n​(θ^n​−θ)]2

Since n​(θ^n​−θ)d​N(0,σ2), we have [n​(θ^n​−θ)]2d​σ2χ2(1). Therefore:

n(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))d​2g′′(θ)σ2​⋅χ2(1)​

The rate of convergence changes from n​ to n, and the limiting distribution is no longer Normal — it is a scaled Chi-Squared. This is a genuinely different regime.

Personal note: This case is subtle and easy to miss. If you’re ever computing the Delta Method and the derivative vanishes at the true parameter value, stop — the whole first-order analysis collapses. You need the second-order method, and your confidence intervals based on the N(0,[g′(θ)]2σ2) approximation are wrong.

6. The Multivariate Delta Method

Let θ^n​∈Rk satisfy:

n​(θ^n​−θ)d​N(0,Σ)

Let g:Rk→R be differentiable at θ. Define the gradient vector:

∇g(θ)=(∂θ1​∂g​,…,∂θk​∂g​)⊤

Then:

n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))d​N!(0,;∇g(θ)⊤Σ,∇g(θ))​

The asymptotic variance is the quadratic form ∇g⊤Σ∇g.

For g:Rk→Rm (vector-valued g): Define the m×k Jacobian matrix Dg(θ) with (i,j)-entry ∂gi​/∂θj​. Then:

n​(g(θ^n​)−g(θ))d​N!(0,;Dg(θ),Σ,Dg(θ)⊤)

The 1D case is the special case k=m=1: Dg=g′(θ), Σ=σ2, giving [g′(θ)]2σ2.

7. Variance Stabilizing Transformations

One of the most powerful applications of the Delta Method is designing g to make the asymptotic variance constant — independent of θ. This is called a variance stabilizing transformation (VST).

Goal: Find g such that [g′(θ)]2σ2(θ)=c (a constant).

This requires:

g′(θ)=σ(θ)const​⟹g(θ)=∫σ(θ)1​,dθ

Example: Binomial Proportion

p^​=X/n where X∼Binomial(n,p). Then p^​ satisfies:

n​(p^​−p)d​N(0,p(1−p))

Here σ2(p)=p(1−p), so σ(p)=p(1−p)​. The VST requires:

g(p)=∫p(1−p)​dp​=2arcsin(p​)

This is the arcsine transformation. By the Delta Method:

g′(p)=p(1−p)​1​,[g′(p)]2⋅p(1−p)=1

n​(2arcsin(p^​​)−2arcsin(p​))d​N(0,1)

The asymptotic variance is exactly 1, independent of p. This is why the arcsine transformation is used in meta-analysis and whenever p is close to 0 or 1 (where the Normal approximation to the Binomial is worst).

Example: Poisson Rate

Xˉn​ from a Poisson(λ) sample: n​(Xˉn​−λ)d​N(0,λ).

VST: g(λ)=∫λ​dλ​=2λ​.

n​(2Xˉn​​−2λ​)d​N(0,1)

Square root stabilizes the Poisson. Used routinely in count data analysis.

8. Application — Asymptotic Confidence Intervals

The Delta Method gives a principled way to build CIs for g(θ) from a CI for θ.

If θ^n​∼⋅N(θ,σ2/n), then by the Delta Method:

g(θ^n​)∼⋅N!(g(θ),;n[g′(θ)]2σ2​)

An approximate 95% CI for g(θ) is:

g(θ^n​)±1.96⋅n​∣g′(θ^n​)∣⋅σ^​

where we plug in θ^n​ for the unknown θ in g′(θ) — justified by the continuous mapping theorem.

Personal note: In practice, g′(θ) is unknown. We estimate it at θ^n​. This is consistent because θ^n​p​θ and g′ is continuous. Don’t be sloppy here — write “by CMT, g′(θ^n​)p​g′(θ), so plugging in θ^n​ is justified.”

9. Application — MLE and the Fisher Information

One of the canonical settings: the MLE θ^MLE​ satisfies (under regularity conditions):

n​(θ^MLE​−θ)d​N(0,I(θ)−1)

where I(θ)=−E[∂2logf(X;θ)/∂θ2] is the Fisher information.

By the Delta Method, for any smooth g:

n​(g(θ^MLE​)−g(θ))d​N!(0,;I(θ)[g′(θ)]2​)

This is the invariance of the MLE combined with the Delta Method — the MLE of g(θ) is g(θ^MLE​), and its asymptotic variance follows from the Delta Method. The asymptotic Cramér-Rao bound for g(θ) is [g′(θ)]2/I(θ), and the MLE achieves it. See Fisher Information and Cramér-Rao.

10. Worked Examples

Log Transformation of a Sample Mean

X1​,…,Xn​∼iid some distribution with mean μ>0 and variance σ2. Let g(μ)=logμ.

By CLT: n​(Xˉn​−μ)d​N(0,σ2).

g′(μ)=1/μ, so [g′(μ)]2=1/μ2.

Delta Method: n​(logXˉn​−logμ)d​N!(0,;μ2σ2​)

The asymptotic variance is σ2/μ2 — the squared coefficient of variation. Makes sense: relative precision of Xˉn​ is what determines precision of logXˉn​.

Ratio of Two Sample Means

X1​,…,Xn​∼iid(μX​,σX2​) and Y1​,…,Yn​∼iid(μY​,σY2​), independent. We want the asymptotic distribution of θ^=Xˉn​/Yˉn​.

Let θ=(μX​,μY​)⊤, g(μX​,μY​)=μX​/μY​.

By CLT and independence: n​((Xˉn​ Yˉn​​)−(μX​ μY​​))d​N!(0,(σX2​​0 0​σY2​​))

Gradient of g: ∇g=(1/μY​ −μX​/μY2​​)

Asymptotic variance via multivariate Delta Method: ∇g⊤Σ∇g=μY2​σX2​​+μY4​μX2​σY2​​=μY2​1​(σX2​+μY2​μX2​​σY2​)

Result: n​(Yˉn​Xˉn​​−μY​μX​​)d​N!(0,;μY2​σX2​​+μY4​μX2​σY2​​)

This is the standard result for the ratio estimator — used in survey sampling, econometrics, and relative risk estimation.

12. Connections to Other Topics

  • Central Limit Theorem: The Delta Method presupposes asymptotic Normality of θ^n​. The CLT is what typically provides this — the Delta Method is one step downstream.
  • Slutsky’s Theorem: The rigorous proof of the Delta Method uses Slutsky. Know both.
  • Continuous Mapping Theorem: Used to justify plugging in θ^n​ for θ in g′(θ) when constructing confidence intervals.
  • Fisher Information and Cramér-Rao: The Delta Method + MLE asymptotics gives the asymptotic efficiency of g(θ^MLE​).
  • Jacobian Formula — Change of Variables: The Delta Method is the asymptotic/distributional counterpart of the Jacobian formula. The Jacobian gives the exact distribution of g(X); the Delta Method gives the approximate distribution for large n.
  • Bootstrap: The Bootstrap is an alternative to the Delta Method when g is complex or g′ is hard to compute. They should give the same answer asymptotically under standard conditions.
