06.Geometric Interpretation of Conditional Expectation

06.Geometric Interpretation of Conditional Expectation

Apr 24, 20269 min read

This is the note that reframes everything. Once you see conditional expectation as orthogonal projection in a Hilbert space, every identity we’ve proved — Adam’s Law, Eve’s Law, the regression formula, the Tower Property — becomes a geometric theorem. The algebra didn’t lie, but the geometry explains why it had to come out that way. I wish I had this picture from day one.

0. The Space

Define the space:

L2=X:Ω→R∣E[X2]<∞

Elements of L2 are random variables with finite second moment. We give this space an inner product:

⟨X,Y⟩=E[XY]

Verification that this is an inner product:

  • Symmetry: ⟨X,Y⟩=E[XY]=E[YX]=⟨Y,X⟩ ✓
  • Linearity: ⟨aX+bZ,Y⟩=E[(aX+bZ)Y]=aE[XY]+bE[ZY] ✓
  • Non-negativity: ⟨X,X⟩=E[X2]≥0 ✓
  • Definiteness: ⟨X,X⟩=0⟺E[X2]=0⟺X=0 a.s. ✓

(Technically we work with equivalence classes of a.s.-equal RVs to make definiteness exact — but don’t lose sleep over this.)

The induced norm is:

∣X∣=⟨X,X⟩​=E[X2]​

This is the L2 norm, or the root mean square. With this structure, L2 is a Hilbert space — a complete inner product space. Infinite-dimensional, but all the Euclidean geometry you know works here.

1. Orthogonality

Two random variables X,Y∈L2 are orthogonal if:

X⊥2​Y⟺⟨X,Y⟩=0⟺E[XY]=0

This is a statement about second moments — not the same as independence (X⊥Y). Independence implies orthogonality of centred variables, but not conversely.

Centering and orthogonality. It is often more natural to work with centred variables. Define:

X~=X−E[X],Y~=Y−E[Y]

Then:

⟨X~,Y~⟩=E[X~Y~]=Cov(X,Y)

So Cov(X,Y)=0 (uncorrelatedness) is orthogonality of the centred versions in L2.

The norm of a centred variable:

∣X~∣2=E[X~2]=Var(X)

Standard deviation is the L2 norm of the centred variable. Correlation is the cosine of the angle between the centred variables:

ρ(X,Y)=SD(X),SD(Y)Cov(X,Y)​=∣X~∣∣Y~∣⟨X~,Y~⟩​=cosθ

where θ is the angle between X~ and Y~ in L2. This is exactly the formula for cosine in Euclidean geometry. Correlation is cosine.

ρ=1: vectors point the same direction (Y is a positive linear function of X). ρ=−1: opposite directions. ρ=0: right angle — uncorrelated, orthogonal centred variables. The ∣ρ∣≤1 bound is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: ∣cosθ∣≤1.

2. Projection

Let V⊆L2 be a closed subspace — think of it as a “flat” (a hyperplane, a line, a plane through the origin) inside the Hilbert space.

Theorem (Projection). For any Y∈L2, there exists a unique element Y^∈V such that:

∣Y−Y^∣=minZ∈V​∣Y−Z∣

This Y^ is the orthogonal projection of Y onto V. It is characterised by the orthogonality condition:

Y−Y^⊥2​Zfor all Z∈V

i.e. E[(Y−Y^)Z]=0 for all Z∈V.

Why this characterises it. For any Z∈V:

∣Y−Z∣2=∣Y−Y^+Y^−Z∣2=∣Y−Y^∣2+∣Y^−Z∣2+2⟨Y−Y^,Y^−Z⟩

The cross term vanishes by the orthogonality condition (Y−Y^⊥2​Y^−Z since Y^−Z∈V). So:

∣Y−Z∣2=∣Y−Y^∣2+∣Y^−Z∣2≥∣Y−Y^∣2

with equality iff Z=Y^. This is the Pythagorean theorem in L2. ■

3. Conditional Expectation IS Projection

Theorem. E[Y∣X] is the orthogonal projection of Y onto:

VX​=g(X):g measurable,E[g(X)2]<∞

the subspace of all (square-integrable) functions of X.

Proof. We need to show E[Y∣X]∈VX​ and Y−E[Y∣X]⊥2​Z for all Z∈VX​.

The first part is clear: E[Y∣X]=g(X) for g(x)=E[Y∣X=x], so it’s in VX​.

For the second: take any Z=h(X)∈VX​. Then:

E[(Y−E[Y∣X])h(X)]=E[Yh(X)]−E[E[Y∣X]h(X)]

For the second term, use “taking out what is known”:

E[E[Y∣X]h(X)]=E[E[Yh(X)∣X]]=E[Yh(X)]

So E[(Y−E[Y∣X])h(X)]=0 for all h(X)∈VX​ — the residual is orthogonal to the subspace. ■

The “taking out what is known” property from Conditional Expectation is doing all the work. Geometrically it’s saying: projections are self-adjoint. The projection of Y onto VX​, dotted against anything in VX​, equals Y dotted against that thing.

4. Every Identity as Geometry

4.1 Adam’s Law: Projection Preserves Mass

E[E[Y∣X]]=E[Y]

Geometrically. The constant random variable 1∈VX​ (since 1=g(X) with g≡1). Take h(X)=1 in the orthogonality condition:

E[(Y−E[Y∣X])⋅1]=0⟹E[Y]=E[E[Y∣X]]

Adam’s Law is orthogonality with respect to the constant vector 1. ■

4.2 Eve’s Law: The Pythagorean Theorem

Var(Y)=E[Var(Y∣X)]+Var(E[Y∣X])

Geometrically. Work with centred Y~=Y−E[Y]. Its projection onto VX​ (centred functions of X) is E[Y∣X]​=E[Y∣X]−E[Y].

The Pythagorean theorem in L2 says:

∣Y~∣2=∣Y~−proj∣2+∣proj∣2

Var(Y)E[Y~2]​​=E[Var(Y∣X)]E[(Y−E[Y∣X])2]​​+Var(E[Y∣X])E[(E[Y∣X]−E[Y])2]​​

Eve’s Law is the Pythagorean theorem. ■

        Y
       /|
      / |
     /  |  ← residual Y - Ŷ (orthogonal to V_X)
    /   |
   /    |
  Ŷ----+-------- V_X
  ↑
  E[Y|X] = projection

The hypotenuse is Y~ (total variation). The leg along VX​ is E[Y∣X]−E[Y] (explained variation). The leg perpendicular to VX​ is the residual (unexplained variation).

4.3 Tower Property: Projection of a Projection

E[E[Y∣X,Z]∣Z]=E[Y∣Z]

Geometrically. Let VX,Z​ be functions of (X,Z) and VZ​ be functions of Z alone. Clearly VZ​⊆VX,Z​.

Projecting Y onto the larger subspace VX,Z​ gives E[Y∣X,Z]. Now project that onto the smaller subspace VZ​. The result is the same as projecting Y directly onto VZ​:

projVZ​​(projVX,Z​​(Y))=projVZ​​(Y)

This is the idempotency of nested projections — a standard theorem in Hilbert spaces. The Tower Property is exactly this. ■

In R3: projecting a vector onto a plane, then projecting that result onto a line within the plane, gives the same answer as projecting the original vector directly onto the line. Draw this. It’s obvious in 3D, and the same fact holds in L2.

4.4 Linear Regression: Projection onto a Line

From 01.Linear Regression, the optimal linear predictor Y^=a+bX minimises E[(Y−a−bX)2].

Geometrically: restrict the projection to the subspace Vlin​=a+bX:a,b∈R⊂VX​, a two-dimensional subspace (spanned by 1 and X).

The slope formula b=Cov(X,Y)/Var(X)=⟨Y~,X~⟩/∣X~∣2 is exactly the formula for the projection coefficient onto X~ in L2 — just like projecting a vector v onto a vector u gives coefficient ⟨v,u⟩/∣u∣2.

The minimum MSE∗=Var(Y)(1−ρ2) is the squared length of the residual after projection — by the Pythagorean theorem in Vlin​.

∣Y~∣2=∣Y~^∣2+∣Y~−Y~^∣2 Var(Y)=ρ2,Var(Y)+(1−ρ2),Var(Y)

R2=ρ2=cos2θ where θ is the angle between Y~ and X~.

5. Cauchy-Schwarz is the Cosine Bound

In Euclidean space: ∣u⋅v∣≤∣u∣∣v∣ because ∣cosθ∣≤1.

In L2: exactly the same.

∣⟨X,Y⟩∣≤∣X∣∣Y∣⟺∣E[XY]∣≤E[X2]​E[Y2]​

Applied to centred variables: ∣Cov(X,Y)∣≤SD(X),SD(Y), i.e. ∣ρ∣≤1.

Proof in L2. For any t∈R:

0≤∣X−tY∣2=∣X∣2−2t⟨X,Y⟩+t2∣Y∣2

This is a non-negative quadratic in t, so its discriminant is ≤0:

4⟨X,Y⟩2−4∣X∣2∣Y∣2≤0■

Equality holds iff X=tY a.s. — i.e. one is a scalar multiple of the other, meaning ρ=±1.

7. What the Geometry Cannot Tell You

The L2 geometry captures second-moment information: means, variances, covariances, correlations, linear regression. It does not capture:

  • Higher moments (skewness, kurtosis)
  • Tail behaviour
  • The actual conditional distribution fY∣X​ (only its mean E[Y∣X])
  • Independence (only uncorrelatedness / orthogonality)

For the bivariate normal, the second-moment picture is the whole picture — because the normal is entirely characterised by its first two moments. That’s why the bivariate normal is the natural home for all of this geometry.

Open Questions

  • Make the subspace VX​ precise as a closed subspace of L2 — why is it closed? (Completeness of L2 + measurability)
  • The projection theorem requires V to be a closed subspace. Why does VX​=g(X) qualify? What goes wrong if it isn’t closed?
  • Gram-Schmidt in L2: orthogonalise 1,X,X2,… to get orthogonal polynomials (Hermite polynomials for the Gaussian measure). This is the beginning of polynomial regression and spectral methods
  • For (X,Y) bivariate normal: the projection of Y onto VX​ equals the projection onto Vlin​ — they coincide because the conditional expectation is exactly linear. Characterise all distributions where this holds
  • Martingales revisited: (Mn​)=(E[Y∣Fn​]) is a sequence of nested projections converging to Y. The L2 convergence Mn​→Y in norm is Lévy’s theorem — prove it using the completeness of L2
