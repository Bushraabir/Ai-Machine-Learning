05.Joint PDF

05.Joint PDF

Mar 28, 20266 min read

The joint PDF is not a probability — it’s a density. The probability lives in the volume under the surface, and that’s everything. Internalize this and the rest follows.

Motivation

In the discrete case, the joint PMF assigns actual probabilities to individual points: P(X=x,Y=y). In the continuous case, individual points have probability zero. The right object is instead a function fX,Y​ such that probabilities of regions are computed by integrating fX,Y​ over those regions.

The passage from discrete to continuous is just: sums become integrals, PMF becomes PDF. The conceptual structure is identical.

Definition

A pair of continuous random variables (X,Y) has joint PDF fX,Y​:R2→R if for every (reasonable) region A⊆R2,

P((X,Y)∈A)=∬A​fX,Y​(x,y),dx,dy.

In particular, for a rectangle:

P(a≤X≤b,;c≤Y≤d)=∫ab​∫cd​fX,Y​(x,y),dy,dx.

Valid Joint PDF: The Two Conditions

fX,Y​ is a valid joint PDF if and only if:

  1. Nonnegativity: fX,Y​(x,y)≥0 for all (x,y)∈R2.

  2. Normalisation: ∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​fX,Y​(x,y),dx,dy=1.

That’s it. The “probability” of any event is then recovered by integrating over the corresponding region. Note that fX,Y​(x,y) itself can exceed 1 — it’s a density, not a probability. What matters is that it integrates to 1.

Marginal PDFs

Exactly parallel to the discrete case, marginalizing means integrating out the unwanted variable:

fX​(x)=∫−∞∞​fX,Y​(x,y),dy​

fY​(y)=∫−∞∞​fX,Y​(x,y),dx.

The proof that fX​ is a valid PDF: nonnegativity is clear, and

∫−∞∞​fX​(x),dx=∫−∞∞​∫−∞∞​fX,Y​(x,y),dy,dx=1

by Fubini (all terms nonneg, so interchange is justified). Exactly the same argument as in the discrete case, just with Fubini instead of Tonelli.

Independence

(X,Y) are independent (as continuous r.v.’s) iff

fX,Y​(x,y)=fX​(x),fY​(y)for all (x,y).

Practical check: can you write fX,Y​(x,y)=g(x)⋅h(y) for some functions g,h, and the support is a rectangle (does not depend on x or y jointly)? If yes, X⊥Y, and then g and h are automatically proportional to fX​ and fY​.

The support condition is easy to forget. If the support is a triangle like 0<x<y<1, the joint does not factor no matter what f looks like — knowing Y tells you something about the range of X, so they cannot be independent.

Worked Example 1: Uniform on the Unit Square

fX,Y​(x,y)=1,0<x<1,;0<y<1.

Marginals:

fX​(x)=∫01​1,dy=1,0<x<1.

So X∼Uniform(0,1), and by symmetry Y∼Uniform(0,1).

Independence: fX,Y​(x,y)=1=fX​(x)⋅fY​(y), and the support is the square (rectangular). So X⊥Y. ✓

Worked Example 2: Uniform on the Triangle

fX,Y​(x,y)=2,0<x<y<1.

(The factor of 2 ensures normalisation: the triangle has area 1/2, so 2⋅1/2=1.)

Marginals: For 0<x<1:

fX​(x)=∫x1​2,dy=2(1−x).

For 0<y<1:

fY​(y)=∫0y​2,dx=2y.

Check: ∫01​2(1−x),dx=2[x−x2/2]01​=1. ✓

Independence? fX​(x)⋅fY​(y)=4y(1−x)=2=fX,Y​(x,y) in general. Also the support is a triangle, not a rectangle. So X and Y are dependent.

Intuition: If I tell you Y=0.1, then X must be less than 0.1. Knowing Y constrains X. Of course they’re dependent.

Worked Example 3: Exponential Joint

fX,Y​(x,y)=e−x−y,x>0,;y>0.

This factors immediately: e−x−y=e−x⋅e−y, and the support is the first quadrant (a rectangle). So X∼Expo(1), Y∼Expo(1), and X⊥Y. No integration needed — the factorisation is the proof.

Computing Probabilities: The Region Matters

Example. With (X,Y) uniform on [0,1]2, find P(X+Y≤1).

The region is a right triangle with vertices (0,0),(1,0),(0,1), area 1/2.

P(X+Y≤1)=∫01​∫01−x​1,dy,dx=∫01​(1−x),dx=21​.

Or just: area of region times constant density =1/2⋅1=1/2. Always draw the region first.

Example. With (X,Y) uniform on the triangle 0<x<y<1, find P(Y−X>1/2).

P(Y−X>1/2)=∫01/2​∫x+1/21​2,dy,dx=∫01/2​2!(21​−x)dx=[x−x2]01/2​=41​.

The Joint CDF

FX,Y​(x,y)=P(X≤x,Y≤y)=∫−∞x​∫−∞y​fX,Y​(s,t),dt,ds.

Recovering the PDF: if FX,Y​ is sufficiently smooth,

fX,Y​(x,y)=∂x,∂y∂2​FX,Y​(x,y).

I rarely need to go through the CDF directly — setting up the double integral over the region is usually more transparent. But the mixed partial formula is worth knowing cold.

Non-Rectangular Supports: The Dangerous Case

When the support of (X,Y) is not a rectangle, the limits of integration in the marginals depend on the other variable, and the order of integration matters.

General strategy: draw the support region. For fX​(x): fix x, ask “what values can y take?” → those are the inner limits. Then integrate x over its full marginal range.

Example. fX,Y​(x,y)=c⋅x on 0<x<1,0<y<x.

Find c:

1=∫01​∫0x​cx,dy,dx=∫01​cx2,dx=3c​⟹c=3.

Marginal of X:

fX​(x)=∫0x​3x,dy=3x2,0<x<1.[Beta(3,1)]

Marginal of Y: Fix y∈(0,1); x ranges from y to 1 (since we need x>y):

fY​(y)=∫y1​3x,dx=23​(1−y2),0<y<1.

The critical step: when switching to fY​, the lower limit of the x-integral is y, not 0. Getting this wrong is the single most common mistake on these problems.

A Note on Existence

Not every pair of random variables admits a joint PDF. It is possible for both marginals to be continuous yet the joint to be singular. The canonical example: X∼N(0,1) and Y=X. Then (X,Y) is concentrated on the line y=x in R2, which has 2D Lebesgue measure zero, so no joint density exists. The formal statement is that a joint PDF exists iff the joint distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to 2D Lebesgue measure.

The Bivariate Normal is the canonical well-behaved example:

fX,Y​(x,y)=2πσX​σY​1−ρ2​1​exp!(−2(1−ρ2)1​[σX2​(x−μX​)2​−σX​σY​2ρ(x−μX​)(y−μY​)​+σY2​(y−μY​)2​]).

Takeaway for now: both marginals are normal, and X⊥Y⟺ρ=0. The bivariate normal is the unique distribution where uncorrelated implies independent — this is special to the Gaussian family and fails in general.
