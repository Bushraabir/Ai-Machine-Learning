17.Independence of Sample Mean and Sample Variance

Apr 01, 20265 min read

Core Theorem: If X1​,X2​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2), then the sample mean Xˉn​ and the sample variance Sn2​ are independent.

This is a striking and non-obvious fact — Xˉ and S2 are both computed from the same data, yet they carry zero information about each other. This independence is what underpins all of classical inference: t-tests, F-tests, confidence intervals.

Uniqueness Warning: This independence is special to the normal distribution. For non-normal populations, Xˉ and S2 are generally not independent.

Definitions

Sample Mean

Xˉn​=n1​∑i=1n​Xi​

Sample Variance (unbiased)

Sn2​=n−11​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2

Why n−1?
Using n−1 (Bessel’s correction) makes S2 an unbiased estimator of σ2: E[S2]=σ2

Intuition: We lose one degree of freedom because S2 is computed relative to Xˉ, which itself was estimated from the data.

The Theorem (Fisher, 1925)

Theorem (Independence of Xˉ and S2):
Let X1​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2). Then:

  1. Xˉn​∼N!(μ,,nσ2​)
  2. σ2(n−1)Sn2​​∼χn−12​
  3. Xˉn​⊥!!!⊥Sn2​

All three conclusions hold jointly.

Proof Strategy (Three Approaches)

Approach 1: Orthogonal Decomposition (Geometric / Most Elegant)

Key Idea: Decompose the data vector into two orthogonal components — one capturing the mean, one capturing the deviations.

Define the data vector X=(X1​,…,Xn​)T∈Rn.

Step 1 — Decompose the sum of squares: ∑i=1n​(Xi​−μ)2=∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)2+n(Xˉ−μ)2

This is a Pythagorean decomposition in Rn:

  • The vector (Xˉ,…,Xˉ) lies in the 1-dimensional subspace V1​=span1
  • The residual vector (X1​−Xˉ,…,Xn​−Xˉ) lies in V1⊥​ (the (n−1)-dimensional orthogonal complement)

Step 2 — Use rotational invariance of the normal:
Since Xi​∼iidN(μ,σ2), the standardized vector Z=(Xi​−μ)/σ is spherically symmetric. Any orthogonal rotation of Z preserves the joint distribution.

Step 3 — Independence follows from orthogonality:
Xˉ is the projection of X onto V1​. S2 depends only on the projection onto V1⊥​. Since these subspaces are orthogonal and the distribution is rotationally symmetric (i.e., normal), the two projections are independent.

Xˉ⊥!!!⊥(X1​−Xˉ,…,Xn​−Xˉ)​

Since S2 is a function of only the residuals, independence follows.

Approach 2: MGF / Direct Calculation

Step 1 — Rewrite Xi​=Xˉ+(Xi​−Xˉ).

Step 2 — Show the cross term vanishes: ∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)=0(always, by definition of Xˉ)

Step 3 — Joint MGF factorizes:
For any linear combination aXˉ+bS2, if the joint MGF factors as MXˉ​(a)⋅MS2​(b), then Xˉ⊥!!!⊥S2.

This works because for normal RVs, uncorrelated ⇒ independent (a property unique to the multivariate normal).

Key calculation: Cov(Xˉ,,Xj​−Xˉ)=Cov!(Xˉ,Xj​)−Var(Xˉ)=nσ2​−nσ2​=0

Since all (Xj​−Xˉ) are uncorrelated with Xˉ, and all are jointly normal, they are independent of Xˉ.

Approach 3: Cochran’s Theorem (Most General)

Cochran’s Theorem:
If Z∼N(0,In​) and ∣Z∣2=Q1​+Q2​+⋯+Qk​ where each Qi​ is a quadratic form with rank ri​, and ∑ri​=n, then:

  • Each Qi​∼χri​2​
  • Q1​,Q2​,…,Qk​ are mutually independent

Application:
∑i=1n​Zi2​=nZˉ2+∑i=1n​(Zi​−Zˉ)2

where Zi​=(Xi​−μ)/σ. The first term has rank 1, the second has rank n−1, and 1+(n−1)=n. By Cochran:

  • nZˉ2∼χ12​ (equivalently, n​Zˉ∼N(0,1))
  • ∑(Zi​−Zˉ)2∼χn−12​, which is σ2(n−1)S2​
  • They are independent ✓

The Chi-Squared Connection

The statement σ2(n−1)S2​∼χn−12​ deserves its own unpacking.

Definition: χk2​=∑i=1k​Zi2​ where Zi​∼iidN(0,1).

Why n−1 degrees of freedom?
The n residuals (Xi​−Xˉ) satisfy the one linear constraint ∑(Xi​−Xˉ)=0, so they have only n−1 free dimensions. Equivalently, estimating μ by Xˉ “uses up” 1 degree of freedom.

Why This Matters: The t-Distribution

The independence theorem is the exact reason the Student’s t-statistic works.

T=S/n​Xˉ−μ​

Step 1: The numerator Z=σ/n​Xˉ−μ​∼N(0,1).

Step 2: V=σ2(n−1)S2​∼χn−12​.

Step 3: Z⊥!!!⊥V (by the independence theorem).

Definition of t-distribution: T=V/(n−1)​Z​ where Z⊥!!!⊥V, Z∼N(0,1), V∼χn−12​.

Notice σ2 cancels: T=S2/σ2​(Xˉ−μ)/(σ/n​)​=S/n​Xˉ−μ​∼tn−1​

Without independence, this derivation collapses. The t-statistic would not have a known distribution.

Why Normality Is Essential

For non-normal distributions, Xˉ and S2 are correlated in general. Concretely:

Counterexample (Exponential):
Let Xi​∼iidExp(λ). Then:

  • Xˉ=1/λ estimates the mean
  • S2 estimates the variance 1/λ2

Both depend on λ, so knowing Xˉ gives information about S2. They are not independent.

The mechanism that breaks for non-normals:
Orthogonality of projections (geometric proof) is always valid, but orthogonal normal vectors being independent is special to the multivariate normal (rotational symmetry). For non-normal distributions, Cov=0 does not imply independence.

Why is this theorem surprising?
Xˉ and S2 are both computed from the same n data points. You might expect that a large Xˉ would somehow be associated with a particular behavior in S2. But for normals, the location of the data cloud (Xˉ) and the spread of the data cloud (S2) are completely decoupled.

Geometric intuition:
Think of the data as a point in Rn. Xˉ is its projection onto the “all-equal” axis (the 1-dimensional diagonal). S2 is determined by how far it is from that axis. These are orthogonal pieces of information.
