12.Birth-Death Chain

Tags: probability stochastic-processes markov-chains birth-death
References: Blitzstein & Hwang — Introduction to Probability; Stat 110 (Harvard)
Status: 🟢 complete
Prerequisite notes: Markov Chains — Definitions and Transition Matrix · Stationary Distributions · Detailed Balance and Reversibility · Existence and Uniqueness of Stationary Distributions · Gamblers Ruin as a Markov Chain

“Birth-death chains are the one-dimensional Markov chains. The state space is a subset of Z, transitions only go left or right by one step, and that constraint — no jumps — forces the chain to be reversible. Always. The entire theory collapses into a single telescoping recurrence, and the stationary distribution is just a product formula. Everything is explicit. There is no other family of chains where I can write down every quantity in closed form this cleanly.”

Definition

A birth-death chain is a Markov chain on state space S⊆Z — either 0,1,…,M (finite) or 0,1,2,… (semi-infinite) — with the constraint that transitions only go to adjacent states:

qk,k+1​=pk​(birth probability) qk,k−1​=rk​(death probability) qk,k​=1−pk​−rk​(stay probability) qk,j​=0for ∣j−k∣≥2

with boundary conditions: r0​=0(can’t go below 0) pM​=0(can’t go above M, for finite chains)

For the chain to be valid: pk​,rk​≥0 and pk​+rk​≤1 for all k.

The name comes from population biology: state k is population size, pk​ is the probability of a birth (population increases by 1), and rk​ is the probability of a death (population decreases by 1). But the model appears everywhere — queues, genetics, chemical reactions, random walks.

I think of the birth-death chain as the Markov chain equivalent of a tridiagonal matrix: the transition matrix Q has nonzero entries only on the main diagonal and the two adjacent diagonals. All the structure is in those three diagonals, and the no-jump constraint is what makes everything solvable.

Irreducibility

The chain is irreducible if and only if pk​>0 for all k<M and rk​>0 for all k>0 (in the finite case). In words: births are always possible (unless at the top) and deaths are always possible (unless at the bottom). Every state can reach every other via a sequence of births and deaths.

If pk​=0 for some interior k, then state k is a barrier the chain can never cross upward — the state space splits. Similarly for rk​=0.

I’ll assume irreducibility throughout: pk​>0 for k<M and rk​>0 for k≥1.

Reversibility: Always

Theorem. Every irreducible birth-death chain is reversible with respect to its stationary distribution.

Proof. I need to verify detailed balance sk​qkj​=sj​qjk​ for all pairs (k,j).

For ∣k−j∣≥2: qkj​=qjk​=0, so both sides are zero. ✓

For adjacent pairs (k,k+1): the detailed balance condition is: sk​qk,k+1​=sk+1​qk+1,k​ sk​pk​=sk+1​rk+1​

This is one equation per adjacent pair. I haven’t yet shown this holds — I’ll use it to find s in the next section. The point is that the only non-trivial detailed balance conditions are for adjacent pairs, and there are only M of them (for a chain on 0,…,M). A birth-death chain satisfies detailed balance if and only if these M conditions hold — and I’ll construct s to make them hold. ■

The structural reason for reversibility: the birth-death chain lives on a path graph (or half-line). A path graph has no cycles. By Kolmogorov’s criterion from Detailed Balance and Reversibility, reversibility requires that the probability of traversing any cycle in one direction equals the probability in the other — but there are no cycles on a path graph, so the condition is vacuously satisfied. Reversibility is a topological fact about the state space, not a special property of the rates.

This is the deepest reason to care about birth-death chains: they are always reversible, which means I always have a clean product formula for the stationary distribution. The absence of cycles is what does it. The moment the state space has a loop — like the three-state cycle — reversibility can fail. On a line or half-line, there’s nowhere for irreversibility to hide.

The Stationary Distribution: The Product Formula

Derivation

Write the detailed balance condition for each adjacent pair:

sk​pk​=sk+1​rk+1​for k=0,1,…,M−1

This gives a telescoping recurrence:

sk+1​=sk​⋅rk+1​pk​​

Unrolling from k=0:

s1​=s0​⋅r1​p0​​ s2​=s1​⋅r2​p1​​=s0​⋅r1​r2​p0​p1​​ sk​=s0​⋅r1​r2​⋯rk​p0​p1​⋯pk−1​​

Define the traffic intensity ratios:

ρ0​=1,ρk​=r1​r2​⋯rk​p0​p1​⋯pk−1​​=∏j=0k−1​rj+1​pj​​for k≥1

Then sk​=s0​ρk​. Normalization ∑k=0M​sk​=1 gives:

s0​∑k=0M​ρk​=1⟹s0​=∑k=0M​ρk​1​

sk​=ℓ=0∑M​ρℓ​ρk​​,ρk​=j=0∏k−1​rj+1​pj​​​

This is the product formula for birth-death chains. It is completely explicit — no linear system to solve, just multiply ratios of birth and death rates and normalize.

I find it remarkable that a problem which in principle requires solving an M×M linear system reduces to a product of M scalars. The detailed balance condition is what makes this possible: instead of the global constraint sQ=s (coupling all states simultaneously), I get local constraints sk​pk​=sk+1​rk+1​ (coupling only adjacent pairs), and those decouple into a one-directional recurrence.

For the Semi-Infinite Chain 0,1,2,…

The same formula holds, but I need the series ∑k=0∞​ρk​ to converge for a stationary distribution to exist.

Stationary distribution exists ⟺ ∑k=0∞​ρk​<∞.

If ∑k=0∞​ρk​=∞, the chain has no stationary distribution. Whether this means the chain is transient or null recurrent depends on further analysis — but either way, there is no limiting distribution.

Classification: When Is the Chain Positive Recurrent?

For the finite irreducible birth-death chain on 0,…,M: always positive recurrent (finite + irreducible, from Existence and Uniqueness of Stationary Distributions). The product formula always gives a valid stationary distribution.

For the semi-infinite chain on 0,1,2,…:

ConditionClassification
∑k=0∞​ρk​<∞Positive recurrent; stationary distribution sk​=ρk​/∑ρℓ​
∑k=0∞​ρk​=∞, ∑k=0∞​1/(pk​ρk​)=∞Null recurrent
∑k=0∞​ρk​=∞, ∑k=0∞​1/(pk​ρk​)<∞Transient

The second and third conditions come from the theory of one-dimensional random walks — the quantities ∑1/(pk​ρk​) appear when computing expected hitting times and absorption probabilities via first-step equations.

The transience/null-recurrence distinction for semi-infinite chains is the one piece of this theory I find genuinely unintuitive. You’d think: if ∑ρk​=∞, the chain must drift to +∞ and be transient. But null recurrence is the sneaky middle case — the chain wanders to +∞ but always comes back, just with infinite expected return time. The simple symmetric random walk on Z≥0​ (constant pk​=rk​=1/2) is the canonical null recurrent birth-death chain.

Examples

Example 1: The Simple Random Walk (Gambler’s Ruin)

pk​=p, rk​=q=1−p for all k∈1,…,N−1, with absorbing boundaries pN​=r0​=0.

ρk​=(qp​)k

For p=q:

sk​=∑ℓ=0N​(p/q)ℓ(p/q)k​=1−(p/q)N+1(p/q)k⋅(1−p/q)​

But wait — the Gambler’s Ruin has absorbing states at 0 and N. Those are absorbing, not reflecting. The stationary distributions are δ0​ and δN​ (all mass at the absorbing boundaries). The product formula above applies when I think of the chain as having reflecting boundaries — i.e., states 0 and N push back into the interior.

For the reflecting version (r0​=0, pN​=0, pk​=p, rk​=q for interior states):

ρk​=(p/q)k,sk​=∑ℓ=0N​(p/q)ℓ(p/q)k​

For the fair walk p=q=1/2: ρk​=1 for all k, so sk​=1/(N+1) — the uniform distribution. This confirms: the simple symmetric random walk on 0,…,N with reflecting boundaries has the uniform stationary distribution. Makes sense: all states have the same degree in the path graph (interior states have degree 2, except the endpoints which have degree 1 — actually the endpoints have a self-loop in the reflecting version, restoring degree 2).

Pedantic but worth noting: for the reflecting boundary at 0 with p0​=1 (deterministically go to 1), then r1​=q gives ρ1​=p0​/r1​=1/q, which breaks the symmetry slightly. The clean uniform result requires p0​=p (same birth probability at the boundary), which is the lazy/reflecting boundary convention. The details matter for the normalization.

Example 2: The Ehrenfest Urn

M balls, d total, state = number in urn 1. From Detailed Balance and Reversibility:

pk​=dd−k​,rk​=dk​

ρk​=∏j=0k−1​(j+1)/d(d−j)/d​=∏j=0k−1​j+1d−j​=(kd​)

sk​=2d(kd​)​=Binomial(d,1/2) PMF at k

The stationary distribution is Binomial(d,1/2). This is the equilibrium distribution of balls between two chambers — the maximum entropy distribution consistent with d balls and equal probability of being in either urn.

Example 3: M/M/1 Queue (Semi-Infinite)

A queueing model: customers arrive at rate λ (Poisson), each served at rate μ. State = number of customers in system. In discrete time approximation:

pk​=λ,rk​=μfor all k≥1,p0​=λ,r0​=0

Let ρ=λ/μ (traffic intensity).

ρk​=(μλ​)k=ρk

The series ∑k=0∞​ρk=1−ρ1​ converges if and only if ρ<1.

For ρ<1: positive recurrent, stationary distribution sk​=(1−ρ)ρk — a Geometric distribution on 0,1,2,… with parameter 1−ρ.

For ρ≥1: no stationary distribution. The queue grows without bound — arrivals outpace service, the system never reaches equilibrium.

sk​=(1−ρ)ρk,k=0,1,2,…(for ρ<1)​

The mean queue length at stationarity:

Es​[X]=∑k=0∞​k(1−ρ)ρk=1−ρρ​

As ρ→1− (arrival rate approaches service rate), the mean queue length →∞. The queue is technically stable but barely — small perturbations cause blowup. This is a classical result in queueing theory, and the birth-death product formula gives it in two lines.

The M/M/1 queue is the reason queueing theorists love birth-death chains. Every M/M/s queue, M/M/∞ queue, and Erlang model is a birth-death chain. The product formula gives the stationary distribution immediately, and from there every queueing metric — mean queue length, mean waiting time, throughput — follows by direct computation. Little’s Law (L=λW) is usually the next step.

Example 4: Moran Model (Population Genetics)

N individuals, each of type A or B. State k = number of type-A individuals. At each step:

  • One individual is chosen to reproduce (uniformly at random)
  • One individual is chosen to die (uniformly at random)

pk​=Nk​⋅NN−k​=N2k(N−k)​,rk​=NN−k​⋅Nk​=N2k(N−k)​

So pk​=rk​ for all k. Then ρk​=∏j=0k−1​pj​/rj+1​=∏j=0k−1​1=1.

All ρk​=1, so the stationary distribution on 0,1,…,N would be uniform: sk​=1/(N+1).

But wait — states 0 and N are absorbing (once all individuals are the same type, the population stays there forever). The chain is not irreducible. The absorbing states have their own stationary distributions δ0​ and δN​. The interior states 1,…,N−1 are transient — the chain eventually fixes at 0 or N.

The birth-death product formula applies only when the chain is irreducible (or when we restrict to a closed class). For the Moran model, the interesting question is the absorption probability — which type fixes — and the expected fixation time, both computed by first-step equations as in Gamblers Ruin as a Markov Chain.

The Moran model is a case where the birth-death structure is visible (pk​=rk​, symmetric), but the chain is reducible due to absorbing boundaries. The product formula gives what would be the stationary distribution on the interior — uniform, reflecting the symmetry between types A and B — but the chain never actually reaches this distribution because it gets absorbed first. Always check irreducibility before applying the product formula.

Hitting Times via First-Step Equations

For birth-death chains, first-step equations reduce to second-order linear recurrences — the same structure as Gambler’s Ruin.

Let hk​=E[T0​∣X0​=k], the expected hitting time to state 0. Boundary: h0​=0.

First-step equation for k≥1:

hk​=1+pk​hk+1​+rk​hk−1​+(1−pk​−rk​)hk​

Simplify:

(pk​+rk​)hk​=1+pk​hk+1​+rk​hk−1​

pk​(hk​−hk+1​)=1−rk​(hk​−hk−1​)⋅pk​rk​​

This doesn’t simplify as cleanly as the absorption probability equation because it’s non-homogeneous. The standard approach: let uk​=hk​−hk−1​ (first differences). Substituting:

pk​uk+1​=rk​uk​−1⇒uk+1​=pk​rk​​uk​−pk​1​

This is a first-order linear recurrence in uk​, solvable by the integrating factor method. The solution involves the ρk​ ratios and sums thereof — explicit but messy. For the M/M/1 queue (pk​=λ, rk​=μ, ρ=λ/μ):

uk+1​=ρ⋅uk​−λ1​

which gives a geometric series. The hitting time hk​=∑j=1k​uj​ then telescopes.

The key insight is that for birth-death chains, first-step equations always reduce to a one-dimensional recurrence — no matrix inversion needed, just algebra — because the chain can only move by ±1.

The Global Balance Interpretation

The detailed balance condition sk​pk​=sk+1​rk+1​ has a physical interpretation that’s worth dwelling on.

Think of probability as “fluid” flowing along the state space. At stationarity:

  • Fluid flows rightward from k to k+1 at rate sk​pk​ (probability of being at k times birth rate).
  • Fluid flows leftward from k+1 to k at rate sk+1​rk+1​ (probability of being at k+1 times death rate).

Detailed balance says these two flows are equal — the net flow across each edge is zero. This is stronger than global balance (which only requires zero net flow at each node). For a one-dimensional chain, these are equivalent: if the net flow is zero across every edge, then summing over a contiguous set of edges gives zero net flow into any interval 0,…,k — which is exactly the global balance condition for all states simultaneously.

This is the “flux” picture. For a birth-death chain, global balance and detailed balance are actually equivalent — not just detailed balance implying global balance. The reason: on a path graph, every “cut” separating the state space into 0,…,k and k+1,…,M has exactly one edge crossing it (the edge (k,k+1)). Zero flow across every single edge is the same as zero flow across every cut. On graphs with cycles, this equivalence breaks — there can be “circulating currents” that satisfy global balance but not detailed balance. The birth-death chain’s path topology forbids such currents.

One-Line Summary for Future Me

Birth-death chains: transitions only ±1, so the path-graph topology forces reversibility (no cycles ⇒ Kolmogorov criterion vacuous). Detailed balance gives sk​pk​=sk+1​rk+1​, which telescopes into the product formula sk​∝ρk​=∏j<k​pj​/rj+1​. For the semi-infinite chain, a stationary distribution exists iff ∑ρk​<∞. Every birth-death quantity — stationary dist, hitting times, absorption probs — is explicit because the tridiagonal structure always reduces to a one-dimensional recurrence.
