04.Convolutions

Apr 01, 20267 min read

Convolution is what happens when you add two independent random variables and ask for the resulting distribution. The operation looks intimidating in the continuous case — an integral of a product of two shifted functions — but it’s the same idea as counting paths in the discrete case. Learn both. The discrete one builds the intuition.

The Core Question

If X and Y are independent random variables, what is the distribution of Z=X+Y?

This is one of the most fundamental questions in probability. The answer is the convolution of their distributions.

The independence assumption is load-bearing. Without it, you’d need the full joint distribution of (X,Y), not just the marginals. Convolution is precisely what independence buys you.

Discrete Case First

Let X and Y be independent, taking values in Z≥0​. Then:

P(Z=k)=P(X+Y=k)=∑j=0k​P(X=j),P(Y=k−j)

Why? Condition on X=j. Given X=j, we need Y=k−j. Since X⊥Y, this is P(X=j)P(Y=k−j). Sum over all valid j. That’s it.

This is literally polynomial multiplication. If you define generating functions GX​(s)=∑k​P(X=k)sk and GY​(s)=∑k​P(Y=k)sk, then:

GX+Y​(s)=GX​(s)⋅GY​(s)

Convolution in PMF-space = multiplication in generating function space. This is why PGFs are so powerful for sums.

Example: Sum of Two Dice

Let X,Y∼Uniform1,…,6 independently. Then P(Z=k)=∑j​P(X=j)P(Y=k−j). You’re counting the number of ways to write k as an ordered pair (j,k−j) with both in 1,…,6. The classic triangular histogram shape comes directly from this count.

Continuous Case

Let X and Y be independent continuous RVs with PDFs fX​ and fY​. The PDF of Z=X+Y is:

fZ​(z)=∫−∞∞​fX​(x),fY​(z−x),dx=(fX​∗fY​)(z)

This is the convolution of fX​ and fY​.

Derivation from First Principles

Don’t just accept this. Derive it via the CDF approach.

FZ​(z)=P(X+Y≤z)=∫−∞∞​P(Y≤z−x),fX​(x),dx=∫−∞∞​FY​(z−x),fX​(x),dx

where we used independence to write P(X∈dx,Y≤z−x)=fX​(x)dx⋅FY​(z−x).

Now differentiate with respect to z (under the integral, which is justified by dominated convergence in nice cases):

fZ​(z)=dzd​FZ​(z)=∫−∞∞​fY​(z−x),fX​(x),dx

Done. ■

Geometric interpretation: fZ​(z) is the “overlap” between fX​ and the reflected-and-shifted fY​. Fix z. Flip fY​ around zero to get fY​(−⋅), then shift it right by z to get fY​(z−⋅). The integral is the inner product of fX​ and this shifted-reflected fY​. As you slide z, the overlap changes — that’s the shape of the convolution.

The MGF/CF Method (Almost Always Easier)

For independent X,Y:

MX+Y​(t)=E[et(X+Y)]=E[etX]E[etY]=MX​(t)⋅MY​(t)

So if you can identify the product of MGFs as the MGF of a known distribution, you’re done — no integral needed.

This works with the characteristic function ϕ(t)=E[eitX] even when the MGF doesn’t exist. The CF always exists.

Strategy: Always try the MGF method first. Only resort to the convolution integral when the MGF approach doesn’t close nicely or you specifically need the PDF.

Key Examples Worked Out

1. Poisson + Poisson

Let X∼Pois(λ), Y∼Pois(μ), independent.

Via MGF: MX​(t)=eλ(et−1), MY​(t)=eμ(et−1).

MX+Y​(t)=e(λ+μ)(et−1)

This is the MGF of Pois(λ+μ). So X+Y∼Pois(λ+μ). □

Directly via PMF: P(X+Y=k)=∑j=0k​j!e−λλj​⋅(k−j)!e−μμk−j​=k!e−(λ+μ)​∑j=0k​(jk​)λjμk−j=k!e−(λ+μ)(λ+μ)k​

Binomial theorem at the last step. Beautiful. This is the reproductive property of the Poisson.

2. Normal + Normal

Let X∼N(μ1​,σ12​), Y∼N(μ2​,σ22​), independent.

Via MGF: MX​(t)=eμ1​t+21​σ12​t2, similarly for Y.

MX+Y​(t)=e(μ1​+μ2​)t+21​(σ12​+σ22​)t2

This is N(μ1​+μ2​, σ12​+σ22​).

Note: variances add, not standard deviations. This is the fact that saves you from a lot of wrong answers. SD(X+Y)=SD(X)+SD(Y) in general.

3. Gamma + Gamma (same rate)

Let X∼Gamma(a,λ), Y∼Gamma(b,λ), independent. (Using shape-rate parameterization.)

Via MGF: MX​(t)=(λ−tλ​)a for t<λ.

MX+Y​(t)=(λ−tλ​)a+b

So X+Y∼Gamma(a+b,λ). The rate must match for this to work.

Corollary: Since Exp(λ)=Gamma(1,λ), the sum of n i.i.d. Exp(λ) RVs is Gamma(n,λ). This is the waiting time for the n-th arrival in a Poisson process.

4. Uniform + Uniform (Convolution Integral Forced)

Let X,Y∼Uniform(0,1) independently. Here the MGF method works but doesn’t simplify to a named distribution, so let’s do the integral.

fZ​(z)=∫01​fX​(x)⋅fY​(z−x),dx=∫01​1[0≤z−x≤1],dx=∫01​1[z−1≤x≤z],dx

We need x∈[0,1] and x∈[z−1,z] simultaneously. There are two cases:

Case 0≤z≤1: The intersection is [0,z], so fZ​(z)=z.

Case 1<z≤2: The intersection is [z−1,1], so fZ​(z)=2−z.

Hence: fZ​(z)={z​0≤z≤1 2−z​1<z≤2 0​otherwise​

This is the triangular distribution on [0,2]. Makes complete sense — the convolution of two box functions is a tent. By the CLT, summing more and more uniforms gives a shape that approaches Gaussian.

Convolution and the Indicator-Function Trick

When the supports are bounded (like Uniform), you often end up integrating an indicator. The key skill is correctly intersecting the indicator constraints.

Template: fZ​(z)=∫fX​(x)⋅1[z−x∈supp(Y)],fY​(z−x),dx

The indicator carves out the valid range of x. Always draw the constraints on a number line and find the intersection. This is where most errors happen — getting the cases wrong.

n-fold Convolution

For i.i.d. X1​,…,Xn​ with PDF f, the PDF of Sn​=X1​+⋯+Xn​ is the n-fold convolution:

fSn​​=f∗f∗⋯∗f(n times)

In Fourier/CF space, this is just f^​(ξ)n — exponentiation. This is exactly why the CLT is most naturally proved using characteristic functions.

The Fourier Perspective

The Fourier transform of fZ​=fX​∗fY​ is:

fZ​​(ξ)=fX​​(ξ)⋅fY​​(ξ)

Convolution in the original domain = pointwise multiplication in the Fourier domain. This is the fundamental theorem of convolution, and it’s the reason CFs/MGFs are so effective.

The CF of X+Y (independent) is ϕX​(t)⋅ϕY​(t) — this is literally the above with ξ=t, up to a sign convention. So the MGF factorization trick isn’t a cute trick; it’s an instance of a deep structural fact.

When Convolution Fails (or Gets Complicated)

SituationProblem
X,Y not independentNeed the full joint distribution; can’t just convolve marginals
Distributions with different supports but weird overlapThe indicator constraints get messy; just be careful
Heavy-tailed distributions (Cauchy)MGF doesn’t exist; must use CF
X−Y instead of X+YConvolve fX​ with the reflection fY​(−⋅); i.e., fZ​(z)=∫fX​(x)fY​(x−z)dx

For Z=X−Y where X,Y independent: this is the same as X+(−Y), and −Y has PDF f−Y​(y)=fY​(−y). So fZ​(z)=(fX​∗f−Y​)(z)=∫fX​(x)fY​(x−z)dx. The cross-correlation rather than autocorrelation.

Reproductive Properties Summary

These are worth memorizing not as facts but as corollaries of the MGF method:

DistributionParameterSum of independent copies
Pois(λi​)ratePois(∑λi​)
N(μi​,σi2​)mean, varianceN(∑μi​,∑σi2​)
Gamma(ai​,λ)shape (same rate!)Gamma(∑ai​,λ)
Binomial(ni​,p)trials (same p!)Binomial(∑ni​,p)
χ2(ki​)dfχ2(∑ki​)
Cauchylocation, scaleCauchy (use CF — MGF doesn’t exist)

The constraint “same rate / same p” is essential. You can’t add Gamma(2,1) and Gamma(3,5) and get a Gamma without doing real work.
