04.Bias of the Sample Standard Deviation

04.Bias of the Sample Standard Deviation

Apr 02, 202614 min read

This one embarrassed me the first time I thought about it carefully. Everyone knows S2=n−11​∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2 is unbiased for σ2 — that’s the whole reason for the n−1. But then I asked: is S=S2​ unbiased for σ? The obvious answer feels like “yes, just take square roots.” That’s wrong. And the reason it’s wrong is Jensen. A single application of Jensen, and the whole thing collapses. This note is about why E[S]<σ always, what the exact bias is for normal data, and why this is a beautiful example of convexity defeating naive intuition.

Tags

#probability #statistics #estimation #bias #jensen #sample-variance #stat110 #blitzstein

Prerequisites

  • Sample mean Xˉ=n1​∑i=1n​Xi​, sample variance S2=n−11​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)2
  • Unbiasedness: E[θ^]=θ
  • Jensen’s inequality: g concave ⇒g(E[Y])≥E[g(Y)]
  • Chi-squared distribution and its connection to normal samples
  • E[W​] for W∼χk2​ — this is where the exact bias lives

1. Setup and the Naive Error

Let X1​,…,Xn​∼iid some distribution with mean μ and variance σ2<∞.

Define:

S2=n−11​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)2

Known fact: E[S2]=σ2. So S2 is unbiased for σ2.

The naive hope: Since E[S2]=σ2, maybe E[S]=σ? After all, S=S2​, and σ2​=σ. So “just take square roots of both sides of E[S2]=σ2.”

Why this fails: Expectation is not a ring homomorphism. You cannot pass nonlinear functions through E. Specifically:

E[S2​]=E[S2]​

in general. The function g(x)=x​ is concave on [0,∞). By Jensen:

E[S2​]≤E[S2]​=σ2​=σ

∴E[S]≤σ

with equality iff S2 is constant a.s., i.e., Var(S2)=0. For any non-degenerate distribution and n<∞, Var(S2)>0, so:

E[S]<σ​

The sample standard deviation S always underestimates σ. Not sometimes, not approximately — always, for any non-degenerate population and any finite n. Jensen is doing all the work, and concavity of ⋅​ is the villain.

2. Why S2 is Unbiased — The Proof

Before going further, I should have the unbiasedness of S2 cold, because it motivates the whole setup.

Theorem. E[S2]=σ2.

Proof. Write S2=n−11​∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)2. The key identity:

∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)2=∑i=1n​Xi2​−nXˉ2

Take expectation:

E![∑i=1n​Xi2​]=n(σ2+μ2)(since E[Xi2​]=Var(Xi​)+(E[Xi​])2=σ2+μ2)

E[nXˉ2]=n,E[Xˉ2]=n!(Var(Xˉ)+(E[Xˉ])2)=n!(nσ2​+μ2)=σ2+nμ2

Subtracting:

E![∑i=1n​(Xi​−Xˉ)2]=n(σ2+μ2)−(σ2+nμ2)=(n−1)σ2

Dividing by n−1: E[S2]=σ2. □

The n−1 in the denominator is exactly what corrects for the fact that Xˉ is estimated from the same data — the deviations (Xi​−Xˉ) have only n−1 degrees of freedom, not n. Dividing by n would give a biased-low estimator of σ2.

3. The Exact Bias for Normal Data

For general distributions, Jensen gives us the sign of the bias (E[S]<σ) but not its magnitude. For normal data, we can compute it exactly.

3.1 The chi-squared connection

Let X1​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2). Then:

σ2(n−1)S2​∼χn−12​

This is a theorem — proof uses the fact that (Xi​−Xˉ)/σ are jointly normal with a covariance matrix of rank n−1, and a sum of squares of independent standard normals is chi-squared. Take it as given for now.

So:

S2=n−1σ2​W,W∼χn−12​

S=σn−1W​​

E[S]=n−1​σ​,E[W​]

3.2 Computing E[W​] for W∼χk2​

The chi-squared distribution with k degrees of freedom is Gamma(k/2,1/2) (shape-rate parametrization), so its PDF is:

fW​(w)=2k/2Γ(k/2)wk/2−1e−w/2​,w>0

E[W​]=∫0∞​w1/2⋅2k/2Γ(k/2)wk/2−1e−w/2​,dw=2k/2Γ(k/2)1​∫0∞​w(k+1)/2−1e−w/2,dw

Recognize this as a Gamma integral. Substitute u=w/2:

=2k/2Γ(k/2)1​⋅2(k+1)/2Γ!(2k+1​)=Γ(k/2)2​,Γ!(2k+1​)​

3.3 The result

With k=n−1:

E[S]=n−1​σ​⋅Γ!(2n−1​)2​,Γ(n/2)​=σ⋅=:,cn​n−12​​,Γ!(2n−1​)Γ(n/2)​​​

The constant cn​<1 (by Jensen), and:

E[S]=cn​σ,cn​=n−12​​,Γ!(2n−1​)Γ(n/2)​​

The bias is:

Bias(S)=E[S]−σ=(cn​−1)σ<0

3.4 Numerical values of cn​

ncn​1−cn​ (relative bias)
22/π​≈0.7979≈20.2
5≈0.9400≈6.0
10≈0.9727≈2.7
25≈0.9896≈1.0
100≈0.9975≈0.25

For n=2: the sample standard deviation underestimates σ by about 20% on average. For n=10 it’s still almost 3%. The bias vanishes as n→∞, but it’s non-trivial for small samples. This matters in practice — small-sample inference with S is not as clean as textbooks make it seem.

3.5 The unbiased estimator

If we want an exactly unbiased estimator of σ (under normality), we use:

σ^=cn​S​

This is called the corrected sample standard deviation. In practice, cn​≈1 for moderate n, so S is used anyway — but the correction exists, and some software applies it.

4. Large n Asymptotics: The Delta Method Perspective

For large n, the bias is approximately:

E[S]≈σ!(1−4(n−1)1​)

Derivation via the delta method. We have E[S2]=σ2 and Var(S2)=n−12σ4​ (for normal data; depends on the fourth cumulant in general).

Let g(x)=x​, so g′(x)=2x​1​, g′′(x)=−4x3/21​. By a second-order Taylor expansion around μS2​=σ2:

E[g(S2)]≈g(σ2)+21​g′′(σ2),Var(S2)=σ+21​⋅(−4σ31​)⋅n−12σ4​=σ−4(n−1)σ​

So Bias(S)≈−4(n−1)σ​, confirming the bias is O(1/n).

The delta method reframes the bias as coming from the curvature of ⋅​ at σ2. The second derivative g′′<0 (concavity) is what makes the bias negative. Jensen and the delta method are telling the same story — Jensen gives the sign, the delta method gives the rate.

5. The General Principle

This example is really a lesson about a general phenomenon:

If θ^ is unbiased for θ, and h is a nonlinear function, then h(θ^) is generally biased for h(θ). The direction of the bias is determined by the convexity or concavity of h:

  • h convex: E[h(θ^)]≥h(θ) — biased high
  • h concave: E[h(θ^)]≤h(θ) — biased low

The magnitude of the bias is approximately 21​h′′(θ),Var(θ^) for large n.

So the sample variance S2 is unbiased for σ2. But S is biased low for σ (concave transformation). And S2 is biased high for σ2… wait, no, that’s circular. Let me think again: logS is biased low for logσ (concave). eS is biased high for eσ (convex). The principle applies at every level of the composition.

6. Summary

EstimatorTargetUnbiased?Reason
S2=n−11​∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2σ2✓ YesDirect calculation; n−1 denominator corrects for estimating μ
n1​∑(Xi​−Xˉ)2σ2✗ NoBiased low by factor (n−1)/n
S=S2​σ✗ No⋅​ is concave; Jensen gives E[S]<σ
S/cn​ (normal only)σ✓ YesCorrects for the exact cn​ factor

7. Things I Should Be Able to Do Cold

  • State why E[S]<σ in one sentence (Jensen, ⋅​ concave)
  • Prove E[S2]=σ2 from scratch
  • Derive E[S]=cn​σ under normality, citing (n−1)S2/σ2∼χn−12​
  • State the approximate bias −σ/(4(n−1)) and derive it from the delta method
  • State the general principle: nonlinear transforms of unbiased estimators are biased, direction determined by convexity

8. Open Questions / Things to Sit With

  • UMVUE for σ: Under normality, S/cn​ is unbiased. Is it the UMVUE? Yes — it follows from the completeness of (Xˉ,S2) as sufficient statistics and the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem. Worth coming back to.
  • Non-normal distributions: The exact bias of S depends on the distribution through the fourth moment. The delta method approximation −σ/(4(n−1)) is distribution-free (depends only on Var(S2), which depends on the fourth cumulant). For heavy-tailed distributions, the bias can be much larger.
  • Why n−1 and not n? The heuristic is “degrees of freedom” — once Xˉ is estimated, only n−1 deviations (Xi​−Xˉ) are free. But the rigorous reason is the calculation in §2. The geometric picture: the vector (X1​−Xˉ,…,Xn​−Xˉ) lies in an (n−1)-dimensional subspace of Rn (orthogonal to (1,1,…,1)). Dividing by n−1 gives the correct normalization.

9. References

  • Blitzstein & Hwang, Introduction to Probability — Jensen’s inequality and its consequences; the sample variance calculation
  • Harvard Stat 110 — the connection between S2 and χn−12​ is developed in the normal distribution lectures
  • Self-note: the cn​ correction is used in quality control (process capability indices). Also appears in the definition of Bessel’s correction. The story of why n−1 is one of the most commonly misunderstood points in introductory statistics.
