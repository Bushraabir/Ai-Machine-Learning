01.Inequalities — Cauchy Schwarz & Second Moment Method

01.Inequalities — Cauchy-Schwarz & Second Moment Method

Apr 02, 20268 min read

These two tools feel very different on the surface — one is linear algebra dressed up in expectation notation, the other is a pure probabilistic trick. But they’re both doing the same philosophical thing: controlling a random variable by controlling its moments. I keep coming back to this note when I forget why the second moment method “works” — it’s because Cauchy-Schwarz is secretly running the show underneath.

Prerequisites I should have cold

  • E[X], Var(X), E[X2] — and the identity Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2
  • Inner product spaces (even just Rn is enough to feel Cauchy-Schwarz in your bones)
  • Indicator r.v.’s: X=∑i​1Ai​​ is the single most powerful modelling trick in discrete probability

1. Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

1.1 The “real” statement (inner product form)

Let (Ω,F,P) be a probability space. For any two r.v.’s X,Y with finite second moments:

∣E[XY]∣2≤E[X2],E[Y2]

This is just the inner product inequality ∣⟨u,v⟩∣2≤∣u∣2∣v∣2 in the Hilbert space L2(Ω,P), where the inner product is ⟨X,Y⟩:=E[XY].

Every time I write this I feel like I should acknowledge: this is not a “probability theorem.” It’s a theorem about inner product spaces that probability inherits for free. That’s why it’s so powerful — it’s not tied to any particular structure of the problem.

1.2 Proof I actually remember (the discriminant trick)

Claim: (E[XY])2≤E[X2]E[Y2].

Proof. For any t∈R, consider:

0≤E[(tX−Y)2]=t2,E[X2]−2t,E[XY]+E[Y2]

This is a quadratic in t that is always ≥0. For a quadratic at2+bt+c≥0 for all t, we need the discriminant to be non-positive:

b2−4ac≤0⟹4(E[XY])2−4,E[X2],E[Y2]≤0

(E[XY])2≤E[X2],E[Y2]​

Equality holds iff E[(tX−Y)2]=0 for some t, i.e., tX=Y almost surely — i.e., X and Y are a.s. linear multiples of each other. □

1.3 The correlation form (what I use most)

Taking X←X−E[X] and Y←Y−E[Y], Cauchy-Schwarz immediately gives:

∣Cov(X,Y)∣2≤Var(X),Var(Y)

which means ∣Corr(X,Y)∣≤1. This is the proof that correlation lives in [−1,1]. It’s not obvious without C-S.

1.4 A useful rearrangement: bounding E[X]2 from below

Set Y=1 (the constant r.v.). Then:

(E[X⋅1])2≤E[X2],E[12]⟹E[X]2≤E[X2]

So Cauchy-Schwarz gives Jensen (f(x)=x2 is convex) as a special case. Or flipped:

E[X]2≤E[X2]

This is the key inequality I need for the Second Moment Method. Write it on your hand.

1.5 Another form: E[X]≥E[X2](E[X])2​ for X≥0

If X≥0, write X=X⋅1X>0​ and apply C-S with Y=1X>0​:

E[X]=E[X⋅1X>0​]≤E[X2]​,P(X>0)​

Squaring and rearranging:

P(X>0)≥E[X2](E[X])2​​

This is the Second Moment Method lower bound. The whole method lives in this one line. I should tattoo this.

2. The Second Moment Method

2.1 What is it, philosophically?

The First Moment Method says: P(X>0)≤E[X] (Markov). It gives upper bounds on the probability that something exists.

The Second Moment Method gives lower bounds: it says that if E[X] is big AND E[X2] is not too much bigger than (E[X])2, then X>0 with positive probability. In words: the count has enough “signal” relative to its “noise” to guarantee existence.

The ratio E[X2]/(E[X])2 measures the “spread” of X relative to its mean. If this ratio is bounded, you’re in business.

2.2 The Paley-Zygmund inequality (the full version)

Theorem (Paley-Zygmund). For X≥0 with E[X2]<∞ and 0<θ<1:

P(X≥θ,E[X])≥(1−θ)2,E[X2](E[X])2​

Proof. Let μ=E[X]. Write:

μ=E[X]=E[X,1X<θμ​]+E[X,1X≥θμ​]

The first term is at most θμ⋅1=θμ, so:

E[X,1X≥θμ​]≥(1−θ)μ

Now apply Cauchy-Schwarz to the left side:

(1−θ)μ≤E[X,1X≥θμ​]≤E[X2]​,P(X≥θμ)​

Square both sides and rearrange:

P(X≥θμ)≥E[X2](1−θ)2μ2​=(1−θ)2,E[X2](E[X])2​□

The θ→0 case recovers our earlier bound P(X>0)≥(E[X])2/E[X2].

2.3 The standard setup (indicator variables)

The method is most powerful when X=∑i=1n​1Ai​​ counts the number of events that occur.

Computing E[X2]:

E[X2]=E![(∑i​1Ai​​)2]=∑i​∑j​E[1Ai​​1Aj​​]=∑i​∑j​P(Ai​∩Aj​)

Split the diagonal from the off-diagonal:

E[X2]=E[X]+∑i=j​P(Ai​∩Aj​)

The diagonal terms P(Ai​∩Ai​)=P(Ai​)=E[1Ai​​], so summing them gives E[X]. The off-diagonal sum is what I need to control.

The ratio to control:

(E[X])2E[X2]​=(E[X])2E[X]+∑i=j​P(Ai​∩Aj​)​

The method works when this ratio stays bounded (ideally →1) as n→∞.

2.4 Canonical example: Birthday matching

This is the Stat 110 example that clicked everything for me.

n people, 365 days. Let Aij​ = event that persons i and j share a birthday. Let X=∑i<j​1Aij​​ count the number of matching pairs.

E[X]=(2n​)⋅3651​≈730n2​

First moment method (upper bound on no match): P(no match)≤1 trivially. More useful: by Markov, P(X=0)≤1 — useless. The first moment doesn’t give us the birthday problem. We need a lower bound on P(X>0).

Second moment method:

E[X2]=E[X]+∑(i,j)=(k,l)​P(Aij​∩Akl​)

Two cases for the pair of pairs: share one person, or disjoint.

  • Disjoint pairs (4 distinct people): P(Aij​∩Akl​)=1/3652
  • One shared person (3 distinct people, e.g., i=k): P(Aij​∩Ail​)=1/3652 (all three share i‘s birthday, probability 1/3652… wait, no. Aij​: j matches i; Ail​: l matches i. Both require matching i‘s birthday: P=(1/365)2. Same.)

So P(Aij​∩Akl​)=1/3652 in all cases. Thus:

E[X2]=E[X]+(2n​)2⋅36521​+O(n3⋅36521​)

More carefully: (E[X])2≈n4/(730)2 and E[X2]/(E[X])2→1 when E[X]→∞. So when n≫365​, P(X>0)→1. ✓

2.5 Warning: when the method fails

The second moment method fails when E[X2]≫(E[X])2, i.e., when the events Ai​ are highly positively correlated. In that case X is “lumpy” — it’s either very large or zero — and a large mean doesn’t guarantee X>0.

Classic failure case: X is a single indicator, say 1A​ with P(A)=p small. Then E[X]=p, E[X2]=p, so the bound gives P(X>0)≥p2/p=p. Which is exact! But if p is small that lower bound is useless. The method doesn’t fail — it’s just tight.

The method really fails when you have a few dominant terms that contribute most of E[X] but are correlated. In that case, break the sum up and handle dominant/non-dominant terms separately.

3. Connection Between the Two

Here is the line I want to remember:

Cauchy-Schwarz is the engine of the Second Moment Method.

Specifically, the key inequality P(X>0)≥(E[X])2/E[X2] is a direct consequence of applying Cauchy-Schwarz with Y=1X>0​. The whole method is one application of one inequality.

The reason we compute E[X2] and not something else is because C-S naturally pairs X with the L2 norm E[X2]​. The “second moment” in the name is not a coincidence — it’s ∣X∣L22​.

7. Open Questions / Things to Think About

  • Why does the second moment method give the right threshold in so many combinatorial problems? Is there a deeper reason, or is it case-by-case?
  • The Paley-Zygmund inequality gives a bound for P(X≥θE[X]). Is there an analogous “higher moment method” using E[Xk]?
  • Connection to Bonferroni inequalities — the second moment method is sort of dual to inclusion-exclusion. Make this precise.
