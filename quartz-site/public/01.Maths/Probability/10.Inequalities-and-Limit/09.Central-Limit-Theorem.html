09.Central Limit Theorem

Tags: probability convergence theorems CLT foundations
References: Blitzstein & Hwang — Introduction to Probability; Stat 110 (Harvard)
Status: 🟡 fleshing out
Prerequisite notes: Law of Large Numbers, Moment Generating Functions, Characteristic Functions

“The LLN tells you where the sample mean goes. The CLT tells you how it gets there — and the answer is shockingly universal.”

The Big Picture

The LLN said Xˉn​→μ. Fine. But Xˉn​−μ→0 is a degenerate statement — it converges to a point. If I zoom in at exactly the right rate, something non-trivial is sitting there. That’s the CLT.

The miracle is this: no matter what distribution you start with (subject to finite variance), if you standardize the sample mean, you always get the same limiting object — the standard normal. The Gaussian isn’t just one distribution among many. It’s an attractor in the space of distributions under convolution and scaling.

This is genuinely shocking. Sum a thousand Bernoullis, sum a thousand Exponentials, sum a thousand Uniforms — zoom in at scale n​, and you see the same bell curve every time. The proof reveals why.

Setup

Let X1​,X2​,… be i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ2∈(0,∞).

Define the standardized sample mean:

Zn​=σ/n​Xˉn​−μ​=σn​i=1∑n​(Xi​−μ)​

The CLT says Zn​ converges in distribution to Z∼N(0,1).

Statement

σ/n​Xˉn​−μ​d​N(0,1)

Equivalently, for all x∈R:

P(Zn​≤x)→Φ(x)as n→∞

Or in the most practically useful form:

∑i=1n​Xi​;∼˙;N(nμ,;nσ2)for large n

Proof via Moment Generating Functions

This is the Stat 110 proof. It’s clean, memorable, and illuminates why the normal appears.

Assume M(t)=E[etX] exists in a neighborhood of 0.

Step 1: Center and normalize.

Let Yi​=(Xi​−μ)/σ, so E[Yi​]=0, Var(Yi​)=1, MGF is MY​. Then:

Zn​=n​1​∑i=1n​Yi​

Step 2: MGF of Zn​.

By independence and the i.i.d. structure:

MZn​​(t)=E![etZn​]=[MY​!(n​t​)]n

Step 3: Taylor expand MY​ around 0.

Since E[Y]=0 and E[Y2]=1:

MY​(s)=1+s=,0E[Y]​​+2s2​=,1E[Y2]​​+O(s3)=1+2s2​+O(s3)

Substituting s=t/n​:

MY​!(n​t​)=1+2nt2​+O!(n−3/2)

Step 4: Raise to the n-th power.

MZn​​(t)=[1+2nt2​+O!(n−3/2)]nn→∞​et2/2

using the standard limit (1+a/n+o(1/n))n→ea.

Step 5: Recognize the limit.

et2/2 is exactly the MGF of N(0,1). Since MGFs uniquely determine distributions and convergence of MGFs implies convergence in distribution:

Zn​d​N(0,1)■

The hinge of this proof is Step 3. The Taylor expansion kills the first-order term because the mean is zero. The s2/2 term — the variance term — is what survives. Everything of order s3 and above vanishes faster than 1/n when raised to the n-th power. The normal is “selected” because it’s the distribution whose MGF is et2/2, i.e., the one whose log-MGF is purely quadratic. The CLT is the statement that all the higher-order terms get annihilated by the scaling.

Proof via Characteristic Functions (the correct general proof)

The MGF proof requires the MGF to exist, which fails for heavy-tailed distributions. The right proof uses characteristic functions:

φX​(t)=E[eitX]

which always exists. The structure is identical: let Yi​=(Xi​−μ)/σ, then

φZn​​(t)=[φY​!(n​t​)]n

Taylor expand (using E[Y]=0, E[Y2]=1, noting the i2=−1 sign):

φY​(s)=1+isE[Y]−2s2​E[Y2]+O(s3)=1−2s2​+O(s3)

So:

φZn​​(t)=[1−2nt2​+O(n−3/2)]n→e−t2/2

And e−t2/2 is the characteristic function of N(0,1). The conclusion follows from Lévy’s continuity theorem: if characteristic functions converge pointwise to a function continuous at 0, the distributions converge in distribution.

This version works for all distributions with finite variance — no MGF assumption. The Cauchy distribution has neither finite mean nor variance, and indeed the CLT fails for it. Characteristic functions are the natural language here, not MGFs. I should think of the MGF proof as the “training wheels” version.

What d​ Really Means

Convergence in distribution is the weakest mode:

a.s.⟹in probability⟹in distribution

It does not say the random variables Zn​ themselves get close to Z in any pathwise sense — only that their laws converge. The CDFs converge at every continuity point of the limit CDF. The CLT lives at the bottom of this hierarchy, and that’s appropriate: what’s universal is the shape of the limiting distribution, not the realizations.

Rate of Convergence: Berry-Esseen

The CLT gives no rate. The Berry-Esseen theorem does:

supx∈R​∣P(Zn​≤x)−Φ(x)∣≤n​C⋅E∣Yi​∣3​

for absolute constant C≤0.4748 (sharpest known). The rate is O(1/n​), controlled by the third absolute moment — a measure of skewness.

  • Symmetric distributions (zero skewness) converge faster.
  • Skewed distributions need larger n.

The folk theorem “n = 30 is enough” is a rough proxy for when O(1/n​) error becomes negligible. For skewed distributions — say, Exponential — you need much larger n.

Why the Normal? The Cumulant Explanation

This is the deep reason, not just the mechanical proof.

Cumulants are the coefficients of the cumulant generating function logMX​(t):

logMX​(t)=κ1​t+2!κ2​​t2+3!κ3​​t3+⋯

where κ1​=μ, κ2​=σ2, κ3​ = third cumulant (related to skewness), etc. Cumulants are additive for independent r.v.’s.

Now track what happens to Zn​=σn​1​∑(Xi​−μ):

  • Centering kills κ1​
  • Scaling by 1/σn​ scales the k-th cumulant by (σn​)−k, so κk​(Zn​)=σknk/2n⋅κk​(X)​=σknk/2−1κk​(X)​

For k≥3: κk​(Zn​)→0 as n→∞ (since k/2−1>0).

The only distribution with κ1​=0, κ2​=1, and all higher cumulants zero is N(0,1). Convolution + scaling drives all cumulants of order ≥3 to zero. The Gaussian is the unique fixed point of this operation.

This is the most satisfying answer to “why normal?” that I know. The proof shows how; the cumulant argument shows why. Both are worth internalizing. The Gaussian isn’t special because someone decided it was — it’s the unique distribution that survives the annihilation of all higher-order structure.

Applications

Normal approximation to Binomial

Sn​∼Bin(n,p) — write it as a sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli(p), so μ=p, σ2=p(1−p):

np(1−p)​Sn​−np​d​N(0,1)

Continuity correction (since Sn​ is discrete): use k+21​ as the cutoff when approximating P(Sn​≤k).

Normal approximation to Poisson

Sn​∼Poisson(λ) with large λ: μ=σ2=λ, so

λ​Sn​−λ​d​N(0,1)

The Delta Method

If n​(Xˉn​−μ)d​N(0,σ2) and g is differentiable at μ with g′(μ)=0:

n​(g(Xˉn​)−g(μ))d​N!(0,;[g′(μ)]2σ2)

Proof idea: Taylor expand g(Xˉn​)≈g(μ)+g′(μ)(Xˉn​−μ) and apply CLT to the linear term. This is one of the most practically powerful consequences of the CLT — it gives asymptotic distributions for any smooth transformation of the sample mean. Variance-stabilizing transformations (like log, arcsin⋅​) are built by choosing g so that [g′(μ)]2σ2(μ)=const.

Multivariate CLT

Let X1​,…,Xn​ be i.i.d. random vectors in Rk with mean μ and covariance matrix Σ.

n​(Xˉn​−μ)d​Nk​(0,Σ)

The proof uses the Cramér-Wold device: a sequence of random vectors converges in distribution iff every linear combination of its components converges. So check t⊤Zn​d​N(0,t⊤Σt) for all t via the univariate CLT, and you’re done.

Failure Modes

  • Infinite variance: Cauchy has no finite mean or variance. The correct framework is stable distributions and the generalized CLT (Gnedenko). The scaling is n1/α for α∈(0,2] instead of n​.
  • Dependence: For dependent sequences, extensions exist (CLT for martingales, m-dependent sequences, mixing processes) but require additional conditions controlling how fast dependence decays.
  • Non-identical distributions: The Lindeberg-Feller theorem gives the necessary and sufficient condition for CLT when the Xi​ are independent but not identically distributed. The condition (Lindeberg condition) says no single term dominates the sum.

Connection Back to LLN

LLN is first-order. CLT is second-order. Large deviation theory is the next layer — it asks about the probability of events far outside the n​ window, i.e., exponentially unlikely deviations.

One-Line Summary for Future Me

CLT: Standardize the sample mean — whatever the original distribution, you get N(0,1) in the limit. Proved via MGF/characteristic function Taylor expansion. The deep reason: convolution + 1/n​ scaling annihilates all cumulants above the second, and the Gaussian is the unique distribution with only the first two cumulants nonzero.
