13.Student's t-Distribution

Prerequisite notes: Normal Distribution, Chi-Squared Distribution, Gamma Distribution, Moment Generating Functions

“The t-distribution is what honesty looks like. When you don’t know σ and have to estimate it, your uncertainty about that estimate inflates the tails. The t quantifies exactly how much inflation.”

The Big Picture

Here is the situation the t-distribution was invented to resolve.

You have X1​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2) and you want to make inference about μ. If σ were known, you’d use the z-statistic:

Z=σ/n​Xˉ−μ​∼N(0,1)

exactly. But σ is never known. You replace it with the sample standard deviation S:

T=S/n​Xˉ−μ​

and now T is not N(0,1). The randomness of S inflates the tails — T is more spread out than a standard normal, and the degree of inflation depends on how well-estimated S is, i.e., on n. The t-distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom captures this exactly.

William Gosset figured this out in 1908 while working at the Guinness brewery. He published under the pseudonym “Student” because Guinness didn’t allow employees to publish. Hence: Student’s t-distribution.

Definition

The Constructive Definition

If Z∼N(0,1) and V∼χν2​ are independent, then:

T=V/ν​Z​∼tν​

The parameter ν is the degrees of freedom.

This is the definition that reveals what the t-distribution is: a normal divided by an independent (scaled) chi. The denominator is random, and that randomness is what fattens the tails.

Why This Is the Right Definition for Inference

In the one-sample setting: X1​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2).

  • Numerator: Xˉ−μ∼N(0,σ2/n), so Z=(Xˉ−μ)/(σ/n​)∼N(0,1).
  • Denominator: by Cochran’s theorem, (n−1)S2/σ2∼χn−12​, and S2⊥Xˉ.

So: T=S/n​Xˉ−μ​=n−1(n−1)S2/σ2​​Z​=V/(n−1)​Z​∼tn−1​

The σ2 cancels exactly — that’s the point. You don’t need to know σ.

The independence of Xˉ and S2 is doing serious work here. Without Cochran’s theorem guaranteeing Z⊥V, the ratio wouldn’t have a t-distribution. This is a deeply non-obvious fact — in non-normal populations, Xˉ and S2 are generally dependent.

The PDF

Claim: T∼tν​ has PDF:

fν​(t)=νπ​;Γ!(2ν​)Γ!(2ν+1​)​(1+νt2​)−(ν+1)/2,t∈R

Proof: Use the constructive definition T=Z/V/ν​ with Z⊥V.

Condition on V=v. Given V=v, we have T∣V=v∼N(0,ν/v), so:

fT∣V​(t∣v)=νv​​⋅2π​1​exp!(−2νvt2​)

The marginal PDF of T is:

fT​(t)=∫0∞​fT∣V​(t∣v),fV​(v),dv

where fV​(v)=2ν/2Γ(ν/2)vν/2−1e−v/2​ is the χν2​ PDF. Substituting:

fT​(t)=∫0∞​νv​​⋅2π​e−vt2/(2ν)​⋅2ν/2Γ(ν/2)vν/2−1e−v/2​,dv

=2πν​,2ν/2Γ(ν/2)1​∫0∞​v(ν+1)/2−1exp!(−2v​(1+νt2​))dv

The integral is a Gamma integral. Let λ=21​!(1+νt2​), so the integral =Γ!(2ν+1​)/λ(ν+1)/2:

fT​(t)=2πν​,2ν/2Γ(ν/2)Γ!(2ν+1​)​⋅(1+t2/ν)(ν+1)/22(ν+1)/2​

Simplify the powers of 2: 2(ν+1)/2/2ν/2=21/2=2​. Then 2​/2π​=1/π​:

fν​(t)=νπ​;Γ!(2ν​)Γ!(2ν+1​)​(1+νt2​)−(ν+1)/2​■

The key move is conditioning on V and recognizing the resulting integral as a Gamma function. This is a standard trick: when a distribution is defined as a ratio involving a χ2, condition on the denominator, do the integral, and you get a closed form. The same strategy proves the F-distribution PDF.

The Heavy Tails, Made Precise

The tν​ PDF has polynomial tails: for large ∣t∣,

fν​(t)∼Cν​⋅∣t∣−(ν+1)

Compare this to the Gaussian, whose tails are ∼e−t2/2 — exponential decay. The t-distribution tails decay polynomially, which is why they’re called heavy tails.

Consequence for moments: E[∣T∣k]<∞ if and only if k<ν.

  • ν=1 (Cauchy): no finite moments at all — not even E[∣T∣].
  • ν=2: finite mean (E[T]=0 by symmetry) but infinite variance.
  • ν≥3: finite variance =ν/(ν−2).
  • ν≥5: finite kurtosis.

For ν≥2:

E[T]=0(by symmetry, when it exists)

Var(T)=ν−2ν​,ν>2

The variance exceeds 1 for all finite ν, reflecting the extra uncertainty from estimating σ. As ν→∞, Var(T)→1, consistent with convergence to N(0,1).

tν​→N(0,1) as ν→∞

Claim: As ν→∞, tν​d​N(0,1).

Proof via the constructive definition:

T=Z/V/ν​ where V∼χν2​.

By the LLN applied to V=∑i=1ν​Wi2​ (i.i.d. χ12​ terms):

νV​=ν1​∑i=1ν​Wi2​a.s.​E[W12​]=1

Therefore V/ν​a.s.​1, and by Slutsky’s theorem:

T=V/ν​Z​d​1Z​=Z∼N(0,1)■

Alternatively, via the PDF directly:

(1+νt2​)−(ν+1)/2=exp!(−2ν+1​log!(1+νt2​))→exp!(−2t2​)

using log(1+x/ν)≈x/ν for large ν. And the normalizing constant νπ​,Γ(ν/2)Γ((ν+1)/2)​→2π​1​ by Stirling’s approximation. So the PDF converges pointwise to ϕ(t).

The Slutsky proof is the one I think of first — it’s the most conceptually direct. LLN kills the randomness in the denominator as ν→∞, and what’s left is just Z. The heavy tails of tν​ exist precisely because V/ν fluctuates when ν is small.

Special Cases

ν=1: The Cauchy Distribution

f1​(t)=π(1+t2)1​

This is the Cauchy distribution. It has:

  • No finite mean (the integral ∫∣t∣/(1+t2),dt diverges).
  • No finite variance.
  • Tails decaying as t−2 — the heaviest possible for a t-distribution.

The Cauchy is Z1​/∣Z2​∣ for independent standard normals Z1​,Z2​, equivalently tan(θ) for θ∼Uniform(−π/2,π/2).

It’s the canonical example of a distribution where the LLN fails — the sample mean of i.i.d. Cauchy r.v.’s does not converge to anything, because there is no population mean to converge to.

ν=2

f2​(t)=21​(1+2t2​)−3/2

Finite mean (zero), infinite variance.

ν≥30 (practical rule of thumb)

At ν=30, the t-distribution is so close to N(0,1) that the difference is negligible in most applications. But for small ν — say, ν=5 or ν=10 — the tails are meaningfully heavier and using a z-table instead of a t-table would give anti-conservative confidence intervals.

No MGF (and why that matters)

The tν​ distribution has no moment generating function for any ν. This is because the polynomial tail decay means E[etT]=∞ for all t=0: the exponential function grows faster than any polynomial tail can suppress.

This is a substantive limitation: MGF-based proofs don’t apply to t. The characteristic function φT​(t)=E[eitT] does exist (it’s bounded), but it doesn’t have a clean closed form for general ν.

For ν=1 (Cauchy): φ(t)=e−∣t∣.

The absence of an MGF is a direct consequence of the heavy tails. Every moment E[∣T∣k] eventually blows up as k exceeds ν, and the MGF — which would require all moments to be finite and well-behaved — can’t exist. This is a good example of why MGFs are a tool of convenience, not universality; characteristic functions are always there when MGFs aren’t.

Connection to the F-Distribution

T2∼F1,ν​

Proof: T2=Z2/(V/ν). Now Z2∼χ12​ and V∼χν2​ independently. So:

T2=V/νZ2/1​=χν2​/νχ12​/1​∼F1,ν​

by definition of the F-distribution. ■

This is why a two-sided t-test at level α is equivalent to an F-test at level α — squaring the t-statistic gives an F1,ν​ statistic, and the rejection regions correspond exactly.

The t-Distribution as a Scale Mixture of Normals

There is a beautiful representation: T∼tν​ can be written as:

T∣W∼N(0,1/W),W∼Gamma(ν/2,,ν/2)

i.e., T is a normal distribution with a random variance, where the precision W follows a Gamma distribution. Marginalizing over W gives the t-distribution.

Why this matters:

  1. It explains the heavy tails intuitively: some draws of W are very small (large variance), making extreme values of T plausible.
  2. It connects to Bayesian statistics: under a Gaussian likelihood with a Gamma prior on the precision, the marginal distribution of the data is t-distributed.
  3. It’s the basis of robust regression algorithms: by treating each observation’s variance as a latent variable drawn from a Gamma, you get t-distributed errors, which downweight outliers automatically (the EM algorithm for this is the basis of IRLS with t-errors).

The representation also makes the PDF derivation feel less magical: you’re just marginalizing a Gaussian with a random variance, and that integral is the Gamma integral that appeared in the proof.

This is the representation I keep coming back to. The t-distribution isn’t just a quirky thing that happens when you don’t know σ — it’s the natural distribution for a Gaussian model where you’re uncertain about the scale. That’s a deep structural fact with implications throughout Bayesian inference.

Applications in Classical Statistics

One-Sample t-Test

H0​:μ=μ0​ vs. H1​:μ=μ0​.

Test statistic: T=S/n​Xˉ−μ0​​∼tn−1​ under H0​.

Reject at level α if ∣T∣>tn−1,,α/2​ (the upper α/2 quantile of tn−1​).

Two-Sample t-Test (equal variances)

X1​,…,Xm​∼iidN(μ1​,σ2) and Y1​,…,Yn​∼iidN(μ2​,σ2), both groups with the same σ2.

Pooled variance: Sp2​=m+n−2(m−1)SX2​+(n−1)SY2​​∼m+n−2σ2​χm+n−22​

Test statistic: T=Sp​1/m+1/n​Xˉ−Yˉ​∼tm+n−2​

Confidence Interval for μ

Xˉ±tn−1,,α/2​⋅n​S​

As n→∞, tn−1,α/2​→zα/2​ and this reduces to the standard z-interval.

Regression Coefficients

In a linear model Y=Xβ+ε with εi​∼iidN(0,σ2), the OLS estimator satisfies:

se^(β^​j​)β^​j​−βj​​∼tn−p​

where p is the number of predictors. Every standard error and p-value in a regression table comes from this t-distribution. Degrees of freedom =n−p because p parameters are estimated, consuming p degrees of freedom from n.

One-Line Summary for Future Me

tν​=N(0,1)/χν2​/ν​ (independent). Heavy polynomial tails because the denominator is random; variance =ν/(ν−2); no MGF. Converges to N(0,1) by LLN killing the denominator. Arises in inference whenever σ is unknown and estimated from data, with degrees of freedom counting the observations minus the parameters estimated.
