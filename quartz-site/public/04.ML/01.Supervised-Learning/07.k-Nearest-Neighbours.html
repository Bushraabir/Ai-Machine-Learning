07.k Nearest Neighbours

07.k-Nearest Neighbours

Apr 26, 202614 min read

“The simplest models are often the most instructive. KNN has no parameters, no training, no assumptions about the data distribution — and yet it perfectly interpolates the Bayes-optimal classifier as n→∞. Understanding why it fails in high dimensions is understanding why deep learning works.”

✦ The Setup

You have a labelled training set D=(xi​,yi​)i=1n​ where xi​∈Rd and yi​∈Y. Given a new query point x, you want to predict y.

KNN’s answer is brutally simple: look at the k training points closest to x and aggregate their labels.

y^​=aggregate(yj​:xj​∈Nk​(x))

where Nk​(x) denotes the k nearest neighbours of x under some distance metric d(⋅,⋅).

  • Classification: majority vote — y^​=argmaxc​∑xj​∈Nk​(x)​1[yj​=c]
  • Regression: mean — y^​=k1​∑xj​∈Nk​(x)​yj​

Weighted variants replace the uniform vote with inverse-distance weights: wj​=d(x,xj​)1​.

✦ The “No Training” Illusion

KNN is called a lazy learner or instance-based learner: there is no explicit training phase. All computation is deferred to inference time.

But this is not free. The costs are:

  • Memory: store all n training points — O(nd) space
  • Inference: compute distance to every training point — O(nd) per query (naïve)
  • No generalisation baked in: the model is the dataset

Compare to parametric models (linear regression, neural networks): they compress the data into a fixed number of parameters θ. KNN compresses nothing.

✦ The Bias-Variance Picture

The choice of k controls the entire bias-variance tradeoff:

kBiasVarianceBehaviour
k=1LowHighMemorises training data; Voronoi cells, decision boundary wiggles around every point
k=nHighLowPredicts the global majority class for everything
k optimalBalancedBalancedSmooth, generalising boundary

Derivation of the k=1 variance intuition. With 1-NN, the decision region of x is its Voronoi cell. Any noise in the label of a training point directly corrupts predictions in a region around it. The boundary is sensitive to every individual data point — that is high variance by definition.

Derivation of the k=n bias intuition. With all n neighbours, the prediction is the global mean/majority. The model has zero capacity to capture local structure — that is high bias.

The optimal k is chosen by cross-validation. Asymptotically, as n→∞ with k→∞ and k/n→0, the 1-NN error converges to at most twice the Bayes error (Cover & Hart, 1967). This is a remarkable result: a non-parametric method with no model assumptions approaches the fundamental limit.

✦ Distance Metrics

KNN is entirely defined by its distance metric. Changing the metric changes the geometry of the neighbourhood — and therefore what the model learns.

Minkowski Family

dp​(x,z)=(∑j=1d​∣xj​−zj​∣p)1/p

pNameCharacter
p=1Manhattan (L1)Robust to outliers; diamond-shaped unit ball
p=2Euclidean (L2)Standard; circular unit ball; sensitive to scale
p→∞Chebyshev (L∞)$\max_j

When Each Matters

  • Euclidean: default for continuous, homogeneous features (same units, same scale)
  • Manhattan: features with different scales when you can’t/won’t normalise; image pixel differences
  • Cosine similarity: cosθ=∣x∣∣z∣x⋅z​ — text data, high-dimensional sparse vectors where magnitude is meaningless
  • Hamming distance: ∑j​1[xj​=zj​] — categorical or binary features
  • Custom kernels / learned metrics: metric learning (LMNN, NCA) trains a transformation L so that d(x,z)=∣Lx−Lz∣ pulls same-class points together

⚠ Normalisation Is Not Optional

KNN is purely distance-based. A feature measured in kilometres and another in milligrams will have wildly different scales, and the large-scale feature will dominate every distance computation, rendering the other features useless.

Always standardise (z-score) before applying KNN, unless you have domain knowledge that raw scales are meaningful:

xj​←σ^j​xj​−μ^​j​​

Fit the scaler on the training set only. Apply it to validation/test sets. This is a leakage requirement, not a suggestion.

✦ The Curse of Dimensionality — A Rigorous Treatment

This is the central pathology of KNN, and of high-dimensional geometry in general. It is worth understanding deeply, because it motivates PCA, autoencoders, and the very existence of deep feature learning.

The Volume Argument

Consider the unit hypersphere Bd​(r)=x∈Rd:∣x∣≤r.

Vol(Bd​(r))=Γ(d/2+1)πd/2​rd

Now compute the fraction of volume in a thin shell of thickness ϵ:

Vol(Bd​(1))Vol(Bd​(1))−Vol(Bd​(1−ϵ))​=1−(1−ϵ)d

As d→∞, this fraction →1 for any fixed ϵ>0.

Interpretation: In high dimensions, almost all the volume of a hypersphere is concentrated in a thin shell near the surface. There is essentially no interior.

d=2:  shell fraction (ε=0.1) ≈ 19%
d=10: shell fraction (ε=0.1) ≈ 65%
d=100: shell fraction (ε=0.1) ≈ 99.997%

The Nearest-Neighbour Consequence

To find k nearest neighbours using a fraction p of the data volume, the required edge length ℓ of a hypercubic neighbourhood satisfies:

ℓd=p⟹ℓ=p1/d

For p=0.01 (1% of data) and d=10: ℓ=0.010.1≈0.63 — the neighbourhood spans 63% of the range in each dimension. Your “local” neighbourhood is not local at all.

The Distance Concentration Phenomenon

In high dimensions, the ratio of the maximum to minimum pairwise distance among random points converges to 1:

dmin​dmax​−dmin​​→0 as d→∞

More precisely, for n i.i.d. points in [0,1]d, the expected distance to the nearest neighbour grows as O(d1/2) while the standard deviation grows as O(1). The signal-to-noise ratio of the nearest neighbour distance collapses.

Implication: all points become approximately equidistant. The concept of “nearest neighbour” loses discriminative power. KNN degrades to a random classifier.

Practical Threshold

KNN tends to work well up to d≈20–30 features. Beyond that, either:

  1. Reduce dimensionality first (PCA, autoencoders) to extract meaningful low-dimensional structure
  2. Use a model that imposes inductive bias (linear models, trees, NNs) instead of relying purely on geometry

✦ Concrete Example: Classification

Setup

Two classes: red (y=0) and blue (y=1). Training points:

Class 0 (red):  (1, 2), (2, 1), (1.5, 1.8)
Class 1 (blue): (4, 4), (5, 3), (4.5, 4.2)

Query point: x=(3,3).

1-NN

Distances from (3,3):

  • (1,2): 4+1​=2.24
  • (2,1): 1+4​=2.24
  • (1.5,1.8): 2.25+1.44​=1.92
  • (4,4): 1+1​=1.41 ← nearest
  • (5,3): 4+0​=2.00
  • (4.5,4.2): 2.25+1.44​=1.92

Nearest neighbour: (4,4), class blue. Prediction: blue.

3-NN

Three nearest: (4,4) [blue, d=1.41], (5,3) [blue, d=2.00], (4.5,4.2) [blue, d=1.92].

Vote: 3 blue, 0 red. Prediction: blue. (Same here, but consider a point closer to the boundary.)

Decision Boundary

With k=1, the decision boundary is the Voronoi diagram of the training set. Each training point “owns” a region. With k>1, the boundary smooths out and shrinks the influence of individual points.

Training data (1D, for clarity):

xy

Query: x=2.5, k=2.

Two nearest: x=2.0 (d=0.5) and x=3.0 (d=0.5).

y^​=23.9+6.2​=5.05

With inverse-distance weighting (but d=0.5 for both, so weights are equal here): same result, y^​=5.05.

KNN regression produces a piecewise constant function with steps at midpoints between training values. It cannot extrapolate beyond the training range.

✦ Efficient Nearest Neighbour Search

Naïve KNN is O(nd) per query — unacceptable for large n or high query throughput.

KD-Trees

A KD-tree recursively partitions Rd by alternating the splitting dimension. At each level ℓ, split on dimension ℓmodd at the median.

Construction: O(ndlogn) — sort each dimension, recurse.

Query: O(logn) average for low dimensions. Traverse the tree, prune subtrees whose bounding boxes are further than the current best distance.

Problem: For d≳20, the pruning becomes ineffective (curse of dimensionality again). Query time degrades toward O(n).

Ball Trees

Partition data into nested hyperspheres (balls) instead of hyperrectangles. The pruning condition: if d(x,ball centre)−ball radius≥dcurrent best​, skip the subtree.

Ball trees outperform KD-trees in moderate-to-high dimensions because spheres tile space more efficiently than boxes.

sklearn default: algorithm='auto' selects KD-tree for d≤20, ball tree otherwise. Use algorithm='brute' for n<30 (overhead of tree construction not worth it).

Approximate Nearest Neighbours (ANN)

For very large n or high d: trade exact correctness for speed.

  • LSH (Locality Sensitive Hashing): hash similar points to the same bucket with high probability. O(1) expected query time with tunable accuracy.
  • HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World): graph-based, state-of-the-art for billion-scale ANN. Used in production vector databases (Faiss, Pinecone, Weaviate).
  • Faiss (Facebook AI Similarity Search): GPU-accelerated ANN, crucial for embedding-based retrieval in LLM systems.

✦ Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

KNN as the Baseline

In any ML project, KNN (with k chosen by cross-validation and features properly normalised) serves as a strong non-parametric baseline. If your fancy model can’t beat KNN, something is wrong.

The Manifold Hypothesis and Why Deep Learning Works

KNN’s curse of dimensionality is the problem that deep learning solves — implicitly.

The manifold hypothesis: real-world high-dimensional data (images, text, audio) does not fill Rd uniformly. It lies near a low-dimensional manifold M⊂Rd where the intrinsic dimension dintrinsic​≪d.

  • A 224×224 image is a point in R150528, but the set of natural images is not a random point cloud — it lies near a manifold of much lower dimension.
  • A neural network learns a feature map ϕ:Rd→Rm that unfolds this manifold, collapsing irrelevant variation (lighting, rotation) while preserving class structure.

KNN in the learned embedding space works dramatically better than KNN in pixel space. This is the basis of:

  • Face verification (DeepFace, FaceNet): train a CNN to embed faces into R128, then use 1-NN at test time
  • Retrieval-augmented generation: embed documents and queries into a shared space, retrieve nearest neighbours
  • Semantic search: all modern vector databases perform ANN in embedding space

Non-Parametric Methods in Modern ML

Several important modern ML techniques are extensions of or motivated by KNN:

TechniqueKNN Connection
Kernel regression / Nadaraya-WatsonWeighted KNN with a smooth kernel function instead of hard k-cutoff
Label propagationSemi-supervised: propagate labels through the KNN graph on unlabelled data
Graph neural networksMessage passing on a KNN graph built from feature similarities
Memory-augmented networksNeural architecture that explicitly stores and retrieves from an episodic memory, analogous to KNN’s instance store
k-NN language models (kNN-LM)At inference, retrieve k nearest contexts from a datastore and interpolate their next-token distributions with the neural LM — significant perplexity improvements on out-of-distribution text

KNN-LM: A Modern Research Application

Khandelwal et al. (2021, “Generalization through Memorization”) showed that adding a KNN retrieval component over a datastore of (ht​,wt+1​) pairs (hidden states and their next tokens) improves a pretrained language model without any fine-tuning:

p(wt​∣context)=λ⋅pKNN​(wt​∣ht​)+(1−λ)⋅pLM​(wt​∣ht​)

where pKNN​ is a softmax over distances to nearest neighbours in the datastore. This is KNN operating in the embedding space of a transformer — conceptually, it is the same algorithm, just at scale.

✦ Choosing k in Practice

Cross-validation is the only principled method. But some heuristics:

  • Rule of thumb: k≈n​ — no theoretical backing, but a reasonable starting point
  • Always use odd k for binary classification to avoid ties
  • Plot validation error vs. k: the curve typically descends then rises; choose the elbow

✦ Strengths and Weaknesses — Honest Assessment

Strengths

  • No assumptions about the data distribution (non-parametric) — works for any P(x,y)
  • Naturally multi-class — no one-vs-rest hacks needed
  • Adapts to local structure — can model complex, non-linear decision boundaries
  • Interpretable predictions — “prediction is blue because its 5 neighbours are all blue”
  • Online learning is trivial — just add new points to the training set
  • Theoretical guarantees — converges to Bayes-optimal as n→∞

Weaknesses

  • Slow inference at scale: O(nd) naïve; tree structures help but break down for d>20
  • Memory: stores the entire training set
  • Curse of dimensionality: accuracy degrades with d; preprocessing required
  • Sensitive to irrelevant features: noisy features pollute every distance computation — feature selection is important
  • Imbalanced classes: majority class dominates the vote — use class-weighted voting or oversample
  • No feature learning: cannot discover useful representations from raw data
