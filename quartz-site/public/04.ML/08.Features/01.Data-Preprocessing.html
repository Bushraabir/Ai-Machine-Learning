01.Data Preprocessing

01.Data Preprocessing

Apr 24, 202617 min read

“Models are only as good as the data and features you give them. Garbage in, garbage out — but more precisely: biased in, overconfident out.”

Part of: 08.Feature Engineering and Data Preprocessing · 00.ML Index Prereqs: 00.Maths · 00.Python · 03.Probability Review Next: 02.Feature Engineering · 03.Feature Selection

✦ Why Preprocessing Deserves Its Own Note

Raw data is almost never in a form a learning algorithm wants. The gap between data collection and model training is where most real ML projects succeed or fail. This note is not about tricks — it is about understanding why each transformation is necessary, what it breaks if you skip it, and what it assumes about the data.

The central tension in preprocessing: every transformation is an assumption about the data-generating process. Standardising a feature assumes the original scale was arbitrary. Mean-imputing assumes missingness is uninformative. These assumptions can be wrong. Knowing when they are wrong is the job.

✦ 1. Scaling

1.1 Why Scaling Matters

Many algorithms are sensitive to the scale (magnitude, units) of features. Consider two features: income in dollars (range ~20,000–200,000) and age in years (range ~18–80). Without scaling:

  • Distance-based algorithms (KNN, SVM with RBF kernel, K-Means): Euclidean distance is dominated by the feature with larger magnitude. Income would completely drown out age.
  • Gradient descent: The loss surface becomes elongated along the large-scale axes, causing slow convergence and oscillation.
  • Regularisation: L1 and L2 penalties are not scale-invariant. A feature on a large scale effectively receives a smaller penalty, distorting model selection.
  • PCA: Principal components are found by maximising variance. Without scaling, high-variance features dominate the first few components regardless of their relevance.

Algorithms that are scale-invariant and do not need scaling:

  • Decision trees and tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost) — splits compare feature values to thresholds, and multiplying a feature by a constant just shifts the threshold.
  • Naive Bayes (in most formulations).

1.2 Standardisation (Z-score Normalisation)

x′=σx−μ​

where μ=n1​∑i=1n​xi​ and σ=n1​∑i=1n​(xi​−μ)2​.

Effect: Transforms the feature to have zero mean and unit variance. The transformed distribution is not necessarily Gaussian — it depends on the original distribution. What you get is a feature centred at 0 with spread measured in standard deviations.

When to use: The default for most algorithms — SVMs, KNN, PCA, neural networks, logistic regression with regularisation.

What it assumes: The mean and variance are meaningful summary statistics. This holds for roughly unimodal, symmetric distributions. For heavy-tailed or multimodal distributions, it can still be done, but interpret with care.

Critical leakage rule: Compute μ and σ only on the training set. Apply the same parameters to the validation and test sets. Computing on the full dataset leaks test information into training.

1.3 Min-Max Scaling

x′=xmax​−xmin​x−xmin​​

Effect: Transforms values to [0,1]. The zero of the original scale maps to 0; the maximum maps to 1.

When to use:

  • When you need bounded output (e.g., feeding into sigmoid activations).
  • When the algorithm is sensitive to magnitude but you want to preserve exact zero (unlike standardisation, which shifts the mean away from zero).
  • Image pixel values: divide by 255 to get [0,1].

Vulnerability: Extremely sensitive to outliers. A single outlier at x=106 compresses everything else near 0. This is its main weakness compared to standardisation.

1.4 Robust Scaling

x′=IQR(x)x−median(x)​

where IQR(x)=Q3​(x)−Q1​(x), the interquartile range (75th percentile minus 25th percentile).

Effect: Like standardisation but using robust statistics. Outliers have little influence on the median and IQR, so the transformation is stable even when the feature has heavy tails or extreme values.

When to use: When your feature has outliers that you cannot or do not want to remove — financial data, medical measurements, sensor readings.

✦ 2. Missing Values

2.1 The Taxonomy of Missingness

This is the most underappreciated topic in preprocessing. Why data is missing determines what you can safely do about it.

MCAR — Missing Completely At Random The probability of a value being missing is independent of both the observed and unobserved data. Example: a sensor fails randomly with no relationship to the readings.

P(missing∣xobs​,xmiss​)=P(missing)

Safe to impute with simple statistics. The missingness is pure noise.

MAR — Missing At Random The probability of being missing depends on observed data, but not on the missing value itself. Example: younger patients are less likely to fill in income — income missingness depends on age (observed), not on income itself.

P(missing∣xobs​,xmiss​)=P(missing∣xobs​)

Can be handled with imputation that conditions on other features (e.g., KNN imputation, model-based imputation). Simple mean imputation is biased but often used pragmatically.

MNAR — Missing Not At Random The probability of being missing depends on the value itself. Example: high-income individuals are less likely to report income. The missing values are systematically different from the observed ones.

P(missing∣xobs​,xmiss​) depends on xmiss​

No imputation method can fully correct for MNAR without external information. The safest approach is to model the missingness mechanism explicitly, or at minimum add a missingness indicator feature. Pretending the data is MCAR when it is MNAR introduces systematic bias.

2.2 Imputation Methods

Mean/Median/Mode Imputation

Replace missing values with the column mean (numerical), median (numerical, robust to skew), or mode (categorical).

x^j​=xˉj​=nobs​1​∑i:xij​ observed​xij​

Why it underestimates variance: Every imputed value is identical (=xˉj​). This artificially reduces the variance of the feature, which then distorts downstream covariance estimates, correlations, and regularisation. As the fraction of missing values grows, this distortion compounds.

KNN Imputation

Find the k nearest non-missing neighbours (using observed features) and impute with their weighted mean.

Advantage: Conditions on other features — approximately handles MAR. Disadvantage: O(n2) in the number of samples, does not scale to large datasets. Sensitive to irrelevant features (curse of dimensionality again).

Missingness Indicator

For any feature j with missing values, add a binary feature:

mij​=1[xij​ is missing]

This lets the model learn that missingness itself is informative — which it is, under MAR and MNAR. Always add this when missingness rate is high or when the mechanism is not MCAR.

2.3 Decision Flowchart for Missingness

What fraction of values are missing in this feature?
    > 70%  → Consider dropping the feature entirely (inspect first)

✦ 3. Outlier Detection and Treatment

3.1 What Is an Outlier?

An outlier is a data point that is “far” from the bulk of the distribution. This is intentionally vague because “far” depends on the distribution, the feature, and the domain.

There are three distinct causes:

  1. Data entry / measurement error — the value is wrong. Drop or correct.
  2. Rare legitimate event — the value is correct but unusual. Handle carefully; it may be the most important data point.
  3. Distribution mismatch — the test data comes from a slightly different distribution. Not an error, but a signal about distribution shift.

3.2 Detection Methods

IQR Rule (Tukey’s fences): xi​ is an outlier if xi​<Q1​−1.5⋅IQR or xi​>Q3​+1.5⋅IQR

Non-parametric. Does not assume Gaussianity. The 1.5 factor is conventional; 3.0 gives “extreme outliers.”

Z-score Rule: xi​ is an outlier if ​σxi​−μ​​>3

Assumes roughly Gaussian distribution. For a true Gaussian, P(∣Z∣>3)≈0.003.

Isolation Forest: Tree-based anomaly detector. Partitions the space randomly; points that are isolated quickly (require few splits) are anomalies. Model-free, scales well.

3.3 Treatment

  • Winsorisation (clipping): Replace outliers with the boundary value (Q3​+1.5⋅IQR). Preserves the row, removes the extreme value.
  • Log transformation: For right-skewed data with extreme values, x′=log(x+1) compresses the tail without removing points. Requires x>0.
  • Robust models: If outliers are frequent, prefer models that are robust to them (e.g., use MAE loss instead of MSE, use median instead of mean, use Huber loss).
  • Do not automatically drop: An outlier is a legitimate data point until proven otherwise. Document every deletion.

✦ 4. Class Imbalance

4.1 The Problem

Consider a fraud detection dataset: 99% legitimate, 1% fraud. A model that always predicts “legitimate” achieves 99% accuracy and is completely useless. Accuracy is the wrong metric; but more fundamentally, the model will not learn to detect fraud because the loss is dominated by the majority class.

Imbalance ratio =Nminority​Nmajority​​. Ratios > 10:1 are often problematic; > 100:1 requires explicit handling.

4.2 Strategies

Cost-Sensitive Learning (Preferred)

Assign higher weight to the minority class in the loss function:

Lweighted​=−∑i​wyi​​[yi​logp^​i​+(1−yi​)log(1−p^​i​)]

where wminority​>wmajority​. A natural choice: wk​∝Nk​1​.

This is the cleanest approach: no synthetic data, no loss of real data. Most sklearn estimators accept class_weight='balanced', which computes wk​=K⋅Nk​N​ automatically.

Oversampling: SMOTE

SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling TEchnique): For each minority class point xi​, select a random neighbour xnn​ from its k nearest neighbours in the minority class, then generate a synthetic point:

xsynthetic​=xi​+λ(xnn​−xi​),λ∼Uniform(0,1)

This creates points along the line segment between existing minority points — interpolation in feature space, not extrapolation.

Important: SMOTE must be applied only to the training set, after the train/test split. Applying before splitting contaminates the test set with synthetic data derived from test points.

Variants:

  • ADASYN: Generates more synthetic samples near the decision boundary (harder examples get more weight).
  • Borderline-SMOTE: Only oversamples minority points near the boundary.

Undersampling

Randomly remove majority class samples to balance the ratio.

Advantage: Faster training (less data). Disadvantage: Discards real data — potentially discards informative majority examples. Use only when the majority class has abundant, redundant data.

Tomek Links: Remove majority class points that are nearest neighbours of minority class points. This cleans the boundary rather than random removal.

Threshold Adjustment

Instead of resampling, change the decision threshold. A classifier outputs p^​(y=1∣x); default threshold is 0.5. For imbalanced classes, lower the threshold to τ<0.5 to classify more instances as the minority class.

This trades off precision for recall. The right threshold is chosen using the Precision-Recall curve on a validation set.

4.3 Decision Guide

Imbalance ratio < 10:1  → class_weight='balanced' often sufficient
Imbalance ratio 10–100:1 → class_weight='balanced' + SMOTE on training data
Imbalance ratio > 100:1 → All of the above + careful threshold tuning + 
                           consider anomaly detection framing

✦ 5. The Preprocessing Pipeline and Leakage

5.1 The Central Rule

Any statistic estimated from data (means, standard deviations, quantiles, imputation values, encoding mappings) must be estimated exclusively on the training set, then applied identically to the test set.

Violating this is called data leakage. It makes your validation metrics optimistic and your model will fail in production.

Common leakage sources:

  • Scaling on the full dataset before splitting.
  • Computing SMOTE before splitting.
  • Target encoding using the full dataset’s target statistics.
  • Feature selection using the full dataset’s correlation with the target.

5.2 sklearn Pipeline: The Right Way

The Pipeline ensures that fit is called on the training fold and transform on the validation fold, for every step, automatically.

✦ 6. Connection to AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

6.1 Classical ML

In classical ML (SVMs, logistic regression, KNN), preprocessing is not optional for many algorithms — it is part of the algorithm. SVM with an RBF kernel is, in practice, SVM with an RBF kernel and standardisation. The two cannot be separated. This is why the sklearn Pipeline was invented.

6.2 Gradient-Based Optimisation (Deep Learning)

The gradient descent dynamics for a neural network are directly affected by feature scale. If input features have very different scales:

  • The loss surface has elongated, narrow valleys. Gradient descent steps that are good for one direction are too large for another, causing oscillation.
  • Batch normalisation (BN) can partially compensate inside the network, but it is not a substitute for preprocessing the inputs. BN normalises activations within the network at each layer — it does not know about the original units.

The standard practice in deep learning:

  • Standardise tabular inputs.
  • For images: divide by 255, then optionally subtract per-channel ImageNet means if using a pretrained backbone.
  • For text: handled by the tokeniser/embeddings.

6.3 Class Imbalance in Deep Learning

Loss weighting (class_weight in Keras, pos_weight in PyTorch’s BCEWithLogitsLoss) is the most common approach. SMOTE is less common in deep learning because:

  1. Models with millions of parameters can often learn minority class patterns from augmentation.
  2. Data augmentation (flipping, cropping, noise injection) serves the same variance-expansion role as SMOTE for images.
  3. Focal Loss (from RetinaNet) is a principled alternative: it down-weights easy majority examples and focuses training on hard minority examples.

Lfocal​=−αt​(1−p^​t​)γlog(p^​t​)

where (1−p^​t​)γ is the modulating factor: γ=0 recovers cross-entropy; γ=2 is the standard choice.

6.4 The Inductive Bias of Preprocessing Choices

Every preprocessing decision is an inductive bias — an assumption baked into the pipeline before the model sees a single parameter. Standardising a feature assumes the original scale was arbitrary (no information in “income measured in dollars vs. thousands of dollars”). Log-transforming assumes multiplicative rather than additive relationships. These are assumptions that can be right or wrong. In production ML systems, preprocessing bugs are at least as common as modelling bugs.

✦ Key Theorems and Facts

Fact 1: Standardisation does not change the shape of a distribution. If X is right-skewed, σX−μ​ is still right-skewed. To change shape, use log or Box-Cox transforms.

Fact 2: L2 regularisation is not scale-invariant. For a model y^​=wTx, the penalty λ∣w∣2 penalises small-scale features more (because a larger wj​ is needed to have the same effect). Standardisation makes the penalty treat all features equally.

Fact 3: SMOTE interpolates in feature space. If the minority class boundary is non-convex, SMOTE can generate points outside the true minority region. This is a known failure mode in high dimensions.

Fact 4: Under MNAR, any imputation method produces biased estimates of the feature distribution. The bias is proportional to the mean difference between missing and observed values, weighted by the missingness probability.

✦ Exit Criterion

You know this when:

  1. You can implement StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler, and RobustScaler in NumPy from scratch.
  2. You can explain why computing the scaler on the full dataset constitutes data leakage.
  3. You can distinguish MCAR, MAR, and MNAR and state which imputation strategies are appropriate for each.
  4. You can implement a leak-free sklearn Pipeline with ColumnTransformer for mixed numerical/categorical data.
  5. You can explain why class_weight=‘balanced’ is equivalent to weighting the loss function, not the data.
  6. You can derive why L2 regularisation is not scale-invariant.
