t-SNE — t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding

My note on the algorithm that made high-dimensional visualisation actually useful. The math is less clean than PCA, but the intuition is deep. Don’t treat this as a black box.

Tags: #dimensionality-reduction #unsupervised #visualisation #nonlinear
Part of: Dimensionality Reduction → UMAP → Representation Learning
Prerequisites: PCA, basic probability, KL divergence from Probability Review

✦ The Problem PCA Cannot Solve

PCA finds directions of maximum variance. It is a linear projection. That means it can only preserve structure that is linearly separable in the original space.

Consider the Swiss roll: a 2D manifold coiled in 3D. PCA projects it flat and destroys the manifold structure entirely. The distances that matter — distances along the manifold — get collapsed.

Swiss roll in 3D          PCA projection          t-SNE projection
     ╭──────╮               ████████               ·  ·  ·
    ╱  ····  ╲              ████████              ·      ·
   ╱  ·    ·  ╲   PCA→     ████████   t-SNE→    ·        ·
  ╱  ·      ·  ╲           ████████              ·      ·
 ╱  ············╲          ████████               ·  ·  ·
     (coiled)             (squashed)              (unrolled)

t-SNE’s insight: forget global distances. Preserve local neighbourhoods. If xi​ and xj​ are close in high-dimensional space, their images yi​ and yj​ should be close in 2D. That’s the whole game.

✦ The Algorithm: Full Derivation

Step 1 — High-Dimensional Similarities

For each pair (i,j), define the conditional probability that point i would pick j as its neighbour, where the neighbourhood is defined by a Gaussian centred at xi​:

pj∣i​=k=i∑​exp!(−∣xi​−xk​∣2/2σi2​)exp!(−∣xi​−xj​∣2/2σi2​)​

Note this is asymmetric: pj∣i​=pi∣j​ in general, because σi​=σj​.

To get a symmetric joint distribution (which is easier to optimise), symmetrise:

pij​=2npj∣i​+pi∣j​​

This guarantees ∑i,j​pij​=1 and pij​=pji​.

Why symmetrise? The original asymmetric SNE (Hinton & Roweis 2002) used pj∣i​ directly and had a separate cost per point. The symmetric version gives a cleaner single KL divergence to minimise, and empirically works at least as well.

Step 2 — The Perplexity Controls σi​

The bandwidth σi​ is not a single global parameter — it is set per point, via a binary search that achieves a target perplexity:

Perp(Pi​)=2H(Pi​),H(Pi​)=−∑j​pj∣i​log2​pj∣i​

Intuitively, perplexity ≈ the effective number of neighbours each point considers.

  • In dense regions, σi​ will be small (points are close; a narrow Gaussian suffices).
  • In sparse regions, σi​ will be large (need a wider Gaussian to include enough neighbours).

This adaptive bandwidth is what makes t-SNE robust to non-uniform density.

Typical values: Perp∈[5,50]. Van der Maaten recommends 30 as a default. For very large datasets (n>104), larger perplexities (50–100) can be better.

Step 3 — Low-Dimensional Similarities (The t-Distribution)

In the 2D embedding, we could use a Gaussian too. SNE does this. But there’s a problem: the crowding problem.

The crowding problem: In d dimensions, the volume of a thin shell of radius r scales as rd−1. When you project to 2D, there simply isn’t enough area to faithfully represent moderate distances — points at moderate distance in high-d get crushed against points that are truly close. The 2D Gaussian decays too fast at moderate distances; it can’t push moderately-distant points far enough apart.

The fix: use a Student’s t-distribution with 1 degree of freedom (the Cauchy distribution) in the low-dimensional space. Its heavier tails let moderately-distant clusters separate clearly:

qij​=k=l∑​(1+∣yk​−yl​∣2)−1(1+∣yi​−yj​∣2)−1​

The (1+∣yi​−yj​∣2)−1 is exactly the unnormalised Cauchy kernel. The key property: it has much heavier tails than a Gaussian, so unlike the Gaussian, it assigns non-negligible probability to moderately large distances.

Probability (unnorm.)
   │
 1 ┤  ██                  ← Gaussian: decays fast
   │  █ █                   t (ν=1): decays slow
   │  █   █        ████████████████████████████
   │  █     ██████
   │  █
   └─────────────────────────────────────────── distance
     0    1    2    3    4    5

The mismatch is the key: high-dim uses Gaussian (pij​), low-dim uses Student-t (qij​). This mismatch is intentional — it’s what creates the gap between clusters.

Step 4 — The Objective: Minimise KL Divergence

The objective is to find a 2D configuration yi​ such that Q is as close to P as possible, in the sense of KL divergence:

L=KL(P,∣,Q)=∑i=j​pij​logqij​pij​​

Why KL divergence, not MSE? Because we’re matching probability distributions, not raw distances. KL has an asymmetry that matters:

KL(P∣Q)=∑pij​logqij​pij​​

  • When pij​ is large (nearby in high-dim) and qij​ is small (far in low-dim): huge penalty. Nearby neighbours must stay nearby.
  • When pij​ is small (far in high-dim) and qij​ is large (close in low-dim): small penalty. The algorithm tolerates “false neighbours” more than it tolerates “torn neighbours”.

This asymmetry is what gives t-SNE its character: it prioritises preserving tight local structure above everything else.

Step 5 — Gradient and Optimisation

Taking ∂L/∂yi​:

∂yi​∂L​=4∑j​(pij​−qij​)(yi​−yj​)(1+∣yi​−yj​∣2)−1

Interpretation: this is a force equation. Each pair (i,j) exerts a force on yi​:

  • If pij​>qij​: they’re closer in high-dim than in low-dim → attractive force, pulls yi​ toward yj​.
  • If pij​<qij​: they’re closer in low-dim than in high-dim → repulsive force, pushes yi​ away from yj​.

The (1+∣yi​−yj​∣2)−1 term is the Cauchy weight — it amplifies attraction for nearby pairs and tapers repulsion at long range.

Optimisation uses gradient descent with momentum. The algorithm also uses early exaggeration: in the first ~250 iterations, multiply all pij​ by a factor (e.g. 4). This makes clusters form quickly before the repulsive forces spread them.

Computational complexity: Naïve: O(n2) per iteration. Barnes-Hut t-SNE approximation: O(nlogn).

✦ Full Algorithm Summary

Input: high-dim data X = {x₁,...,xₙ}
       perplexity Perp, iterations T, learning rate η, momentum α

1. Compute pairwise p_{j|i} via Gaussian with σᵢ found by binary search on Perp
2. Symmetrise: p_{ij} = (p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}) / 2n
3. Initialise Y = {y₁,...,yₙ} ~ 𝒩(0, 10⁻⁴ · I)
4. Early exaggeration: multiply p_{ij} × 4 for first 250 iterations
5. For t = 1 to T:
     a. Compute q_{ij} using Student-t kernel on current Y
     b. Compute gradient ∂ℒ/∂yᵢ for all i
     c. Update: Y(t) = Y(t-1) + η · ∇ℒ + α(t)(Y(t-1) - Y(t-2))
6. Return Y

✦ A Worked Example

Suppose n=4 points in 2D (toy high-dim space), and we want a 1D embedding.

Points: x1​=0, x2​=1, x3​=5, x4​=6

Distances: (1,2) are close. (3,4) are close. (1,3), etc., are far.

After computing pij​, the high similarities will be: p12​,p34​ large; p13​,p14​,p23​,p24​ small.

What t-SNE will do: push y1​,y2​ close together; push y3​,y4​ close together; push the two clusters apart. The gap between clusters will be amplified relative to the gap in the original data — that’s the heavy-tailed qij​ at work. In 2D MNIST embeddings, you can see this: the 10 digit classes form well-separated blobs, with the within-cluster distances compressed.

High-dim:   ·· · · · ·····
            0 1       5 6

t-SNE 1D:   ··             ····
           y₁y₂           y₃y₄
           (gap amplified by heavy tail)

✦ What t-SNE Preserves vs. Destroys

PropertyPreserved?Notes
Local neighbourhood structure✅ YesThe whole point
Cluster existence✅ Yes (qualitatively)Clusters that exist will be visible
Inter-cluster distances❌ NoCannot compare distances between clusters
Cluster sizes❌ NoCluster area in plot is meaningless
Global geometry❌ NoFar-apart points are unreliable
Out-of-sample points❌ No (standard)Must re-run entire algorithm

The most common misuse of t-SNE: “Cluster A is far from cluster B in the plot, so they are very different.” This is wrong. The inter-cluster distances in a t-SNE plot encode almost nothing. The existence and cohesion of clusters is meaningful; the layout of clusters relative to each other is not.

✦ Hyperparameters and Practical Guidance

Perplexity

  • Range: typically 5–50 (some recent work goes up to 100 for n>105)
  • Think of it as: how many effective neighbours does each point have?
  • Always run multiple perplexities. Different values reveal different structure.
  • If perplexity ≥n: every point is a neighbour of every other — you get a blob.

Number of iterations

  • Minimum ~250 for early exaggeration, ~750+ total. For large data, 1000–5000.
  • Watch the KL divergence — it should decrease and plateau.

Learning rate

  • Default: 200 (scikit-learn). If the embedding looks like a ball, increase it. If it looks like many tiny clusters, decrease it.
  • Recent recommendation (Belkina et al. 2019): \eta = n/\text{early_exaggeration}.

Random seed

  • t-SNE is non-convex. Always try multiple seeds before trusting a plot.
  • Two runs with different seeds can look qualitatively different.

✦ Connection to AI / ML / Deep Learning

1. Visualising Neural Network Representations

The canonical use: take a trained network (e.g., a CNN trained on MNIST or ImageNet), extract the penultimate layer activations for the test set, run t-SNE, and plot. If the network has learned good representations, the t-SNE plot will show semantically meaningful clusters.

Images → CNN → [h₁,...,h₅₁₂] → t-SNE → 2D plot
                  ↑
           high-dim feature space
           (not pixels — learned features)

This was how early deep learning papers demonstrated that CNNs learn semantic features rather than just memorising pixels (Krizhevsky et al., 2012; Maaten & Hinton, 2008).

2. Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning

In SimCLR and MoCo, the quality of learned representations is routinely assessed via t-SNE of the embedding space. A good contrastive model should push same-class examples together and different-class examples apart — exactly what t-SNE will make visible.

3. NLP: Word and Sentence Embeddings

Word2Vec, GloVe, and transformer embeddings are typically 300–768 dimensional. t-SNE collapses these to 2D for inspection. The famous word embedding plots (king − man + woman ≈ queen) are often visualised via PCA first, then t-SNE for finer structure.

4. Single-Cell Biology / Genomics (ML application)

One of the most successful ML applications of t-SNE outside CV/NLP: single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Each cell is a ~20,000-dimensional vector (gene expression). t-SNE reveals cell-type clusters that are impossible to see otherwise. This is now standard in computational biology.

5. Diagnosing Representation Collapse

In representation learning, representation collapse = the encoder maps all inputs to nearly the same embedding. t-SNE immediately reveals this: instead of structured clusters, you see a single blob with no structure.

6. Limitations in Deep Learning Pipelines

t-SNE’s O(n2) complexity (or O(nlogn) with Barnes-Hut) makes it impractical for full datasets. In practice: subsample n≤104 points before running t-SNE. UMAP is now often preferred at scale (see 04.Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)).

Use PCA first to reduce to ~50 dims, then run t-SNE on the PCA output. This is standard practice (van der Maaten’s original paper recommends it) — it removes noise while making t-SNE tractable.

This is for understanding. In practice, use sklearn.manifold.TSNE or openTSNE (which supports out-of-sample and parallelism).

✦ Common Failure Modes and Diagnostics

Problem: All points collapse to one blob
→ Learning rate too low, or too few iterations. Increase lr and check KL divergence is decreasing.

Problem: Many tiny isolated clusters with no structure
→ Learning rate too high, or perplexity too low. Reduce lr or increase perplexity.

Problem: Different runs give completely different plots
→ This is expected for t-SNE. Average multiple runs or switch to UMAP for reproducibility.

Problem: “I can see 5 clear clusters, but the true number is 3”
→ t-SNE can split true clusters. Each cluster’s internal structure can itself be projected as sub-clusters. Never use t-SNE for counting clusters — use silhouette analysis on the original space.
