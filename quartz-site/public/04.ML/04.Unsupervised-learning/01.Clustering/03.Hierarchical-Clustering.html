03.Hierarchical Clustering

Apr 26, 202617 min read

“Hierarchical clustering does not commit to a particular number of clusters. It reveals all scales of structure simultaneously — hand the dendrogram to the domain expert and let them decide where to cut.”

Part of: Clustering · 04.Unsupervised Learning
Requires: distance metrics (cf. 07.k-Nearest Neighbours), matrix operations
Connects to: 02.GMM (soft vs. hard assignment), 01.K-Means (flat vs. hierarchical), 01.Principal Component Analysis(PCA) (structure discovery)

✦ The Core Problem with Flat Clustering

Both K-Means and GMM require you to commit to k before you look at the data. That is a strong assumption — and often the wrong one.

Hierarchical clustering asks a different question: at every possible level of granularity, which points are most similar to each other? The answer is a tree, not a partition. You get a partition as a free byproduct by cutting the tree at any height.

There are two strategies:

StrategyDirectionStartEnd
AgglomerativeBottom-upn singletons1 cluster
DivisiveTop-down1 clustern singletons

Almost all practical use is agglomerative. Divisive methods are O(2n) to do optimally and rarely appear outside of theory.

✦ Agglomerative Clustering: The Algorithm

Procedure

Input: data X = {x₁, ..., xₙ}, linkage criterion L, distance metric d
Output: a binary merge tree (the dendrogram)

1. Initialise: each point is its own cluster Cᵢ = {xᵢ}
2. Compute the n×n distance matrix D where D[i,j] = d(xᵢ, xⱼ)
3. Repeat until one cluster remains:
   a. Find the pair (Cᵢ, Cⱼ) with minimum inter-cluster distance under L
   b. Merge Cᵢ and Cⱼ into a new cluster Cₖ = Cᵢ ∪ Cⱼ
   c. Record the merge height h = L(Cᵢ, Cⱼ) in the dendrogram
   d. Update the distance matrix: remove rows/cols for Cᵢ, Cⱼ; add row/col for Cₖ
4. Return the dendrogram

The algorithm is exact — no random initialisation, no local minima in the usual sense. The merge decisions are irrevocable (greedy), but the tree is globally determined.

Complexity

Naively: O(n3) — at each of the n−1 merge steps, scan the full (n−k)×(n−k) distance matrix.

With a priority queue (min-heap over cluster pairs): O(n2logn).

With specific linkages (single, complete, average) and the Lance-Williams update formula, the distance matrix can be updated in O(1) per merge rather than recomputed — bringing the best implementations to O(n2) space and O(n2logn) time.

The bottom line: Hierarchical clustering does not scale. For n≫104, consider approximate variants or switch to HDBSCAN.

✦ Linkage Criteria: The Heart of the Method

The linkage criterion defines what “distance between two clusters” means. This single choice determines the shapes of clusters the algorithm can discover.

Let CA​ and CB​ be two clusters. Let d(x,y) be a point-to-point distance (Euclidean by default).

Single Linkage (Minimum)

dsingle​(CA​,CB​)=minx∈CA​,,y∈CB​​d(x,y)

Uses the closest pair of points across the two clusters.

Behaviour: Chains. If there is any path of close points connecting two groups, single linkage will merge them early. Produces elongated, non-convex clusters. Extremely sensitive to noise and bridges.

•  •  •  •  •        •  •  •  •
     ↑ one bridging point merges everything

Use when: The true clusters are filamentary or non-convex (e.g., genomic sequences, manifold data). Single linkage is the basis for the minimum spanning tree — the dendrogram from single linkage encodes the MST of the data.

Complete Linkage (Maximum)

dcomplete​(CA​,CB​)=maxx∈CA​,,y∈CB​​d(x,y)

Uses the farthest pair of points across the two clusters.

Behaviour: Compactness. Merges are only made when the diameter of the resulting cluster is controlled. Tends to produce tight, roughly equal-sized clusters. Sensitive to outliers (a single far point inflates the inter-cluster distance).

Use when: You expect compact, roughly spherical clusters and want to avoid chaining.

Average Linkage (UPGMA)

davg​(CA​,CB​)=∣CA​∣∣CB​∣1​∑x∈CA​​∑y∈CB​​d(x,y)

Uses the mean pairwise distance between all cross-cluster point pairs.

Behaviour: A compromise. Less sensitive to outliers than complete linkage, less prone to chaining than single linkage. The workhorse choice for most applications. Used extensively in phylogenetics.

UPGMA = Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic mean. There is also WPGMA (weighted), which accounts for unequal cluster sizes differently.

Ward’s Linkage

Ward’s criterion is fundamentally different — it is not based on pairwise point distances at all.

dWard​(CA​,CB​)=∣CA​∣+∣CB​∣∣CA​∣∣CB​∣​∣μA​−μB​∣2

where μA​,μB​ are the centroids of CA​,CB​.

Interpretation: At each step, Ward merges the pair of clusters whose union results in the minimum increase in total within-cluster variance (sum of squared deviations from centroid):

ΔW=W(CA​∪CB​)−W(CA​)−W(CB​)

where W(C)=∑x∈C​∣x−μC​∣2.

You can verify the equivalence algebraically — the formula above for dWard​ is exactly this incremental variance.

Behaviour: Strongly prefers merging small, compact clusters. Tends to produce roughly equal-sized clusters of similar variance. The most commonly recommended default for exploratory work on Euclidean data.

Requires: Euclidean distance specifically. Ward’s criterion is not well-defined with non-Euclidean metrics (you need a notion of centroid).

Centroid Linkage

dcentroid​(CA​,CB​)=∣μA​−μB​∣2

Distance between cluster centroids.

Pathology: Can exhibit inversions — a merge at a later step has a lower height than an earlier merge, making the dendrogram non-monotone. Generally avoided.

✦ The Dendrogram

The dendrogram is the primary output. It is a binary tree where:

  • Leaves are the individual data points x1​,…,xn​.
  • Each internal node represents a merge event.
  • The height of an internal node = the inter-cluster distance at which that merge occurred (under the chosen linkage).
  • The horizontal position of leaves is arbitrary (reordered for visual clarity).

Reading a Dendrogram

Height
  │
  │    ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
4 │    │                                     │
  │  ┌─┴──────┐                        ┌────┴────┐
2 │  │        │                        │         │
  │ ┌┴┐    ┌──┴──┐                  ┌──┴──┐   ┌──┴──┐
1 │ │ │    │     │                  │     │   │     │
  │ A B    C     D                  E     F   G     H
  • Cut at height h=2: three clusters A,B, C,D, E,F,G,H
  • Cut at height h=4: two clusters A,B,C,D, E,F,G,H
  • The cophenetic distance between points A and C is 2 (the height of their LCA).

The Cophenetic Correlation Coefficient

For a given dendrogram and pairwise distance matrix D, define the cophenetic matrix C where C[i,j] = height at which xi​ and xj​ first merged.

rcophenetic​=corr(D,C)

High cophenetic correlation (>0.8) means the dendrogram faithfully represents the pairwise distances. Use this to compare linkages on a given dataset.

✦ Choosing the Number of Clusters

Since the dendrogram gives you all possible k simultaneously, the question is where to cut.

Method 1: Visual inspection

Cut where there is a large gap in merge heights — a long vertical line in the dendrogram before the next merge. This gap signals that the next merge would join genuinely dissimilar clusters.

Method 2: Silhouette Score

For a cut producing k clusters with assignment C(x):

s(x)=max(a(x),b(x))b(x)−a(x)​

where:

  • a(x) = mean distance from x to all points in its own cluster (cohesion)
  • b(x) = mean distance from x to all points in the nearest other cluster (separation)

s(x)∈[−1,1]. Mean silhouette score near 1 → well-separated clusters. Choose k that maximises the mean silhouette.

Method 3: Inconsistency Criterion

For each merge at height h, compare h to the mean and standard deviation of the heights of all merges below it (within a depth window). If h is many standard deviations above the local mean, it is “inconsistent” — a natural cut.

✦ The Lance-Williams Update Formula

Rather than recomputing all inter-cluster distances from scratch after each merge, most implementations use the Lance-Williams recurrence: after merging Ci​ and Cj​ into Ck​, the distance from Ck​ to any other cluster Cq​ is:

d(Ck​,Cq​)=αi​⋅d(Ci​,Cq​)+αj​⋅d(Cj​,Cq​)+β⋅d(Ci​,Cj​)+γ⋅∣d(Ci​,Cq​)−d(Cj​,Cq​)∣

The coefficients (αi​,αj​,β,γ) depend on the linkage:

Linkageαi​αj​βγ
Single1/21/20−1/2
Complete1/21/20+1/2
Average (UPGMA)ni​+nj​ni​​ni​+nj​nj​​00
Wardni​+nj​+nq​ni​+nq​​ni​+nj​+nq​nj​+nq​​ni​+nj​+nq​−nq​​0

This turns an O(n2) recomputation into O(n) per merge — a crucial speedup.

✦ Worked Example (by hand)

Data: 5 points with the following distance matrix:

- & 1 & 5 & 7 & 8 \ 1 & - & 4 & 6 & 7 \ 5 & 4 & - & 2 & 3 \ 7 & 6 & 2 & - & 1 \ 8 & 7 & 3 & 1 & - \end{pmatrix}$$ Points: $A, B, C, D, E$ (rows/columns in order). **Single linkage step-by-step:** 1. Minimum distance: $D[A,B] = 1$ and $D[D,E] = 1$. Say we merge $D$ and $E$ first → cluster $DE$ at height $1$. 2. Update distances to $DE$ (single linkage = min): - $d(A, DE) = \min(7,8) = 7$ - $d(B, DE) = \min(6,7) = 6$ - $d(C, DE) = \min(2,3) = 2$ New matrix ($A, B, C, DE$): $$\begin{pmatrix} - & 1 & 5 & 7 \ 1 & - & 4 & 6 \ 5 & 4 & - & 2 \ 7 & 6 & 2 & - \end{pmatrix}$$ 3. Minimum: $D[A,B] = 1$ → merge $AB$ at height $1$. 4. Update ($AB, C, DE$): - $d(AB, C) = \min(5,4) = 4$ - $d(AB, DE) = \min(7,6) = 6$ - $d(C, DE) = 2$ 5. Minimum: $D[C, DE] = 2$ → merge $CDE$ at height $2$. 6. Final merge: $AB$ and $CDE$ at height $4$. **Resulting dendrogram heights:** $1, 1, 2, 4$. Cut at height $h < 2$: four clusters ${A,B}, {C}, {D}, {E}$. Cut at $2 \leq h < 4$: two clusters ${A,B}$, ${C,D,E}$. --- ## ✦ Divisive Clustering (DIANA) DIANA (DIvisive ANAlysis) works top-down: 1. Start with all $n$ points in one cluster. 2. At each step, select the cluster with the **highest average dissimilarity** (the most "spread out"). 3. Find the point within that cluster that is most dissimilar to all others. It becomes a "splinter group." 4. Assign each remaining point in that cluster to either the original group or the splinter group based on which has smaller average distance. 5. Repeat. **Complexity:** $O(n^2)$ per split (computing average dissimilarities), $O(n^3)$ total in the naive form. DIANA is implemented in R's `cluster` package. Rarely used in Python/sklearn contexts. The primary advantage: it may discover the largest split more accurately (the first split of the whole dataset is globally informed, whereas agglomerative methods build up from many local decisions). --- ## ✦ Visualisation ``` SINGLE LINKAGE COMPLETE LINKAGE WARD'S LINKAGE (chaining tendency) (compact clusters) (equal-size tendency) Height Height Height │ ┌────────────┐ │ ┌──────┐ ┌──────┐ │ ┌────┐ ┌────┐ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ │ ┌┴┐ ┌───┐ │ │ ┌┴┐ ┌─┴┐ ┌┴┐ ┌─┴┐ │ ┌┴┐ ┌┴┐ ┌┴┐ ┌┴┐ │ A B C D E │ A B C D E F G H │ A B C D E F G H → Elongated clusters, → Compact, spherical → Balanced, similar sensitive to noise clusters, less chaining sized clusters ``` --- ## ✦ Connection to Minimum Spanning Tree **Theorem:** The dendrogram produced by **single linkage** agglomerative clustering is equivalent to the **Minimum Spanning Tree (MST)** of the complete graph on $n$ points (where edge weights are pairwise distances). _Proof sketch._ The MST merges nodes by the smallest edge weight not forming a cycle — exactly the greedy nearest-pair rule of single linkage. The merge order in single linkage is the edge order of Kruskal's algorithm. **Consequence:** Single linkage runs in $O(n^2)$ (computing pairwise distances dominates) — but can be accelerated to $O(n \log n)$ for low-dimensional data via Prim's or Kruskal's algorithm on a spatial graph. --- ## ✦ Generalisation: HDBSCAN Standard hierarchical clustering requires all points to belong to a cluster. HDBSCAN (Hierarchical DBSCAN) builds a hierarchy of density-based clusters and automatically identifies noise: 1. Compute **core distances**: $d_{\text{core},k}(x)$ = distance to $k$-th nearest neighbour. 2. Compute **mutual reachability distance**: $d_{\text{mreach}}(x, y) = \max(d_{\text{core}}(x), d_{\text{core}}(y), d(x,y))$ — smooths out low-density regions. 3. Build the MST of the mutual reachability graph. 4. Extract a **condensed cluster tree** by collapsing small clusters below a `min_cluster_size` threshold. 5. Select persistent clusters from the condensed tree (those with high "stability"). **Advantages over vanilla hierarchical clustering:** - Automatically determines the number of clusters. - Handles varying-density clusters. - Identifies noise points explicitly. - Scalable: $O(n \log n)$ in practice with approximate NN graphs. HDBSCAN should be considered the modern default when DBSCAN or hierarchical clustering is appropriate. Available as `hdbscan` (pip) or `sklearn.cluster.HDBSCAN` (sklearn ≥ 1.3). --- ## ✦ Connection to AI, ML, and Deep Learning Hierarchical clustering is not merely a standalone tool — it appears throughout the ML ecosystem in both practical and theoretical roles. ### Taxonomic Embeddings and Word Hierarchies Hierarchical structure is latent in many ML problems. In NLP, **WordNet** organises concepts hierarchically (hypernym/hyponym relations). Dendrograms built on word embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe) often recover semantic groupings — verbs cluster by type, nouns by domain — without labels. ### Transfer Learning and Feature Hierarchy Deep neural networks learn **hierarchical representations**: edges → textures → parts → objects (cf. [[04.Representation Learning]]). Applying agglomerative clustering to neuron activations at intermediate layers is a diagnostic tool for understanding what a network has learned. SVCCA and representational similarity analysis use hierarchical clustering to compare layer representations across networks. ### Hierarchical Softmax In language models with large vocabularies ($\sim 10^5$ words), the softmax over all classes is expensive: $O(V)$ per token. **Hierarchical softmax** organises vocabulary into a binary tree (often built by agglomerative clustering of word frequencies or embeddings). Prediction traverses the tree: $O(\log V)$ per token. Used in early Word2Vec training. ### HAC for Coreference Resolution Coreference resolution (deciding which mentions in text refer to the same entity) is naturally hierarchical. Agglomerative clustering with a learned similarity model (e.g., a trained neural scorer) is a standard approach in NLP pipelines. ### Neural Clustering and Differentiable Hierarchies Recent work makes the dendrogram itself differentiable. **DIFFRAC** (Wilder et al., 2019) and **SoftTree** methods relax the hard merge decisions to probability distributions, enabling end-to-end gradient-based learning of cluster structure — pushing hierarchical clustering into the deep learning paradigm. ### Hyperparameter Tuning Visualisation When performing a grid search over hyperparameters, agglomerative clustering on the resulting performance matrix (with Ward's linkage) reveals which hyperparameter combinations behave similarly — useful for pruning search spaces. ### Federated Learning In federated learning, client models can be hierarchically clustered by their gradient similarity. This reveals data heterogeneity: clients with similar gradient updates probably have similar data distributions. The cluster structure informs personalisation strategies. --- ## ✦ Implementation (NumPy, from scratch) ```python import numpy as np from scipy.spatial.distance import pdist, squareform def hierarchical_cluster(X, linkage='single'): """ Agglomerative clustering returning the merge sequence. Returns: list of (cluster_i, cluster_j, height, new_size) — the linkage matrix. """ n = len(X) D = squareform(pdist(X, metric='euclidean')) # (n x n) distance matrix np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf) # Track which original indices belong to each "active" cluster clusters = {i: [i] for i in range(n)} active = list(range(n)) linkage_matrix = [] for step in range(n - 1): # Find the minimum inter-cluster distance min_dist = np.inf merge_i, merge_j = -1, -1 for ii, i in enumerate(active): for jj, j in enumerate(active): if ii >= jj: continue if D[i, j] < min_dist: min_dist = D[i, j] merge_i, merge_j = i, j new_id = n + step size_new = len(clusters[merge_i]) + len(clusters[merge_j]) linkage_matrix.append((merge_i, merge_j, min_dist, size_new)) # Compute distances from new cluster to remaining clusters new_dists = {} for k in active: if k == merge_i or k == merge_j: continue if linkage == 'single': new_dists[k] = min(D[merge_i, k], D[merge_j, k]) elif linkage == 'complete': new_dists[k] = max(D[merge_i, k], D[merge_j, k]) elif linkage == 'average': ni, nj = len(clusters[merge_i]), len(clusters[merge_j]) new_dists[k] = (ni * D[merge_i, k] + nj * D[merge_j, k]) / (ni + nj) elif linkage == 'ward': ni = len(clusters[merge_i]) nj = len(clusters[merge_j]) nk = len(clusters[k]) new_dists[k] = ( (ni + nk) / (ni + nj + nk) * D[merge_i, k] + (nj + nk) / (ni + nj + nk) * D[merge_j, k] - nk / (ni + nj + nk) * D[merge_i, merge_j] ) # Update data structures clusters[new_id] = clusters[merge_i] + clusters[merge_j] active.remove(merge_i) active.remove(merge_j) active.append(new_id) # Expand D with new row/col D_new = np.full((len(D) + 1, len(D) + 1), np.inf) D_new[:len(D), :len(D)] = D for k, dist in new_dists.items(): D_new[new_id, k] = dist D_new[k, new_id] = dist D = D_new return linkage_matrix def cut_tree(linkage_matrix, n_clusters): """Cut the dendrogram to produce a flat clustering.""" n = len(linkage_matrix) + 1 # number of original points # Each step in the linkage_matrix corresponds to a merge # Stop after (n - n_clusters) merges cuts = n_clusters # Use a union-find structure parent = list(range(2 * n - 1)) for step, (i, j, h, size) in enumerate(linkage_matrix): if n - 1 - step < cuts: break new_id = n + step parent[int(i)] = new_id parent[int(j)] = new_id def find(x): while parent[x] != x: parent[x] = parent[parent[x]] x = parent[x] return x return [find(i) for i in range(n)] ``` **Using scipy (production):** ```python from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster import matplotlib.pyplot as plt Z = linkage(X, method='ward', metric='euclidean') # Z[k] = [i, j, height, size] for the k-th merge # Plot dendrogram plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5)) dendrogram(Z, labels=labels, color_threshold=0.7 * max(Z[:, 2])) plt.xlabel('Sample'); plt.ylabel('Distance') plt.title('Ward Linkage Dendrogram') plt.tight_layout() # Extract flat clusters labels_k = fcluster(Z, t=4, criterion='maxclust') # exactly 4 clusters labels_h = fcluster(Z, t=2.5, criterion='distance') # cut at height 2.5 ``` --- ## ✦ Compared to K-Means and GMM |Property|K-Means|GMM|Hierarchical| |---|---|---|---| |Requires $k$ upfront|Yes|Yes|No (choose at cut time)| |Cluster shape|Spherical|Ellipsoidal|Depends on linkage| |Soft assignments|No|Yes|No| |Probabilistic model|No|Yes|No| |Deterministic|No (init)|No (init)|Yes (given $d$, linkage)| |Scales to large $n$|Yes|Yes (approx)|No ($O(n^2)$ min)| |Hierarchical output|No|No|Yes| |Handles noise|No|No|No (HDBSCAN does)| The key unique value proposition of hierarchical clustering: **it reveals all scales of structure without committing to $k$**, and the result is interpretable as a tree. --- ## ✦ Practical Guidance **When to use hierarchical clustering:** - You do not know $k$ and want to explore cluster structure visually. - You need a tree representation (phylogenetics, taxonomy, customer hierarchy). - $n$ is modest ($n \lesssim 5000$) and interpretability matters. - The ground truth clusters are non-convex and you suspect single linkage will capture them. **When not to use it:** - $n > 10^4$: use HDBSCAN, K-Means, or mini-batch variants. - You need cluster probabilities: use GMM. - You need online/streaming clustering: use MiniBatchKMeans or BIRCH. **Feature scaling:** Required. Like all distance-based methods, hierarchical clustering is not scale-invariant. Always standardise features before computing the distance matrix (unless using a domain-specific metric that already accounts for scale). **Linkage choice heuristic:** - Default: Ward's (compact, equal-size clusters, Euclidean data). - Non-Euclidean or precomputed distance matrix: average or complete. - Known non-convex structure: single. - High noise: avoid single; use complete or Ward's. --- ## ✦ Exit Criterion > _You know this when:_ you can implement agglomerative clustering with all four linkages from the Lance-Williams update formula without looking it up; read a dendrogram and identify the appropriate cut; explain why single linkage chains and Ward's does not; prove the equivalence of single linkage and MST; and articulate exactly why hierarchical clustering does not scale and what to use instead. --- ## ✦ References - **ESL** Ch. 14.3.2 — Agglomerative clustering, linkage criteria, dendrograms - **PRML** Ch. 9 (brief) — connection to mixture models and EM - **Géron** Ch. 9 — Practical sklearn usage, DBSCAN comparison - **Müller & Guido** Ch. 3 — Worked examples with dendrograms, linkage comparison - **AIMA** Ch. 20 — Clustering in the context of unsupervised learning - **CS 229** — Lecture notes on unsupervised learning - Murtagh, F. & Legendre, P.
