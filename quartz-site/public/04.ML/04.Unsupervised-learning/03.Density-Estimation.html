03.Density Estimation

03.Density Estimation

Apr 26, 202614 min read

The most honest unsupervised learning problem: given samples from an unknown distribution p(x), estimate p itself — not a clustering, not a projection, but the actual probability measure over the input space. Everything generative — VAEs, diffusion models, normalising flows — is a density estimator dressed up in deep learning clothing.

Tags: #unsupervised #probability #generative #nonparametric
Part of: Unsupervised Learning → Part 4
Prerequisites: MLE, KL divergence, exponential family, covariance structure, basic calculus

✦ What Density Estimation Actually Is

We observe D=x1​,…,xn​∼i.i.d.px, where px is the true but unknown data-generating distribution. The goal: construct an estimate p^​(x) such that p^​≈p∗ in some meaningful sense.

This is harder than it sounds. Three fundamental difficulties:

1. The curse of dimensionality. In d dimensions, the volume of space grows as rd. To cover the space with fixed-size bins, you need exponentially many bins. Data becomes exponentially sparse. Every nonparametric method eventually breaks down as d grows — the question is only how gracefully.

2. The normalisation constraint. A valid density must satisfy ∫p^​(x),dx=1. Enforcing this while fitting data is non-trivial. Some methods (histograms, KDE) build normalisation in by construction; others (energy-based models) require explicit computation of the partition function, which is itself intractable in high dimensions.

3. The evaluation problem. How do you measure how well p^​ approximates p^_? You cannot compute \int p^_(x) \log \hat{p}(x), dx exactly because you don’t have p∗. You must estimate goodness-of-fit from the same data used for fitting — which requires care to avoid circular reasoning.

✦ Parametric Density Estimation

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (Review)

Given a parametric family pθ​:θ∈Θ, MLE finds:

θ^=argmaxθ​∑i=1n​logpθ​(xi​)

This is equivalent to minimising the KL divergence from the empirical distribution p^​n​=n1​∑i​δxi​​ to pθ​:

DKL​(p^​n​∣pθ​)=Ep^​n​​[logp^​n​(x)]−Ep^​n​​[logpθ​(x)]

The first term is constant in θ, so minimising KL is the same as maximising ∑i​logpθ​(xi​).

Implication: MLE is always doing implicit density estimation — it fits a model pθ​ to minimise KL from the data. This unifies regression, classification, and density estimation under one objective.

For a Gaussian: μ^​=xˉ, Σ^=n1​∑i​(xi​−xˉ)(xi​−xˉ)T. These are the closed-form MLEs, derivable by setting ∇μ​logL=0 and ∇Σ​logL=0.

Limitation: If the true p∗ is not in the chosen family, MLE gives the best approximation within the family — which may be very poor. This is model misspecification, and it is the central limitation of parametric methods.

✦ Histograms

The simplest nonparametric density estimator. Partition Rd into bins Bk​ of equal width h. Estimate:

\hat{p}(x) = \frac{#{x_i \in B_k(x)}}{n \cdot h^d}

where Bk​(x) is the bin containing x, and hd is the bin volume in d dimensions.

Bias-Variance Analysis

Let p^_ be the true density, and suppose p^_ is differentiable. Within a bin of width h, the density is approximated as constant. The bias is:

Bias[p^​(x)]≈2h​⋅∂x∂p∗​​x0​​

proportional to h — larger bins mean more averaging, more bias.

The variance at a point is (by a Binomial argument, since bin counts are Binomial(n,pk​)):

Var[p^​(x)]=nhdp∗(x)​

The mean squared error at a point is then:

\text{MSE}[\hat{p}(x)] \approx \frac{h^2}{4}\left(\frac{\partial p^_}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \frac{p^_(x)}{nh^d}

Setting dhd​MSE=0 gives the optimal bandwidth:

h∗∝n−1/(d+2)

and the optimal MSE scales as n−2/(d+2). For d=1: n−2/3. For d=10: n−1/6. This is the curse of dimensionality made concrete — convergence slows dramatically in high dimensions.

Problems with Histograms

  • Discontinuities at bin boundaries — the estimated density is a step function, which is often a poor model.
  • Bin origin sensitivity — shifting the bin grid by h/2 can give a qualitatively different picture.
  • Scaling to d>2 — the number of bins grows as (1/h)d; most bins empty; estimate is zero almost everywhere.

KDE fixes all three.

✦ Kernel Density Estimation

Definition

KDE places a smooth kernel centred at each data point:

p^​(x)=nhd1​∑i=1n​K!(hx−xi​​)

where K:Rd→R≥0​ is a kernel satisfying:

∫K(u),du=1,K(u)=K(−u),∫uK(u),du=0

The bandwidth h>0 controls the smoothness.

Normalisation is automatic: since each K integrates to 1, and we have n terms each scaled by 1/n, ∫p^​(x),dx=1 always.

Intuition

Histograms assign a point’s contribution only to the bin it falls in — a hard boundary. KDE spreads each point’s contribution smoothly over its neighbourhood. The bandwidth h controls the radius of this neighbourhood.

Common Kernels

All kernels K(u) are written in the univariate case. For multivariate, use the product kernel K(u)=∏j=1d​K(uj​) or a radially symmetric version K(∣u∣).

| Kernel | K(u), ∣u∣≤1 | Support | Notes | |---|---|---|---| | Gaussian | 2π​1​e−u2/2 | R | Smooth, infinite support; most common | | Epanechnikov | 43​(1−u2) | [−1,1] | MSE-optimal; compact support | | Uniform | 21​ | [−1,1] | Box kernel; same as histogram | | Triweight | 3235​(1−u2)3 | [−1,1] | Very smooth, compact | | Biweight | 1615​(1−u2)2 | [−1,1] | Common in practice |

The Epanechnikov kernel is asymptotically optimal in terms of minimising integrated MSE (MISE). In practice the difference between kernels is small — bandwidth matters far more than kernel choice.

Bias-Variance Decomposition for KDE

Under smoothness assumptions on p^_ (specifically, p^_ twice differentiable):

Bias[p^​(x)]≈2h2​σK2​,∇2p∗(x),σK2​=∫u2K(u),du

Var[p^​(x)]≈nhdp∗(x)∣K∣22​​,∣K∣22​=∫K2(u),du

The integrated (over all x) mean squared error (MISE) is:

MISE(p^​)≈4h4​σK4​∫(∇2p∗)2,dx+nhd∣K∣22​​

Setting dhd​MISE=0:

h∗=(σK4​n∫(∇2p∗)2,dxd∣K∣22​​)1/(d+4)

h∗∝n−1/(d+4)

and optimal MISE scales as n−4/(d+4). For d=1: n−4/5. For d=10: n−2/7≈n−0.286. Again, the curse — but KDE converges slightly faster than histograms (exponent d+44​ vs d+22​).

Bandwidth Selection

The optimal h^_ depends on \int (\nabla^2 p^_)^2, dx, which requires knowing p∗ — circular. In practice:

Silverman’s Rule of Thumb (for Gaussian kernel, 1D, assuming p∗ is Gaussian):

hSilverman​=1.06,σ^,n−1/5

where σ^=min(std dev,IQR/1.34) — the IQR term makes it robust to outliers.

For multivariate data, generalises to:

hj​=((d+2)n4​)1/(d+4)σ^j​

This is fast but assumes Gaussianity. For multimodal p∗, it over-smooths.

Cross-Validation Bandwidth Selection:

Minimise the leave-one-out (LOO) log-likelihood:

h^=argmaxh​∑i=1n​logp^​−i​(xi​)

where p^​−i​ is the KDE computed without point xi​. This is principled but O(n2) to evaluate for each h.

Likelihood cross-validation is equivalent to minimising DKL​(p∗∣p^​) — it finds h such that the KDE is closest to the truth in KL sense, given the data.

KDE as a Convolution

Notice that:

p^​(x)=nhd1​∑i​K!(hx−xi​​)=hd1​(Kh​∗p^​n​)(x)

where p^​n​=n1​∑i​δxi​​ is the empirical measure and Kh​(u)=K(u/h).

KDE is smoothing the empirical distribution by convolution with the kernel. In the Fourier domain, convolution becomes pointwise multiplication — the kernel attenuates high-frequency components of the empirical distribution. Larger h → more attenuation → smoother estimate. This is the exact same operation as applying a low-pass filter in signal processing.

✦ A Worked Example

Suppose n=5 1D observations: x1​=0.2,,x2​=0.4,,x3​=0.5,,x4​=1.8,,x5​=2.0.

Using a Gaussian kernel with h=0.3:

p^​(x)=5⋅0.31​∑i=15​2π​1​exp!(−2⋅0.09(x−xi​)2​)

At x=0.35 (between x1​ and x2​):

  • Contribution from x2​=0.4: exp(−(0.35−0.4)2/0.18)=exp(−0.014)≈0.986
  • Contribution from x1​=0.2: exp(−(0.35−0.2)2/0.18)=exp(−0.125)≈0.882
  • Contributions from x3​,x4​,x5​: negligible (more than 2h away)

The density at x=0.35 is notably higher than at x=1.0 (between the two clusters). With h=0.3, the two clusters at 0.2,0.4,0.5 and 1.8,2.0 remain separated. With h=1.0, they blur together into one mode.

✦ Multivariate KDE and the Bandwidth Matrix

In d dimensions, the full bandwidth generalisation uses a bandwidth matrix H (symmetric positive definite, d×d):

p^​(x)=n∣H∣1/21​∑i=1n​K!(H−1/2(x−xi​))

The diagonal case H=diag(h12​,…,hd2​) uses a separate bandwidth per dimension — appropriate when dimensions have different scales. The full matrix H also captures correlations between dimensions.

In practice: most implementations use a single scalar bandwidth (or one per dimension after standardising). Full bandwidth matrix selection is O(d2) parameters — expensive, rarely done.

The practical rule: always standardise features before applying KDE in d>1.

✦ Connection to Other Methods

KDE and Naive Bayes

In Gaussian Naive Bayes, we fit a Gaussian p(xj​∣y=k) per feature per class — this is parametric density estimation within each class. Kernel Naive Bayes replaces the Gaussian fit with a KDE: p^​(xj​∣y=k)=KDE of class-k samples. More flexible, no Gaussianity assumption.

KDE and KNN

The k-nearest-neighbour density estimator is closely related:

p^​(x)=nVk​(x)k​

where Vk​(x) is the volume of the ball containing the k nearest neighbours of x. This is KDE with a variable bandwidth — the bandwidth adapts to local density. In dense regions, the ball is small (narrow bandwidth); in sparse regions, the ball is large (wide bandwidth). This is more adaptive than fixed-bandwidth KDE.

KDE and Anomaly Detection

An observation x is anomalous if p^​(x)<τ for some threshold τ. This is the cleanest formulation of density-based anomaly detection. Local Outlier Factor (LOF) is an extension that computes local density ratios rather than absolute densities.

KDE and Clustering: Mean Shift

The mean shift algorithm is gradient ascent on the KDE surface:

x←∑i​K!(hx−xi​​)∑i​xi​K!(hx−xi​​)​

Each step moves x toward the local mean of nearby points, weighted by the kernel. Iterate to convergence → find local modes of p^​. Cluster by assigning points to the mode they converge to. Mean shift discovers clusters as basins of attraction of the density, without requiring the number of clusters to be specified.

✦ Evaluating a Density Estimator

Log-Likelihood on Held-Out Data

The most principled metric: fit p^​ on training data, evaluate ntest​1​∑i​logp^​(xitest​) on a held-out set. Higher is better. This is an unbiased estimator of \mathbb{E}_{p^_}[\log \hat{p}(x)] = -H(p^_, \hat{p}) (the negative cross-entropy).

Equivalently, minimising negative log-likelihood on test data is the same as minimising DKL​(p∗∣p^​) (up to a constant H(p∗)).

Integrated Squared Error (ISE)

ISE(p^​)=∫(p^​(x)−p∗(x))2,dx

Theoretically clean, rarely computable in practice without knowing p∗. Used for theoretical analysis (MISE = E[ISE]).

Visual / Qualitative Assessment

For d≤2: plot p^​ and compare to histograms at multiple resolutions. Check that modes correspond to data clusters. A density that places mass in empty regions or misses visible clusters is poor.

The Kernel Trick for ISE

An interesting fact: for KDE, E[ISE] can be computed analytically up to the unknown term ∫p∗2(x),dx, which is constant in h. This enables cross-validation bandwidth selection without numerical integration:

MISE(h)=∫p^​2(x),dx−n2​∑i​p^​−i​(xi​)+const

The first term has a closed form for Gaussian kernels (it’s a sum of Gaussian convolutions), and the second term is the LOO estimate. This is the least-squares cross-validation criterion for h.

✦ Deep Generative Models as Density Estimators (Preview)

Classical nonparametric methods break down in high dimensions. Deep generative models are the modern answer. A brief orientation:

Normalising Flows

An invertible differentiable map fθ​:Z→X transforms a simple base distribution pZ​ (e.g., N(0,I)) into a complex pX​. The change-of-variables formula gives the exact likelihood:

logpX​(x)=logpZ​(fθ−1​(x))+log​detJfθ−1​​(x)​

Trained by MLE. Exact samples and exact likelihoods. Expensive to compute the Jacobian determinant — modern flows (RealNVP, Glow) use architectures where the Jacobian is triangular, making the determinant O(d).

VAEs

Approximate the density via the ELBO. No exact likelihood, but scalable and flexible. Covered in 05.Autoencoders.

GANs

Implicit density model — no tractable p^​(x) at all. Train a generator Gθ​:Z→X adversarially. Can sample but cannot evaluate p^​(x).

Diffusion Models

Model the density implicitly via a denoising process. At inference, reverse a Markov chain from N(0,I) to p^_(x). Connection to KDE: the forward process is exactly repeated Gaussian convolution — progressive smoothing of p^_ toward a Gaussian.
